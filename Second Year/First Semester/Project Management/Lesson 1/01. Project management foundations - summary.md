# Project management foundations — Lesson 1

Source: *01_ENG Project Management 2026_ LESSON 1 Foundations for students.pdf*. Page numbers below refer to PDF pages. This note follows lesson order; repeated quiz and recap slides are condensed.

## 1. Projects, operations, and value (pp. 5–21)

> [!info] Definition — Project
> A **temporary endeavor undertaken in a unique context, with the objective of generating value** (PMI definition used in slides). It has a start and an end, coordinates people and resources, creates a distinct result or change, and involves uncertainty. “Temporary” describes project work; its product may last for years.

**Operations** are ongoing, repeatable work that sustains a product or service. Building a student portal is a project; running its help desk is an operation. A project ends successfully when its result is accepted **and** operational staff can sustain it through ownership, training, support, data, and documentation. Size or complexity alone does not decide whether work is a project.

**Iron triangle:** scope (what), time (when), and cost (resources) constrain one another. Changing one requires a decision about the others, quality, or risk. Meeting all three shows delivery efficiency, but success also requires fitness for purpose, stakeholder acceptance, adoption, outcomes, benefits, and strategic fit. Adapt scope when evidence shows original assumptions no longer hold; define success measures and decision rights early.

![[Pics/iron-triangle-and-success.png|650]]

*Figure (p. 15): classic constraints plus quality and stakeholder satisfaction.*

> [!info] Definition — Output → outcome → benefit → value
> **Output/deliverable:** accepted product, service, or result created by project. **Outcome:** changed behavior, capability, or condition when people use it. **Benefit:** measurable improvement from outcome, with owner, baseline, target, and review date. **Value:** benefit assessed against cost, risk, timing, opportunity cost, and strategic fit.

Example: new enrolment portal (**output**) → fewer student submission errors (**outcome**) → fewer corrections and faster processing (**benefit**) → improvement justifies total cost and supports university priorities (**value**). Each link is an assumption to test. **Goal** names desired change; deliverable names what team produces. Benefit ownership often continues after project closure.

**Practical value chain:** (1) define current and desired conditions; (2) separate output from outcome; (3) name benefit owner; (4) choose indicators, baseline, target, data source, and review point. Then test assumptions: “If users adopt this output, what should change?” (pp. 18–21).

**Lifecycle:** initiate (justify and authorize) → plan (define delivery and controls) → execute (create and integrate outputs) → monitor and control (compare evidence, forecast, adapt) → close and transition (accept, hand over, learn). Phases organize decisions and may overlap; delivery within them may be predictive or adaptive (p. 19).

## 2. Project, program, portfolio (pp. 22–33)

| Level | Definition and management question | Main success evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Project** | Temporary endeavor for a unique result. *How do we deliver it?* | Accepted result and enabled change. |
| **Program** | Related projects and activities coordinated to obtain benefits unavailable separately. *How do components work together?* | Shared outcomes and realized benefits. |
| **Portfolio** | Projects, programs, and sometimes operations grouped to achieve strategic objectives. *Which work deserves investment?* | Strategic alignment, balanced risk, capacity, and funding. |

Think **house → neighborhood → city strategy**: deliver one house; coordinate related work for a livable neighborhood; choose investments for the city. These are management objectives, not size categories. Independent initiatives competing for the same budget belong in portfolio decisions; interdependent work producing a shared benefit may need a program.

## 3. Project manager and authority (pp. 34–44)

**Project manager (PM)** leads team and integrates work to achieve project objectives: turns intent into scope, plan, coordinated decisions, delivery, monitoring, and handover. **Sponsor** authorizes and funds project, protects direction, and remains accountable for benefits. **Stakeholder** can affect, be affected by, or perceive itself affected by project (p. 63).

Effective PMs combine **ways of working** (planning, delivery, risk, quality, change), **power skills** (communication, influence, problem solving, empathy, strategic thinking), and **business acumen** (strategy, priorities, investment, benefits). Plans expose trade-offs; people make them work. PM need not perform every specialist's job.

Formal PM authority depends on who assigns work, controls resources, and resolves conflicts: **functional** organization gives PM low authority and functional manager resource control; **matrix** shares authority (weak matrix favors functional manager, strong matrix gives PM more budget/schedule control); **projectized** organization gives PM dedicated team and high authority, with corresponding accountability.

## 4. PMO and governance (pp. 45–50)

> [!info] Definition — Project Management Office (PMO)
> Management structure that standardizes project governance and shares resources, methods, tools, and techniques. Common reporting makes cost, progress, risk, and quality comparable across projects.

**Mandate:** supportive PMO coaches and supplies templates (low authority); controlling PMO sets standards and checks compliance (medium); directive PMO owns delivery and may assign PMs (high). 
**Value:** consistent methods, better governance decisions, and portfolio visibility. More control alone does not guarantee more value.

**Position:** enterprise PMO sees strategy and whole portfolio; business-unit PMO governs one unit's roadmap and capacity; project/program office integrates one major initiative. 
**Operating model:** central serves many projects, embedded works inside one initiative, hub-and-spoke connects central standards with local support. Match mandate to reporting line and actual decision rights.

## 5. Communication and Vision Board (pp. 51–59)

“Tree swing” illustrates how customer, sales, analysts, engineers, and developers can interpret one request differently. Validate shared understanding before design and build.

![[Pics/tree-swing-problem.jpg|650]]

*Figure (p. 53): five interpretations of one request.*

**Vision Board** aligns team around four questions: **target group** (who, including exclusions), **needs** (problem for each group), **product** (3–5 essential features tied to needs), and **business value** (expected improvement). Record barriers to meeting needs and realizing value; finish with one credible, memorable **vision statement**. For each value claim, specify indicator, baseline, and owner. Reconsider target groups first when their needs differ substantially.

![[Pics/vision-board-template.png|650]]

*Figure (p. 58): board template; connect each feature to a need.*

Workshop output: completed board and one-sentence vision. Rubric: target/needs **25**, product/value **35**, vision statement **30**, peer review **10** points. Rework board if feature lacks stated need or value claim lacks indicator and owner.
