## Autonomous Driving

- An autonomous vehicle is a car capable of sensing the surrounding environment and taking actions without human intervention.
- These sensors include thermographic cameras, radar, lidar, cameras, GPS, and inertial measurement units.
- The sensors’ output is used as input for a controller that creates a model to identify the most appropriate navigation path.

### Perception

- Autonomous vehicles use different types of sensors to perceive the environment and monitor their own physical parameters:
	- **Cameras:** Capture visual data to recognize objects, lanes, and traffic signs, providing essential information for navigation and decision-making.

	- **LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging):** Uses laser pulses to measure distances, creating 3D maps of the environment for object detection and localization.

	- **RADAR (Radio Detection and Ranging):** Utilizes radio waves to detect the position, speed, and direction of objects, useful for long-range detection and adverse weather conditions.

	- **SONAR (Sound Navigation and Ranging):** Uses sound waves to detect objects and measure distances, commonly used for proximity sensing in close-range environments.

	- **IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit):** Measures the vehicle's acceleration, angular velocity, and orientation, helping to determine its precise position and movement.

	- **GNSS (Global Navigation Satellite System):** Provides geospatial data through satellite signals, assisting with precise vehicle localization and navigation.
- Sensor fusion techniques are used to reduce measurement uncertainties due to noise.

## Planning

- Using the data obtained from the perception system, the ego vehicle performs behavior planning.
- The optimal behavior needs to be decided by predicting the states of the ego vehicle as well as other objects in the surroundings.
- Based on the planned behavior, generate an optimal trajectory.
- The entire process is called **motion planning**.

### SAE Levels of Automation

- The Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE) defined 6 levels of driving automation:
    - **Level 0:** No automation, manual control.
    - **Level 1:** Driver assistance with monitoring functionalities (e.g., cruise control).
    - **Level 2:** Partial automation with the car taking autonomous actions such as steering and acceleration, but the human can always take back control.
	- **Level 3**: Conditional automation, where the vehicle can perform most of the driving task, but human override is still required.
	- **Level 4**: High automation, where the vehicle performs all driving tasks under specific circumstances and in geofenced areas. Human override is still an option.
	- **Level 5**: Full automation, with the vehicle performing all driving tasks in all conditions. No human attention or interaction is required.

### Cruise Control
- Cruise Control (CC) is a system that automatically controls the speed of a motor vehicle.
- It is a servomechanism, i.e., a system that automatically uses error-sensing negative feedback to correct the throttle of the car and maintain a constant speed.
- CC is a simple implementation of a control system that, in control theory, is called proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controller.

### Basic Stuff on Control Theory
- A field of engineering and applied math that deals with the control of dynamical systems.
- The objective is to develop a controller, i.e., a device that acts on the system to achieve a desired state.
- The controller monitors the process via a process variable and compares it with a reference or set point.
- The difference between these values is called the error signal.

## PID Controller
- It is a control loop mechanism using feedback to apply continuously modulated control.
- It continuously computes an error as the difference between a desired setpoint and a measured process variable.
- Based on the error value, it applies a correction based on proportional, integral, and derivative terms.

### Control Strategies
- A control system for an autonomous vehicle is broken down into two fundamental components:
  - **Longitudinal control**: control of the longitudinal motion of the vehicle, with variables being the throttle and brake inputs.
  - **Lateral control**: control of the lateral dynamics of the vehicle, with variables being the steering inputs to govern the steering angle and heading (two different things).

### Kinematic Bicycle Model

To model vehicle dynamics for normal driving, the **Kinematic Bicycle Model** is often used. It strikes a good balance between simplicity and accuracy. The model defines a vehicle's state and observes how it evolves based on the previous state and current control inputs.

The vehicle state $q$ is represented by its x,y coordinates, heading angle $\theta$ (theta), and velocity $v$:
$$q = [x, y, \theta, v]^T$$

![[Pasted image 20251022145057.png]]

The control vector $u$ consists of longitudinal acceleration $a$ (from brake/throttle) and the steering angle $\delta$ (delta):
$$u = [a, \delta]^T$$

The model uses the "slip angle" $\beta$ (beta), which is the angle between the vehicle's heading and its actual direction of travel. This is calculated using the steering angle $\delta$, the vehicle's wheelbase $L$, and the distance from the rear axle to the center of gravity $l_r$:
$$\beta = \tan^{-1}(\frac{l_r}{L} \cdot \tan(\delta))$$

The continuous-time kinematic model is then defined by the derivatives of the state variables.
Resolving the velocity vector $v$ into $x$ and $y$ we get:
$$\dot{x} = v \cdot \cos(\theta + \beta)$$

$$\dot{y} = v \cdot \sin(\theta + \beta)$$
We then get Theta:
$$\dot{\theta} = \frac{v \cdot \tan(\delta) \cdot \cos(\beta)}{L}$$
Then we can compute:
$$\dot{v} = a$$
We hence get the continuous-time kinematic model:
$$\dot{\mathbf{q}} = \begin{bmatrix} \dot{x} \\ \dot{y} \\ \dot{\theta} \\ \dot{v} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} v \cdot \cos(\theta + \beta) \\ v \cdot \sin(\theta + \beta) \\ \frac{v \cdot \tan(\delta) \cdot \cos(\beta)}{L} \\ a \end{bmatrix}$$

This can be converted into a discrete-time model to calculate the vehicle's state at the next time step ($t+1$) based on the current state ($t$) and the change over the time interval ($\Delta t$).

$$\begin{cases} x_{t+1} = x_t + \dot{x}_t * \Delta t \\ y_{t+1} = y_t + \dot{y}_t * \Delta t \\ \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t + \dot{\theta}_t * \Delta t \\ v_{t+1} = v_t + \dot{v}_t * \Delta t \end{cases}$$

## Adaptive Cruise Control

**Adaptive Cruise Control (ACC)** is an SAE Level 1 automation system that automatically controls the vehicle's speed. Unlike basic cruise control, ACC integrates sensing capabilities (like radar or LiDAR) to detect the distance to a vehicle in front. The controller then acts on the speed to maintain a minimum safety distance.

In a typical ACC scenario, the "ego vehicle" (with ACC) follows a "lead vehicle." The ego vehicle's radar measures both the distance to the lead vehicle and the relative speed between them.

ACC has two main working modes:
1.  **Speed Control:** When the relative distance is greater than or equal to the safe distance, the ACC operates in this mode, tracking the driver-specified set speed.
2.  **Space Control:** When the relative distance is less than the safe distance, the system switches to this mode. Its objective is to control the space between the two vehicles by adjusting the ego vehicle's speed.

The system switches between these modes based on real-time measurements.

### ACC Attack Scenarios

The presentation explores two attack scenarios where an attacker compromises the ACC unit:
* **Attack 1:** The attacker remains dormant, monitoring the vehicle. At a critical moment, such as when the distance to the lead vehicle is at its minimum or when the lead vehicle suddenly brakes, the attacker creates a spike in the control signal. This can either make the vehicle accelerate unexpectedly or refuse to reduce speed, leading to a potential collision.
* **Attack 2:** This is a "covert" or "stealth" attack. While the ACC is in space control mode (Mode 2), the attacker "trivially lowers" the ACC's reference for the "safe distance." The vehicle will then follow this new, false reference, bringing it dangerously close to the lead vehicle without the driver noticing the small, incremental change.

### Attack Detection Strategy

To counter these threats, a detection and compensation strategy is proposed. This approach relies on modeling the ACC's expected behavior. The strategy involves four main sub-components:
1.  A main controller (e.g., Model Predictive Control) which is assumed to be vulnerable.
2.  An Intrusion Detection System (IDS) block that includes a "model identifier."
3.  A PID (Proportional-Integral-Derivative) compensation strategy to act as a secure fallback.
4.  A switch that can substitute the main controller with the compensation PID once an attack is detected.

### Physics-Based Attack Detection

This detection strategy is a form of **Physics-Based Attack Detection (PBAD)**. PBAD leverages physical models of a system's behavior to detect anomalies. The core idea is that any system behavior that significantly deviates from its physically modeled behavior must be the result of an attack on its sensors or actuators.

PBAD algorithms consist of two parts:
1.  **Offline:** A model of the system's "physical invariants" is built.
2.  **Online:** An online tool continuously monitors the system, comparing its *predicted* measurements (from the model) with its *observed* measurements (from the sensors) to see if they match.

### Extracting Physical Invariants

To build the required physical model, the system's dynamics are described using linear algebra. For example, a vehicle's position and velocity can be modeled as a **Linear Dynamical State-space system**:
$$
\mathbf{x}^{k} = [\mathbf{p}^k, v^k]^T, \quad \mathbf{u}^k = a^k, \quad \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0.1 \end{bmatrix}^T \\
$$
$$
\mathbf{x}^{k+1} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{k} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}^{k}, \quad \mathbf{y}^{k} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}^{k} \quad \text{represents the reason reading with} \quad \mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

The bicycle model is a more complex example of this, describing the interaction between actuators (acceleration, steering) and the vehicle's state (position, velocity, orientation). The goal is to "learn" the parameters of this model (e.g., $A$, $B$, $C$) from real-world data.

One method for this parameter estimation is **non-linear least squares data fitting**.

### Non-linear Least Squares Data Fitting

This method assumes we have a dataset of input/output data (U/Y) from the vehicle, as well as prior knowledge of the system's differential equations (like the bicycle model), $F(.)$, which contain a set of unknown parameters $P$.

The goal is to find the set of parameters $P$ that *best fit* the observed data. This is formulated as a least squares optimization problem, which aims to minimize the sum of squared differences between the model's estimated output $H()$ and the actual measured output $Y^t$.

$$\min(P) \sum_{t=1}^{T} (H^t(P, U^t) - Y^t)^2$$

Once the model parameters are learned, the system needs a way to predict its behavior in real-time. This is achieved using estimation theory, specifically the **Kalman Filter**.

However, since vehicle models are non-linear (e.g., the bicycle model),

### EKF Formulation (Predict and Update)
Given differentiable state transition and observation functions
  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  \mathbf{x}_k &= f(\mathbf{x}_{k-1}, \mathbf{u}_{k-1}) + \mathbf{w}_{k-1} \\
  \mathbf{z}_k &= h(\mathbf{x}_k) + \mathbf{v}_k
  \end{aligned}
  $$
- we define $\mathbf{w}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ as the **process** and **observation noises**, assumed to be zero-mean multivariate Gaussian noises with covariances $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{R}$
- $f(\cdot)$ can be used to compute the **predicted state** from the previous estimate
- $h(\cdot)$ can be used to compute the **predicted measurement** from the predicted state

The Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) operates in two main steps:

1.  **Predict Step:**
    * **State Estimate:** The algorithm first predicts the *next* state ($\hat{x}_{k|k-1}$) by applying the non-linear state transition function $f()$ to the *previous* corrected state ($\hat{x}_{k-1|k-1}$) and the control input ($u_{k-1}$).
    * **Covariance Estimate:** It then estimates the covariance (uncertainty) of this new prediction ($P_{k|k-1}$). This requires linearizing the state transition function around the previous state, which is done by computing its **Jacobian** ($F_k$).

2.  **Update Step (Correction):**
    * The algorithm computes the **measurement residual** ($\tilde{y}_k$), which is the difference between the *actual* sensor reading ($z_k$) and the *predicted* sensor reading (derived from $h(\hat{x}_{k|k-1})$).
    * It calculates the **Kalman gain** ($K_k$), a value that determines how much the prediction should be corrected based on the sensor reading (i.e., how much to trust the sensor). This also requires the Jacobian of the observation function ($H_k$).
    * Finally, it generates the **updated state estimate** ($\hat{x}_{k|k}$) by adding the (Kalman gain $\times$ residual) to the predicted state.
    * It also updates the **covariance estimate** ($P_{k|k}$) to reflect the new, reduced uncertainty after incorporating the sensor measurement.

In simple terms, the EKF first **makes a prediction** based on the system's model and the last known state. Then, it **corrects** that prediction using the actual data from the sensors.

### Anomaly Detection

This EKF-based prediction provides the foundation for attack detection.
To detect anomalies, the system:
1.  Uses the EKF to generate a *prediction* of what the sensor readings should be.
2.  Computes the **residuals** by finding the difference between the EKF's *predicted* readings and the *actual* sensor readings.
3.  Applies methodologies, such as those from information theory, to these residuals to decide if an anomaly (an attack) is present.

### Detection Methods

The presentation focuses on **sequential detection theory**, which is a methodology for detecting anomalies in real-time. Unlike methods that use a fixed measurement time, this approach relies on an "online-chosen detection time". These are formulated as **optimal stopping problems**.

Two key formulations are:
* **Sequential Detection (Hypothesis Testing):** Assumes the data comes from *either* a normal state ($H_0$) or an attack state ($H_1$) from the very beginning. The goal is to decide which hypothesis is true in the minimum amount of time.
* **Quickest Detection (Change Detection):** Assumes the data *starts* in a normal state ($H_0$) and *changes* to an attack state ($H_1$) at some unknown time. The goal is to detect this change as soon as possible (ASAP).

### Sequential Detection: SPRT

For sequential hypothesis testing, the main goal is to minimize the number of observations needed to make a decision, given a fixed "false alarm probability" (detecting an attack when there isn't one) and "missed detection probability" (failing to detect a real attack).

The solution is the **Sequential Probability Ratio Test (SPRT)**.
SPRT works by:
1.  Calculating the log-likelihood ratio (LLR) for each new observation ($z(k)$), which measures if the observation is more likely under the attack hypothesis ($p_1$) or the normal hypothesis ($p_0$).
2.  Adding this LLR to a cumulative sum, $S(k)$.
3.  This sum, $S(k)$, performs a "random walk".
4.  The test continues as long as the sum $S(k)$ stays between a lower threshold ($L$) and an upper threshold ($U$).
5.  If $S(k)$ crosses the upper threshold $U$, the test stops and declares $H_1$ (Attack).
6.  If $S(k)$ crosses the lower threshold $L$, the test stops and declares $H_0$ (Normal).

The thresholds $L$ and $U$ are calculated based on the desired probabilities for false alarms and missed detections. Examples in the slides show this cumulative sum starting from zero and moving up or down with each sample, eventually crossing a threshold to make a decision.

### Change Detection: CUSUM

For change detection, the **CUSUM (Cumulative Sum)** algorithm is commonly used. It is very similar to SPRT and aims to minimize the detection delay after a change occurs, given a fixed false alarm rate.

The CUSUM statistic $S(k)$ is updated using the LLR, just like SPRT:
$S(k+1) = (\log\frac{p_1(z(k))}{p_0(z(k))} + S(k))^+$

The key difference is the $(...)^+$ operator, which means the sum is **reset to 0** if it ever becomes negative.
* This is equivalent to an SPRT where the lower threshold $L$ is set to 0, and the statistic restarts every time it hits that lower bound.
* The CUSUM test triggers an alarm when the statistic $S(k)$ exceeds a single upper threshold, $\tau$ (tau).

Selecting the threshold $\tau$ involves a trade-off:
* A **high threshold** (e.g., $\tau=5$) leads to a large "Average Run Length" under $H_0$ (few false alarms) but is slow to detect real changes.
* A **low threshold** (e.g., $\tau=2$) is very fast to detect changes (small "Average Run Length" under $H_1$) but results in many more false alarms.

### Detecting Compromised Sensors

A major problem with SPRT and CUSUM is that they assume we know the probability distribution $p_1$ for an attack. In reality, an attacker can be strategic and non-stationary, making this assumption limiting.

A **nonparametric** approach is used instead, which does not assume a specific attack distribution. It only places mild constraints on the observations.

The constraint is based on the *expected value* (mean) of the processed residuals $Z_i(k)$:
* Under $H_0$ (Normal): $\mathbb{E}_0[Z_i] < 0$
* Under $H_1$ (Attack): $\mathbb{E}_1[Z_i] > 0$

This condition is achieved by defining the input to the detector as $z_i(k) = ||\tilde{y}_i(k) - \hat{y}_i(k)|| - b_i$, where the first term is the magnitude of the EKF residual and $b_i$ is a small positive constant chosen to make the expected value negative during normal operation.

### Cooperative Adaptive Cruise Control (CACC)

The presentation then introduces **Cooperative Adaptive Cruise Control (CACC)**, an advanced system that enhances ACC by integrating **Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) communication**.

In addition to using its own sensors (like ACC), a CACC-equipped vehicle also receives data from the preceding vehicle, specifically its **acceleration**, in a feed-forward loop. This information is shared via **Cooperative Awareness Messages (CAMs)**. This allows CACC vehicles to form "platoons" with much shorter and more stable inter-vehicle distances.
