## 1.0 Executive Introduction

This document provides a detailed analysis of the cybersecurity vulnerabilities inherent in autonomous vehicles (AVs), examining the threat landscape across the entire autonomous stack. The central thesis is that malicious attacks can target every layer of a vehicle's operation, from its fundamental control systems to its advanced collaborative perception networks. The analysis follows a narrative of escalating complexity, beginning with attacks on the vehicle's "brain" (control systems), moving to its "nervous system" (vehicle-to-vehicle communication), its "senses" (physical sensors like LiDAR), its "interpretation of reality" (perception models), and finally its "social interactions" (collaborative data sharing). The key takeaway is that securing autonomous systems requires a multi-faceted, defense-in-depth strategy, integrating control-theoretic models, physics-based invariants, statistical anomaly detection, and cross-agent consistency checks to ensure the integrity of the vehicle's entire sense-understand-act loop.

## 2.0 Foundational Concepts of Autonomous Driving

To comprehend the security risks facing autonomous vehicles, it is essential to first establish a firm grasp of their fundamental operating principles. This includes understanding how they use a suite of sensors to perceive their environment, how they process this information to make critical driving decisions, and the standardized levels of automation that define their capabilities and limitations.

### 2.1 Core Components of an Autonomous Vehicle

An autonomous vehicle is a car capable of sensing its surrounding environment and taking safe actions without human intervention. To achieve this, it relies on a sophisticated array of sensors and two core operational processes: perception and planning.

The primary sensors used for environmental perception include:

- Thermographic cameras
- Radar
- Lidar
- Cameras
- GPS
- Inertial measurement units (IMUs)

These components support the vehicle's core operational processes:

1. **Perception:** AVs utilize the sensors listed above to build a model of their environment and monitor their own physical state. To improve accuracy and reduce measurement uncertainties caused by noise, data from multiple sensors is combined using a technique known as sensor fusion.
2. **Planning:** The vehicle uses the data from the perception system to perform behavior and motion planning. This involves predicting the states of the ego-vehicle and other surrounding objects to generate an optimal and safe trajectory.

### 2.2 The SAE Levels of Automation

The Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE) has established a widely adopted framework that defines six distinct levels of driving automation, clarifying the roles and responsibilities of the human driver and the autonomous system at each stage.

|   |   |
|---|---|
|Level|Description and Examples|
|**Level 0**|**No Automation:** The human driver performs all driving tasks with manual control.|
|**Level 1**|**Driver Assistance:** The system provides monitoring functionalities, such as cruise control.|
|**Level 2**|**Partial Automation:** The car performs autonomous actions like steering and acceleration, but the human can take back control at any time.|
|**Level 3**|**Conditional Automation:** The vehicle performs most driving tasks, but human override is still required under certain conditions.|
|**Level 4**|**High Automation:** The vehicle performs all driving tasks under specific circumstances and within geofenced areas. Human override remains an option.|
|**Level 5**|**Full Automation:** The vehicle performs all driving tasks in all conditions, with no human attention or interaction required.|

Despite progress, significant barriers hinder widespread adoption, including the need for resolved liability laws, assurance of data security, protection against cyberattacks, and changes in societal attitude.

## 3.0 Core Principles of Vehicle Control Systems

Many critical AV functions are managed by control systems, representing the vehicle's digital "brain." Understanding their theoretical basis, such as feedback loops and PID controllers, is essential for analyzing how they can be manipulated by attackers seeking to influence the vehicle's physical actions.

### 3.1 Introduction to Control Theory

Control theory is a field of engineering and applied mathematics that deals with the control of dynamical systems. The primary objective is to develop a controller—a device that acts on a system to achieve a desired state. It accomplishes this by monitoring a process variable (e.g., current speed) and comparing it to a setpoint (e.g., desired speed), using the difference, or error signal, to apply a correction.

There are two main types of controllers:

- **Controller (Open Loop):** This type of controller does not use feedback from the process itself. An example is a boiler that heats water for a predetermined amount of time, regardless of the water's actual temperature.
- **Closed Loop (Feedback) Controller:** This controller uses the output of the process to adjust its action. Common examples include a vehicle's cruise control, which adjusts the throttle based on measured speed, and a thermostat, which controls heating based on room temperature.

### 3.2 The PID Controller Explained

A Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) controller is a widely used control loop mechanism that employs feedback to continuously calculate an error value and apply a correction. It is the basis for many automotive systems, including cruise control. The controller's action is based on a weighted sum of three terms. This reliance on a predictable, error-driven feedback loop makes the PID controller a foundational attack surface; by manipulating the error signal, an attacker can directly command the vehicle's most critical physical actions.

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|Term|Represents|Function in Cruise Control|
|**Proportional (P)**|Proportional to the current error.|Adjusts the throttle in proportion to the difference between the target speed and the actual speed.|
|**Integral (I)**|The integral of the error over a past time window.|Deals with sustained errors, such as those caused by driving up hills, by considering the accumulated difference in distance traveled versus the ideal distance.|
|**Derivative (D)**|The derivative of the error, estimating its future trend.|Helps the system respond quickly to changes by considering the vehicle's acceleration (the derivative of speed).|

Overall vehicle control is typically divided into two components: **longitudinal control**, which manages throttle and brake inputs, and **lateral control**, which governs steering inputs. Understanding these control principles provides the foundation for modeling a vehicle's motion mathematically.

## 4.0 Vehicle Dynamics and Kinematic Modeling

To effectively control a vehicle or detect anomalous behavior indicative of an attack, a mathematical model of its movement is required. The Kinematic Bicycle Model is a foundational model used in autonomous driving that provides a good balance between simplicity and accuracy for describing vehicle dynamics under normal driving conditions, such as lower speeds and smooth maneuvers.

### 4.1 The Kinematic Bicycle Model

A kinematic model is a simplified representation that describes the motion of a system without considering the forces and torques that cause it. The Kinematic Bicycle Model defines the vehicle's state and how it evolves over time based on control inputs.

- The **vehicle state vector (**`**q**`**)** comprises its `x,y` coordinates, its heading angle (`theta`), and its velocity (`v`).
- The **control vector** consists of the longitudinal acceleration (from throttle/brake) and the steering angle.

The model uses a set of state transition equations to calculate the vehicle's future state (position, heading, and velocity) based on its current state and the control inputs of acceleration and steering. This theoretical model serves as the basis for real-world driver-assistance features like Adaptive Cruise Control.

## 5.0 Adaptive Cruise Control (ACC): System, Vulnerabilities, and Defenses

Adaptive Cruise Control (ACC) is a practical, real-world application of the sensing and control principles previously discussed. As an SAE Level 1 system, it represents a critical case study for cyber-physical attacks where the line between software commands and physical vehicle motion is blurred.

### 5.1 ACC System Operation

Adaptive Cruise Control is a system that uses sensors, such as radar or LiDAR, to automatically control a vehicle's speed and maintain a safe following distance from a vehicle ahead. It operates in one of two primary modes:

1. **Speed Control Mode:** This mode is active when the relative distance to the lead vehicle is greater than or equal to the predefined safe distance. The system maintains a speed set by the driver.
2. **Space Control Mode:** This mode activates when the relative distance is less than the safe distance. The system adjusts the vehicle's speed to maintain this minimum distance.

### 5.2 ACC Attack Scenarios

Assuming an attacker has successfully compromised the ACC unit, several malicious actions become possible. Two specific scenarios highlight these vulnerabilities:

1. **Sudden Acceleration Attack:** The attacker remains dormant, monitoring the distance to the lead vehicle. When this distance is at its minimum (near the safe following distance), the attacker injects a sudden spike into the control signal, causing the vehicle to accelerate dangerously toward the car in front.
2. **Stealthy Reference Attack:** The attacker subtly lowers the ACC's internal reference value for the "safe distance." This causes the vehicle to follow the car ahead more closely than is safe, but the change is small enough that it may not be immediately noticeable to the driver, allowing the attack to remain covert.

### 5.3 Physics-Based Attack Detection (PBAD)

Physics-Based Attack Detection (PBAD) is a defensive strategy that leverages a mathematical model of the system's expected physical behavior to identify anomalies. The core idea is that any system behavior that significantly deviates from the physical model likely indicates an attack.

The PBAD process consists of two parts:

1. **Offline:** A model of the system's physical invariants is constructed. This can be done by using the kinematic model and fitting its parameters to real-world driving data using techniques like non-linear least squares.
2. **Online:** A monitoring tool continuously compares the system's observed measurements with the model's predictions to detect discrepancies.

To predict the behavior of a non-linear system like a vehicle, an **Extended Kalman Filter (EKF)** is used. The EKF's power lies in its two-step process: first, it uses the vehicle's kinematic model to _predict_ where the vehicle should be in the next instant. Second, it uses actual sensor readings to _correct_ that prediction. The "residuals"—the difference between the prediction and the reality—become the primary signal for anomaly detection. Once residuals are generated, statistical methods are needed to formally decide if they constitute an attack.

## 6.0 Statistical Methods for Anomaly Detection

A robust attack detection system requires more than just a physical model; it needs a formal mathematical framework to decide when a deviation from the model is significant enough to be classified as an attack. Sequential detection theory provides a methodology for making such decisions in real-time as new data arrives.

### 6.1 Sequential Detection vs. Change Detection

At the heart of this problem are **Optimal Stopping Problems**: determining the minimum number of data samples needed to confidently decide between a "normal behavior" hypothesis and an "attack" hypothesis. Two primary formulations exist:

- **Sequential Detection:** Assumes the entire time series is generated from _either_ a normal process or an attack process from the very beginning. The goal is to identify which one it is as quickly as possible.
- **Change Detection:** Assumes the time series begins as normal and, at some unknown point in time, _changes_ to an attack. The goal is to detect this change as soon as possible after it occurs.

### 6.2 Key Algorithms and Concepts

Two key algorithms are used to implement these detection strategies. Their characteristics are summarized below.

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|Algorithm|Description|Key Metrics/Goal|
|**SPRT (Sequential Probability Ratio Test)**|Uses a cumulative sum of Log-Likelihood Ratios (LLRs) to test a running statistic against upper and lower thresholds.|Minimize the number of observations needed to make a decision, given fixed probabilities for false alarms and missed detections.|
|**CUSUM (Cumulative Sum)**|Similar to SPRT but with a lower bound of zero. The statistic is not allowed to become negative and effectively restarts if it would.|Minimize the detection delay after a change has occurred, for a given fixed false alarm rate.|

When setting the detection threshold for an algorithm like CUSUM, a critical trade-off must be managed between two key metrics:

- **Average Run Length under H0 (ARL0):** The average number of samples before a false alarm is triggered. A large ARL0 is desirable.
- **Average Run Length under H1 (ARL1):** The average number of samples needed to detect a real change after it has happened. A small ARL1 is desirable.

These statistical methods provide a rigorous foundation for detecting attacks on individual vehicle systems. Security challenges evolve, however, as vehicles begin to communicate directly, forming a vehicular "nervous system."

## 7.0 Cooperative Adaptive Cruise Control (CACC) and Platoon Security

Cooperative Adaptive Cruise Control (CACC) represents an evolution of ACC that incorporates Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) communication. This allows vehicles to share data, enabling efficiency gains like platooning (driving in tight, coordinated convoys). However, this direct communication channel also introduces new attack surfaces that can be exploited to compromise the entire platoon.

### 7.1 CACC System and Control Model

CACC enhances standard ACC by using the preceding vehicle's acceleration data, received via V2X, in a feed-forward control loop. This allows for faster and smoother reactions than relying solely on radar or LiDAR measurements. The CACC operation follows the same perception, planning, and actuation phases as other autonomous systems. In a platooning context, the primary control goal is for each vehicle to maintain the same longitudinal speed as the car in front of it while keeping a fixed, short inter-vehicle distance.

### 7.2 CACC Attack Strategies

The interconnected nature of a CACC platoon enables several unique attack strategies that can be launched by a malicious insider or a compromised vehicle.

- **Reduced Headway Attack:** A driver intentionally ignores the system's recommended headway and follows the vehicle in front more closely to increase fuel savings, compromising the platoon's stability and safety margins.
- **Joining Without Radar:** A vehicle equipped only with DSRC (V2X) communication but lacking the necessary radar attempts to join the platoon. This makes it dangerously reliant on the feed-forward communication data, which can be disrupted or spoofed.
- **Mis-report Attack:** A malicious vehicle misinforms the car following it about its own behavior (e.g., its acceleration) to manipulate the following car's headway and disrupt the platoon's formation.
- **Collision Induction Attack:** An attacker broadcasts a false acceleration profile to the following car, causing it to speed up, while the attacker's vehicle simultaneously brakes aggressively. This is designed to induce a rear-end collision.

### 7.3 Defenses Against CACC Attacks

Defensive strategies for CACC focus on verifying the integrity of both physical observations and communicated data.

- **Model-Based Detection:** This strategy requires each vehicle to maintain a mathematical model of the expected behavior of the vehicle directly in front of it. It constantly compares this model's predictions to the observed behavior (via radar) and received V2X data. If a significant anomaly is detected, the vehicle defaults to a safer, radar-only standard ACC mode.
- **Defense Against Replay Attacks:** To counter attackers who record and replay old, legitimate data, a more sophisticated defense uses a physical authentication signal. The process involves four steps:
    1. The lead vehicle, assumed to be secure, injects a unique, low-power **noisy signal** into its control actions.
    2. This "authentication signal" naturally **propagates** down the platoon through the physical dynamics of the vehicles' control systems.
    3. Each vehicle runs a **virtual model** of the platoon ahead of it. This model takes the leader's noisy signal as input and predicts what the effect of this signal _should_ be on the preceding car's acceleration.
    4. A **cross-correlator** statistically compares the received acceleration signal from the car ahead with the virtual model's prediction. A high correlation confirms authenticity, while a low correlation indicates a replay attack where the authenticating noise is absent.

Beyond the control system and its communication channels, an even more fundamental threat lies in attacking the physical sensors that provide the data in the first place.

## 8.0 Security of the Sensing Layer: LiDAR Attacks

The focus now shifts from the vehicle's control systems to its "senses"—the physical sensors that feed them information. LiDAR is critical for creating a 3D environmental model, making it a high-value target. Attackers can exploit the physical properties of light with low-cost equipment (e.g., a $40 laser module) to blind or deceive a sensor system worth thousands of dollars, making this a highly asymmetric attack vector.

### 8.1 How LiDAR Works and Its Vulnerabilities

LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) is an active remote sensing method that measures distance by emitting a laser pulse and calculating the round-trip time it takes for the light to reflect off an object and return. Modern vehicles primarily use scanning LiDARs, which rotate to create a 360-degree view, though non-moving solid-state LiDARs are emerging.

Any sensor is vulnerable to two primary types of physical attack:

- **Saturation (Denial-of-Service):** Overwhelming the sensor with a strong external signal to blind it or render it inoperable.
- **Spoofing:** Deceiving the sensor by feeding it a malicious signal that simulates a real-world object or event, causing it to perceive a false reality.

### 8.2 LiDAR Attack Analysis

Two specific attacks demonstrate how LiDAR sensors can be physically manipulated.

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|Attack Type|Method|Key Characteristics & Effects|
|**Blinding Attack (Saturation)**|An attacker points a light source of the same wavelength as the LiDAR (e.g., a 905nm infrared laser) at the sensor.|The attack is **stealthy**, as infrared light is invisible to the human eye. The effect is limited by the LiDAR's receiving angle, but a strong source can blind an entire sector. The curved reception glass on some LiDARs can cause stray light to create fake dots in other directions.|
|**Spoofing by Relaying**|An attacker's tool receives the victim LiDAR's laser pulse, holds it for a calculated delay, and then fires an identical pulse back at the sensor.|This is also **stealthy** and can be used to create a "ghost" vehicle or obstacle at a specific distance determined by the delay. An attacker can induce multiple fake dots, but the attack is also constrained by the LiDAR's receiving angle.|

These attacks target the raw sensor data. The next level of vulnerability lies in attacking the perception models that interpret this data, compromising the vehicle's "interpretation of reality."

## 9.0 Attacks on the Perception Pipeline and Physical-Invariant Defenses

Even if a sensor receives data correctly, the deep learning models responsible for 3D object detection can be attacked directly. Adversarial attacks aim to manipulate these models by injecting a small, carefully crafted set of malicious points into the LiDAR point cloud, causing the system to misinterpret its surroundings.

### 9.1 Adversarial Attacks on 3D Object Detection

LiDAR 3D object detection models can be grouped into three main categories: Byrd’s-eye view (BEV)-based, Voxel-based, and Point-Wise models. The core concept of an adversarial attack against these models is to add or modify a very small number of points in the 3D point cloud to cause one of two outcomes: either the model fails to detect a real object that is present, or it detects a "ghost" object that does not exist.

To make spoofed objects appear more realistic and bypass simple filters, attackers can exploit physical properties of how LiDAR works. Two key occlusion patterns can be imitated:

- **Inter-occlusion:** The way a solid object in the foreground partially hides an object behind it.
- **Intra-occlusion:** The way the front surface of an object occludes its own back and interior points from the LiDAR's perspective.

### 9.2 The CARLO Defense System

CARLO (oCclusion-Aware hieRarchy anomaLy detectiOn) is a defense mechanism designed to counter these attacks. It uses the physical laws of occlusion as an invariant to detect spoofed fake vehicles that are merely sparse collections of points.

CARLO is built on two core detection blocks:

- **Free Space Detection (FSD):** This method calculates the ratio of empty "free space" within the 3D bounding box of a detected object. A real, solid object will have a very low free space ratio. In contrast, a fake object made of a few sparse points will have a very high ratio, as most of the box will be empty.
- **Laser Penetration Detection (LPD):** This method calculates the ratio of laser rays that pass completely _through_ a detected object's bounding box. For a real car, this ratio should be very low (rays might only pass through windows). A fake, sparse object will not block the rays behind it, resulting in a high penetration ratio.

CARLO employs a hierarchical design for efficiency. It first uses the faster, less computationally intensive LPD method to filter obvious cases. Any uncertain detections are then passed to the more rigorous FSD method for a final check. The CARLO system is a prime example of leveraging _physical invariants_—the fact that real-world objects are solid and occlude light—as a powerful defense against attacks occurring in the _cyber_ or software domain.

## 10.0 Security in Collaborative Perception Environments

Collaborative perception is an advanced technique where connected autonomous vehicles share sensor data to enhance their individual awareness. This helps overcome limitations like occlusion, allowing one vehicle to "see" around a corner using data from a teammate. However, this creates a new attack surface by targeting a vehicle's "social interactions"—the direct sharing of data, which enables data fabrication attacks where a malicious vehicle injects false information into the network.

### 10.1 Data Fabrication Attacks

The goal of a data fabrication attack is for an attacker-controlled vehicle to modify the sensor data it shares (`f(A_i)`) in order to either spoof a fake object or remove a real object from a victim vehicle's merged perception. The technique uses **Black Box Ray Casting**, which serves a dual purpose. For _spoofing attacks_, it generates a dense and physically plausible point cloud of a non-existent object by simulating LiDAR rays hitting a 3D model. For _object removal attacks_, it generates an adversarial shape designed specifically to obscure the point cloud of a real object, effectively hiding it from the perception system.

Key constraints and techniques for such an attack include:

- **Constraints:** The fabricated data must be physically plausible and obey the laws of sensor physics to avoid trivial detection. The attack must also be generated in real-time.
- **Technique:** An attacker uses Black Box Ray Casting to produce a point cloud that inherently follows the laws of physics, thereby bypassing physics-based defenses like CARLO.

### 10.2 Collaborative Anomaly Detection (CAD)

Collaborative Anomaly Detection (CAD) is a proposed defense against data fabrication that relies on cross-agent consistency checks. The core idea is that the sensor data from multiple benign vehicles can be used to identify and reject malicious data from an attacker.

The CAD process unfolds in several steps:

1. Each vehicle independently generates a 2D **Occupancy Map** from its own LiDAR data, labeling areas of the road as occupied, free, or unknown.
2. Vehicles exchange these maps via V2V communication.
3. Each vehicle performs an **Occupancy Consistency Check**, comparing its own map with those received from others. Regions where one map shows "occupied" and another shows "free" are identified as conflicted.
4. A merged, consistent map is created by combining the trustworthy data from all maps and marking conflicted regions as unknown.
5. A final **Perception-Occupancy Consistency Check** is performed. This step ensures that the final objects detected by the collaborative perception system (e.g., 3D bounding boxes) are consistent with the "occupied" regions of the merged map. Any object detected in a region marked as "free" is flagged as an anomaly.

This approach highlights the need to consider multiple sensor modalities and the powerful role of sensor fusion.

## 11.0 Sensor Fusion and Overall Conclusions

This analysis demonstrates that autonomous vehicle security is a deeply layered problem, and robust solutions require a holistic, defense-in-depth approach. A key strategy for improving both the accuracy and security of vehicle perception is sensor fusion—the process of combining data from multiple, diverse sensors to create a more complete and reliable understanding of the environment.

### 11.1 The Role of Cameras and Sensor Fusion

Cameras are a cost-effective and ubiquitous sensor in AVs, excelling at tasks like road sign recognition. While they have limitations, their data can be combined with that from LiDAR and radar through **sensor fusion**. This process merges information from different sensors to reduce uncertainty and create a more robust perception system. A simple example is **inverse variance weighting**. If two different sensors provide measurements of the same quantity, each with its own variance (a measure of uncertainty), they can be fused into a single, more reliable estimate. The resulting fused measurement will have a lower variance than either of the individual measurements, demonstrating how fusion improves confidence.

### 11.2 Concluding Summary

Across this analysis, four major defensive paradigms have emerged to counter the escalating threat landscape. **Model-based detection** leverages control theory and physics to validate vehicle dynamics. **Statistical detection** applies rigorous mathematical frameworks to identify anomalous deviations in real-time. **Invariant-based detection** uses the fundamental laws of physics, such as occlusion, to unmask spoofed sensor data. Finally, **consistency-based detection** uses cross-agent validation in collaborative environments to identify fabricated information.

The primary implication of this analysis is unambiguous: defending autonomous vehicles requires a multi-layered, defense-in-depth strategy. There is no single silver bullet. A truly resilient system must integrate these complementary defensive paradigms to protect against attacks on control systems, communication channels, physical sensors, perception software, and collaborative networks. Only by combining these approaches can the integrity of the vehicle's critical sense-understand-act loop be preserved against sophisticated attacks.
