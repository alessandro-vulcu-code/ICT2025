## Introduction

### Limitations of Block-based Transforms (JPEG)

> [!note] Recall from JPEG
> JPEG divides the image into **8×8 pixel blocks** and applies the DCT independently to each block. This leads to several issues at low bitrates:

**Problems with JPEG:**

1. **Blocking artefacts**: at low bitrates, heavily quantized blocks become visually distinct from each other. The image appears "tiled" because each block is coded independently — there is no information shared across block boundaries.
2. **Ringing artefacts (Gibbs phenomenon)**: the DCT struggles near sharp edges. A sharp edge (step function) requires many high-frequency cosine coefficients to represent faithfully. When high-frequency DCT coefficients are discarded (quantized to zero), the edge becomes "blurry" with oscillations on either side.
3. **Fixed resolution**: JPEG operates at a single spatial resolution; there is no natural support for progressive decoding by quality or spatial resolution.
4. **No multi-resolution representation**: you cannot easily extract a lower-resolution version from a JPEG bitstream.

> [!tip] What we want instead
> An ideal compression system would:
> - Avoid blocking artefacts (no block boundaries)
> - Handle edges well without ringing
> - Support **progressive transmission** (receive coarse image first, then refine)
> - Support **resolution scalability** (extract a lower-resolution version without full decoding)
>
> Wavelets address all these needs.

---

### What is a Wavelet?

> [!important] Intuitive definition
> A **wavelet** is a small wave — a function $\psi(t)$ that is:
> - **Localized in time** (it is zero outside a finite or short interval)
> - **Oscillatory** (it has zero mean: $\int \psi(t)\,dt = 0$)
>
> The word "wavelet" = "small wave" (French: *ondelette*). Unlike the sine/cosine basis of the Fourier transform (which are infinite in time), wavelets are *local*.

The key insight behind wavelet-based compression is the **trend + anomalies model**:

> [!note] Trend + Anomalies Model
> A natural signal (image, audio) can be modelled as a **smooth trend** (low-frequency behaviour) plus **local anomalies** (edges, textures, transients). Wavelets separate these two components efficiently:
> - The **low-pass (approximation)** part captures the trend → carries most of the signal energy
> - The **high-pass (detail)** part captures the anomalies → sparse (mostly zero away from edges)

## Discrete Wavelet Transform and Multiresolution Analysis

### Filter Banks and DWT

#### Two-Channel Filter Bank

The Discrete Wavelet Transform is implemented using a **filter bank** — a system of filters and downsamplers.

> [!important] Analysis filter bank (one level, 1D)
> Given input signal $x[n]$, one level of DWT is:
>
> $$\text{Low-pass output (approximation):} \quad a[n] = (x * h)[2n] = \sum_k h[k]\, x[2n - k]$$
>
> $$\text{High-pass output (detail):} \quad d[n] = (x * g)[2n] = \sum_k g[k]\, x[2n - k]$$
>
> where $h[n]$ is the **low-pass analysis filter** and $g[n]$ is the **high-pass analysis filter**, and $\downarrow 2$ denotes downsampling by 2.

Both $a[n]$ and $d[n]$ have **half the samples** of $x[n]$ → no redundancy, same total data.

> [!important] Synthesis filter bank (perfect reconstruction)
> The decoder recovers $x[n]$ from $a[n]$ and $d[n]$ using the **synthesis filters** $\tilde{h}[n]$ and $\tilde{g}[n]$:
>
> $$x[n] = \sum_k \tilde{h}[k]\,a\!\left[\frac{n-k}{2}\right] + \sum_k \tilde{g}[k]\,d\!\left[\frac{n-k}{2}\right]$$
>
> (i.e., upsample $a$ and $d$ by 2, then filter with $\tilde{h}$ and $\tilde{g}$, and sum)

**Perfect reconstruction** condition: the filters $h, g, \tilde{h}, \tilde{g}$ must satisfy certain algebraic conditions (related to biorthogonality or orthogonality) so that $x[n]$ is recovered exactly from $a[n]$ and $d[n]$ (assuming no quantization).

**Conjugate Mirror Filters (CMF):** A common choice for orthogonal DWT. If $h[n]$ is the low-pass filter, then:

$$g[n] = (-1)^n h[L-1-n]$$

where $L$ is the filter length. The synthesis filters are $\tilde{h}[n] = h[-n]$ and $\tilde{g}[n] = g[-n]$.

> [!note] Key insight: no redundancy
> Unlike the STFT (Short-Time Fourier Transform), the DWT has **no redundancy**: the total number of output samples equals the input. This is essential for compression.

---

#### Biorthogonal Filter Banks

> [!note] Biorthogonal wavelets
> In practice, **biorthogonal** filter banks are preferred over orthogonal ones for image compression. They allow:
> - **Symmetric filters** (orthogonal filters with finite length cannot be symmetric, except the Haar filter)
> - Symmetric filters avoid phase distortion and are more visually pleasant
> - The analysis and synthesis filters are different but related (biorthogonal pair)

The most important family for image compression is the **Cohen-Daubechies-Feauveau (CDF) 9/7 filter bank** (used in JPEG 2000 lossy mode) and the **CDF 5/3** (used in JPEG 2000 lossless mode).

| Filter bank | Analysis low-pass length | Analysis high-pass length | Used in |
|---|---|---|---|
| CDF 9/7 | 9 taps | 7 taps | JPEG 2000 (lossy) |
| CDF 5/3 | 5 taps | 3 taps | JPEG 2000 (lossless) |
| Haar | 2 taps | 2 taps | Simplest wavelet |

---

#### Multi-level DWT (Iterated Filter Bank)

> [!tip] Key idea: apply DWT recursively to the low-pass output
> One level of DWT splits the signal into a *low-pass* (approximation, most of the energy) and a *high-pass* (detail, sparse). Since the low-pass output is itself a smooth, low-resolution version of the signal, we can apply another level of DWT to it. This is the **iterated filter bank**.

After $N$ levels of 1D DWT on a signal of length $M$:
- **Approximation subband**: $M / 2^N$ samples
- **Detail subbands** at levels $1, 2, \ldots, N$: $M/2, M/4, \ldots, M/2^N$ samples each
- **Total**: $M/2 + M/4 + \ldots + M/2^N + M/2^N = M$ samples (no redundancy)

The resulting structure is a **multi-resolution** decomposition: each detail subband captures the signal features at a specific scale (resolution level).

---

#### 2D-DWT (for Images)

> [!important] 2D DWT as a separable transform
> For 2D signals (images), the DWT is applied **separably**:
> 1. Apply 1D DWT to each **row** → produces left (low-pass) and right (high-pass) halves
> 2. Apply 1D DWT to each **column** of the result → produces 4 quadrants (subbands)

After one level of 2D-DWT, the image is split into **4 subbands**:

| Subband | Operation | Content |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| **LL** (top-left) | Low-pass rows + Low-pass cols | Low-resolution approximation of the image |
| **HL** (top-right) | High-pass rows + Low-pass cols | **Horizontal details** (vertical edges, slow vertical variations) |
| **LH** (bottom-left) | Low-pass rows + High-pass cols | **Vertical details** (horizontal edges, slow horizontal variations) |
| **HH** (bottom-right) | High-pass rows + High-pass cols | **Diagonal details** (diagonal edges and textures) |

> [!example] Subband interpretation for a synthetic image (black background, white square)
> - **LL subband**: a blurred, quarter-size version of the original image
> - **HL subband**: thin vertical lines at the left and right edges of the square
> - **LH subband**: thin horizontal lines at the top and bottom edges of the square
> - **HH subband**: small dots at the four corners of the square (intersections of edges)
>
> Everything else is zero — the signal is **very sparse** in the wavelet domain!

Then DWT is applied recursively to the **LL subband only**, producing the multi-level structure:

```
After level 1: [LL1 | HL1]    →   After level 2: [LL2 | HL2 | HL1]
               [LH1 | HH1]                       [LH2 | HH2 |    ]
                                                  [LH1      | HH1 ]
```

At each level, the LL subband is further decomposed, producing an increasingly coarser approximation and finer detail subbands.

### Multiresolution Analysis (MRA)

> [!important] Definition: Multiresolution Analysis
> **Multiresolution Analysis (MRA)** is the mathematical framework that underlies the wavelet transform. It provides a rigorous way to decompose a signal into components at different resolutions (scales).

**Key concepts of MRA:**

- A sequence of nested **approximation spaces** $V_j \subset V_{j-1} \subset \cdots \subset V_0 \subset L^2(\mathbb{R})$
- Each $V_j$ contains all functions that can be represented at resolution $2^{-j}$ (coarser as $j$ increases)
- The **detail space** $W_j$ is the orthogonal complement of $V_j$ in $V_{j-1}$: $V_{j-1} = V_j \oplus W_j$
- The **scaling function** $\phi(t)$ generates the spaces $V_j$ by dilation and translation
- The **mother wavelet** $\psi(t)$ generates the spaces $W_j$

The DWT coefficients are the inner products of the signal with dilated and translated versions of $\psi$:

$$d_{j,k} = \langle x, \psi_{j,k} \rangle = \int x(t)\, \psi_{j,k}(t)\, dt, \quad \psi_{j,k}(t) = 2^{-j/2}\psi(2^{-j}t - k)$$

> [!note] Wavelet = measuring anomalies at a given scale and position
> The coefficient $d_{j,k}$ measures "how much of an anomaly exists at **position** $\approx k \cdot 2^j$ and **scale** $2^j$". If the signal is smooth at that location and scale, $d_{j,k} \approx 0$.

**Vanishing moments:** A wavelet $\psi$ has $M$ **vanishing moments** if:

$$\int t^m \psi(t)\, dt = 0 \quad \forall m = 0, 1, \ldots, M-1$$

> [!tip] Why vanishing moments matter
> A wavelet with $M$ vanishing moments is **orthogonal to polynomials of degree up to $M-1$**. This means:
> - If the signal is locally polynomial of degree $< M$, the wavelet coefficient is *exactly zero*
> - More vanishing moments → better sparsification of smooth signals → better compression
> - But more vanishing moments → longer filters → more computational cost

---

#### Haar Wavelet (simplest example)

The **Haar wavelet** is the simplest possible wavelet:

$$\phi(t) = \begin{cases} 1 & 0 \leq t < 1 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \qquad \psi(t) = \begin{cases} +1 & 0 \leq t < 1/2 \\ -1 & 1/2 \leq t < 1 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Haar filters:
- Low-pass: $h = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[1, 1]$
- High-pass: $g = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[1, -1]$

> [!example] 1D Haar DWT (one level)
> Input: $x = [4, 6, 10, 12, 8, 6, 4, 4]$
>
> Low-pass (sum/√2): $a = [5\sqrt{2}, 11\sqrt{2}, 7\sqrt{2}, 4\sqrt{2}]$
>
> High-pass (diff/√2): $d = [-\sqrt{2}, -\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2}, 0]$
>
> The detail coefficients are much smaller — the signal is *mostly smooth*.

**Property:** The Haar wavelet has **1 vanishing moment** (annihilates constants). Haar is the simplest but also least efficient for smooth signals.

---

#### Daubechies Wavelets

**Ingrid Daubechies** (1988) constructed a family of orthogonal wavelets with *maximum vanishing moments* for a given filter length:

| Wavelet | Filter length | Vanishing moments |
|---------|--------------|-------------------|
| Haar (db1) | 2 | 1 |
| db2 | 4 | 2 |
| db4 | 8 | 4 |
| db8 | 16 | 8 |

> [!note] Trade-off
> Daubechies wavelets have the **maximum vanishing moments** for a given filter length among orthogonal wavelets. More vanishing moments = better for compression of smooth signals, but longer filters = more computation and border effects.

**Limitation:** Daubechies orthogonal wavelets (except Haar) are **not symmetric** — this causes phase distortion. For image compression, symmetric filters are strongly preferred → biorthogonal wavelets.

### Lifting Scheme

> [!important] Lifting: an efficient way to compute the DWT
> The **lifting scheme** (Sweldens, 1995) is an elegant and efficient method to implement any wavelet transform using only simple operations:
>
> **Split → Predict → Update**

1. **Split**: divide the signal into even samples $x_e[n] = x[2n]$ and odd samples $x_o[n] = x[2n+1]$
2. **Predict**: predict odd samples from even samples; the prediction error = high-pass (detail) coefficient
   $$d[n] = x_o[n] - P(x_e)[n]$$
3. **Update**: update the even samples using the detail coefficients to preserve signal statistics; the result = low-pass (approximation) coefficient
   $$a[n] = x_e[n] + U(d)[n]$$

**Advantages of lifting:**
- **In-place computation**: can be done without auxiliary memory
- **Integer wavelet transforms**: if the predict/update operations round to integers, the transform is integer-to-integer → **lossless coding possible**
- **Easy inverse**: just reverse the steps (negate the predict and update operations)
- **Faster**: roughly 2× faster than convolution-based DWT

> [!example] Lifting steps for the CDF 5/3 wavelet (used in JPEG 2000 lossless)
> **Predict step:** $d[n] = x_o[n] - \lfloor (x_e[n] + x_e[n+1]) / 2 \rfloor$
>
> **Update step:** $a[n] = x_e[n] + \lfloor (d[n-1] + d[n] + 2) / 4 \rfloor$
>
> All operations are integer: this defines an **integer DWT**, usable for lossless compression.

## Images compression with wavelets

### 2D-DWT of a Natural Image

> [!note] Sparsification effect of 2D-DWT
> For a natural image (not sparse in the pixel domain), the 2D-DWT progressively sparsifies the representation with each additional level.

**Evolution of sparsity with decomposition levels:**

| Levels | Effect |
|--------|--------|
| 0 (no DWT) | Image is not sparse — all pixels carry information |
| 1 level | Some sparsity achieved; but LL subband (1/4 of coefficients) still has many large values |
| 2 levels | Number of large coefficients drops dramatically — histogram becomes very peaked at 0 |
| 3–5 levels | Trend continues — histogram becomes increasingly sparse |
| > 5–6 levels | Further improvement is marginal; diminishing returns |

> [!example] Synthetic image (white square on black background)
> - **1 level**: LL subband = blurred square; detail subbands = edges only (very sparse)
> - **2 levels**: apply DWT again to LL → LL2 (tiny square) + a few detail coefficients; signal is **even sparser** (only LL subband + some details non-zero)
> - **3 levels**: non-zero coefficients are $1/64$ of the original number, plus "a few" detail coefficients
>
> After 3 levels, the synthetic image is *almost perfectly sparse* in the wavelet domain!

---

### Choosing the Number of Decomposition Levels

> [!important] Practical rule: 4 to 6 levels for natural images
> Typically, **4 to 6 levels** of 2D-DWT is the best choice. Further increasing the number of levels is often ineffective because:
>
> 1. **The LL subband becomes smaller and smaller**: a new level adds only a few coefficients. If the LL subband is already very small (e.g., 8×8 pixels), another level splits it into a 4×4 LL — very few new coefficients are gained.
>
> 2. **The low-resolution LL subband loses spatial smoothness**: the "trend + anomalies" model assumes the LL subband is smooth (so its DWT detail coefficients are small). But if the LL is already very small, it no longer looks like a smooth version of the original — it becomes irregular, and transforming it further does not improve sparsity.

---

### Compression with DWT: Two Families

After computing the 2D-DWT, we need to decide how to code the wavelet coefficients. There are two main approaches:

> [!abstract] Two approaches to wavelet-based compression

**1. Methods based on inter-scale dependencies (tree-based):**
- **EZW** (Embedded Zerotrees of Wavelet coefficients) — Shapiro, 1993
- **SPIHT** (Set Partitioning In Hierarchical Trees) — Said & Pearlman, 1996
- Use tree-structured dependencies across scales to efficiently code groups of insignificant coefficients
- **Advantages**: good exploitation of inter-scale dependencies, low complexity
- **Disadvantage**: no random access, no resolution scalability

**2. Methods not based on inter-scale dependencies (subband-based):**
- Explicit bit-rate allocation among subbands
- Entropy coding of coefficients per subband
- **Advantages**: good exploitation of intra-scale dependencies, random access, **resolution scalability**
- **Disadvantage**: no exploitation of inter-scale dependencies
- Used in **JPEG 2000**

## Embedded Zerotrees of Wavelet Coefficients (EZW)

### Main Characteristics

> [!important] EZW (Shapiro, 1993)
> EZW is a landmark algorithm in image compression with the following properties:
> - **Quality scalability** (progressive representation): the bitstream can be truncated at any point to obtain a valid (lower-quality) reconstruction
> - **Lossy-to-lossless coding**: if an integer DWT is used, coding all bitplanes gives the original image back (lossless); stopping early gives lossy compression
> - **Small complexity**: the algorithm is simple and fast
> - **Rate-distortion performance much better than JPEG**, especially **at low bitrates**

---

### Progressive Representation of DWT Coefficients

> [!note] The core idea of progressive coding
> In a progressive system, each new coding bit must:
> - **Convey the maximum amount of information**
> - **Reduce distortion as much as possible**
>
> This means: **send the largest coefficients first!** Large coefficients carry most of the signal energy.
>
> **Problem**: if we send coefficients in decreasing order of magnitude, we need to tell the decoder the position (location) of each coefficient. This "localization overhead" can be expensive.

---

### Subband Scan Order (SB Order)

The EZW algorithm scans the wavelet coefficients in a **subband-by-subband order**, following a specific zig-zag pattern:

```
[LL2 | HL2] → [HL1] → [LH2 | HH2] → [LH1] → [HH1]
```

The scan follows the **resolution levels from coarsest to finest**, visiting all subbands at a given scale before moving to finer scales. Within each subband, a raster scan is used.

> [!note] Is subband order enough?
> The subband scan order ensures that coarser-scale (higher-energy) subbands are visited first. However, **within each subband** the coefficients are not ordered by magnitude. The subband scan order alone is **not sufficient** to guarantee that the globally largest coefficients are sent first.
>
> We need a smarter approach — the **zero-tree** idea.

---

### Zero-Tree of Wavelet Coefficients

> [!important] Definition: Zero-tree
> Consider **trees** of transform coefficients across scales. The root is a coefficient in the **LL subband**. It has **three descendants**, one in each of the three detail subbands at the first resolution level (HL, LH, HH), at the colocated (same spatial) position. From the second level on, each coefficient has **four descendants**: the co-located coefficients in the same orientation subband at the next finer resolution level.

The tree structure reflects the spatial and orientation relationships between wavelet coefficients across scales.

**Key observation (auto-similarity / self-similarity):**
- A *small* coefficient (below a threshold $T$) in a subband corresponds to a spatial region with little energy at that scale
- Since the image is smooth in that region, we expect the *descendants* (the same region at finer scales) to also be small
- If a coefficient $c$ **and all its descendants** are below $T$, the subtree is called a **zero-tree**; $c$ is a **zero-tree root (ZR)**

> [!tip] Why zero-trees save bits
> A zero-tree root $c$ at level $n$ covers a subtree of $1 + 4 + 4^2 + \cdots + 4^{N-n}$ coefficients — potentially hundreds of coefficients. All of them are encoded with a **single symbol** (ZR), instead of coding each individually. This is an enormous compression gain!
>
> The localization information is **implicit** in the significance information — no extra overhead needed to specify the positions of insignificant coefficients.

---

### EZW Algorithm

The EZW algorithm uses **bitplane coding**: at each pass, it codes one bitplane of all coefficients (from most significant to least significant). The threshold decreases by halving at each pass.

**Initialization:**
1. $k = 0$ (pass counter)
2. $n = \lfloor \log_2(|c|_\text{max}) \rfloor$ (index of the most significant bitplane)
3. $T_k = 2^n$ (initial threshold)
4. $\mathcal{L}$ = list of all DWT coefficients, in SB scan order (each subband scanned in raster order)
5. $\mathcal{S} = \emptyset$ (list of significant coefficients found so far)

**Main loop:** `while (rate < available rate):`
- Perform **Dominant pass**
- Perform **Refining pass**
- $T_{k+1} \leftarrow T_k / 2$ (halve the threshold)
- $k \leftarrow k + 1$

---

#### Dominant Pass

> [!important] Dominant pass algorithm
> **Until $\mathcal{L}$ is not empty:**
>
> 1. Let $c$ be the first coefficient of $\mathcal{L}$
>    - If $c > T_k$: encode $c$ as **SP** (Significant Positive), add to $\mathcal{S}$
>    - Else if $c < -T_k$: encode $c$ as **SN** (Significant Negative), add to $\mathcal{S}$
>    - Else if $c$ is **below threshold**: check all descendants
>      - If **no descendant** has absolute value $\geq T$: encode $c$ as **Zero-tree Root (ZR)** and **remove all its descendants** from $\mathcal{L}$
>      - Else: encode $c$ as **Isolated Zero (IZ)** (it is small, but has significant descendants)
> 2. Remove $c$ from $\mathcal{L}$

**The four symbols:**

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| **SP** (Significant Positive) | $c \geq T_k$ |
| **SN** (Significant Negative) | $c \leq -T_k$ |
| **ZR** (Zero-tree Root) | $\|c\| < T_k$ and **all descendants** $< T_k$ |
| **IZ** (Isolated Zero) | $\|c\| < T_k$ but some descendant $\geq T_k$ |

> [!note] Symbol probabilities
> - ZR is by far the most frequent symbol (sparse wavelet coefficients!)
> - SP/SN become more frequent as we descend bitplanes (more coefficients become significant)
> - IZ is relatively rare: a "hole" in an otherwise-zero tree
>
> These symbols are coded with an **arithmetic coder** (not Huffman) for maximum efficiency.

---

#### Refining Pass

> [!important] Refining pass algorithm
> 1. Let $b = \log_2(T_k)$ be the current bitplane index
> 2. **For all** $c \in \mathcal{S}$ (coefficients already declared significant): encode the $b$-th bit of the binary representation of $|c|$

The refining pass adds **one bit of precision** to each coefficient that has already been declared significant. This gradually refines the quantization of significant coefficients.

---

#### Iteration and Termination

> [!note] Bitplane coding interpretation
> - The $k$-th dominant pass codes the $b$-th bitplane (identifies coefficients with $2^b \leq |c| < 2^{b+1}$)
> - The refining pass codes the $b$-th bit of previously significant coefficients
> - After the dominant pass, we halve the threshold: $T_{k+1} = T_k/2$ → equivalent to passing to the next (less significant) bitplane
>
> **Termination:** the algorithm stops when:
> - The **bit budget** is exhausted (target bitrate reached), or
> - All **bitplanes** have been coded (lossless reconstruction achieved)

---

#### EZW Algorithm: Summary

> [!abstract] EZW summary
> 1. **Bitplane coding**: at the $k$-th pass, we encode bitplane $\log_2 T_k$
> 2. **Progressive coding**: each new bitplane refines the quantization of all coefficients — the reconstruction quality improves monotonically with every decoded bit
> 3. **Lossless coding of significance symbols**: the significance map (SP/SN/ZR/IZ) can be coded losslessly (e.g., with arithmetic coding)
> 4. **Lossy-to-lossless coding**: when an integer wavelet transform is used (all operations exact in integer arithmetic), and **all bitplanes are coded**, the original image can be restored with **zero distortion** (lossless)
>
> The bitstream can be **truncated at any point** to obtain a valid lossy reconstruction — this is the "embedded" property of EZW.

## JPEG 2000

### Overview

> [!important] JPEG 2000 standard
> **JPEG 2000** (ISO/IEC 15444) is the successor to JPEG, standardized in 2000. It is based on the **Discrete Wavelet Transform** instead of the DCT.
>
> Key features of JPEG 2000:
> - **Lossy and lossless** compression in the same standard
> - **Progressive transmission** by quality and by resolution
> - **Region of Interest (ROI)** coding: some regions can be coded at higher quality than others
> - **Error resilience**: designed for noisy channels (e.g., wireless, internet)
> - **Scalability**: spatial, quality, and component scalability
> - No blocking artefacts (no fixed blocks)

---

### JPEG 2000 Pipeline

```
Input Image
  → Color Space Conversion (RGB → YCbCr, or ICT/RCT)
  → [Optional] Tiling
  → DC Level Shifting
  → DWT (CDF 9/7 for lossy, CDF 5/3 for lossless)
  → Quantization (scalar, uniform dead-zone)
  → EBCOT (Embedded Block Coding with Optimal Truncation)
       → Tier 1: bitplane coding of code blocks (MQ coder)
       → Tier 2: rate allocation and packetization
  → JPEG 2000 Bitstream
```

---

### Pre-processing

#### Color Space Conversion

For lossy compression: **ICT** (Irreversible Color Transform) — equivalent to the standard RGB→YCbCr conversion using floating-point coefficients:

$$\begin{bmatrix} Y \\ C_b \\ C_r \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.299 & 0.587 & 0.114 \\ -0.16875 & -0.33126 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & -0.41869 & -0.08131 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} R \\ G \\ B \end{bmatrix}$$

For lossless compression: **RCT** (Reversible Color Transform) — an integer approximation:

$$Y = \left\lfloor \frac{R + 2G + B}{4} \right\rfloor, \quad C_b = B - G, \quad C_r = R - G$$

with $G = Y - \lfloor (C_b + C_r)/4 \rfloor$ for reconstruction. This is perfectly reversible in integer arithmetic.

---

#### Tiling

> [!note] Tiles in JPEG 2000
> JPEG 2000 supports **tiling**: dividing the image into non-overlapping rectangular tiles, each coded independently. Benefits:
> - Enables **random access** to parts of the image (decode only the needed tile)
> - Reduces memory requirements for large images
> - Each tile can have its own DWT decomposition level count and quantization parameters
>
> When a tile size equals the full image size (one tile), the result is equivalent to global DWT — typically best for compression performance.

---

### DWT in JPEG 2000

JPEG 2000 uses **two DWT options**:

| Mode | Filter bank | Transform type | Application |
|------|-------------|----------------|-------------|
| Lossy | **CDF 9/7** (biorthogonal) | Floating-point (irreversible) | Best compression performance |
| Lossless | **CDF 5/3** (biorthogonal) | Integer (reversible, via lifting) | Perfect reconstruction |

Both are implemented using the **lifting scheme** for efficiency.

**Number of decomposition levels:** typically **5 levels** (configurable). Each level applies the 2D-DWT to the LL subband.

---

### Quantization in JPEG 2000

> [!important] Scalar uniform dead-zone quantization
> JPEG 2000 uses **scalar quantization** with a **dead zone** (larger zero-bin). Each wavelet coefficient $c_{b,k}$ in subband $b$ is quantized as:
>
> $$\tilde{c}_{b,k} = \text{sign}(c_{b,k}) \cdot \left\lfloor \frac{|c_{b,k}|}{\Delta_b} \right\rfloor$$
>
> where $\Delta_b$ is the **quantization step size** for subband $b$.

The step size $\Delta_b$ for subband $b$ is specified as:

$$\Delta_b = 2^{R_b - \epsilon_b} (1 + \mu_b / 2^{11})$$

where $R_b$ is the dynamic range of the subband, $\epsilon_b$ is the **exponent** (3-bit field), and $\mu_b$ is the **mantissa** (11-bit field). Both are encoded in the JPEG 2000 codestream header.

For **lossless mode**, quantization step = 1 (no quantization applied).

### EBCOT: Embedded Block Coding with Optimal Truncation

> [!important] EBCOT (Taubman, 2000)
> EBCOT is the entropy coding engine of JPEG 2000. It works in two tiers:
>
> - **Tier 1**: bitplane coding of small **code blocks** (typically 64×64 or 32×32 wavelet coefficients) using a **context-based arithmetic coder (MQ coder)**
> - **Tier 2**: rate allocation — find the optimal truncation point for each code block's embedded bitstream to meet a global rate target

#### Tier 1: Bitplane Coding of Code Blocks

Each subband is divided into **code blocks** (typically 64×64 coefficients). Each code block is coded **independently**, producing an **embedded bitstream** — a stream that can be truncated at any point.

For each bitplane $b$ (from MSB to LSB), three coding passes are performed:

| Coding pass | What it does |
|-------------|-------------|
| **Significance propagation pass** | Codes coefficients adjacent to already-significant ones |
| **Magnitude refinement pass** | Codes refinement bits for already-significant coefficients |
| **Cleanup pass** | Codes remaining coefficients not yet visited |

Each coefficient is coded using a **context-adaptive binary arithmetic coder (MQ coder)**. The context is determined by the significance state of neighbouring coefficients.

> [!note] Context model
> The coding context for a coefficient includes the significance states of its 8 neighbours (and some sign information). There are 9 significance contexts, 5 refinement contexts, and 3 run-length contexts.

#### Tier 2: Rate Allocation (PCRD-opt)

> [!important] Post-Compression Rate-Distortion Optimization (PCRD-opt)
> After Tier 1 coding, each code block has an **embedded bitstream** with a rate-distortion curve: for each possible truncation point, we know the resulting bitrate and distortion.
>
> Tier 2 uses the **PCRD-opt algorithm** (a form of greedy slope selection) to find the **optimal truncation point** for each code block, subject to the total bitrate constraint:
>
> $$\min \sum_b D_b(R_b) \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_b R_b \leq R_\text{Tot}$$
>
> This is exactly the Huang-Schulteiss type of allocation, but applied to code blocks instead of individual coefficients.

### Packet Structure and Scalability

> [!note] Packets = unit of scalability
> In JPEG 2000, the bitstream is organized into **packets**. Each packet contains the data from one **layer** (quality level) for one **resolution level** for one **tile** for one **component**.
>
> This organization enables:
> - **Quality scalability**: receive more packets = better quality
> - **Resolution scalability**: receive packets from lower resolution levels = lower resolution image
> - **Region of interest**: decode only the packets covering a spatial region
> - **Component scalability**: decode only selected color components

**Progression orders** (the order in which packets appear in the bitstream):

| Order | Meaning |
|-------|---------|
| LRCP | Layer → Resolution → Component → Position |
| RLCP | Resolution → Layer → Component → Position |
| RPCL | Resolution → Position → Component → Layer |
| PCRL | Position → Component → Resolution → Layer |
| CPRL | Component → Position → Resolution → Layer |

Each progression order enables a different type of scalability as the primary axis.

### JPEG 2000 vs JPEG: Comparison

> [!abstract] Comparison summary

| Feature | JPEG | JPEG 2000 |
|---------|------|-----------|
| **Transform** | 8×8 block DCT | Full-image DWT (with tiling) |
| **Filter bank** | N/A | CDF 9/7 (lossy), CDF 5/3 (lossless) |
| **Blocking artefacts** | Yes (at low bitrates) | No |
| **Ringing artefacts** | Yes (near edges) | Much less |
| **Lossless mode** | JPEG-LS (separate standard) | Built-in (CDF 5/3 + integer DWT) |
| **Progressive transmission** | Limited (sequential/progressive) | Full quality + resolution scalability |
| **Region of Interest** | No | Yes |
| **Random access** | No | Yes (tile-based) |
| **Compression performance** | Good at mid/high bitrates | Better at all bitrates, especially low |
| **Complexity** | Low | Higher |
| **Adoption** | Universal | Medical, cinema (DCP), archival |

> [!tip] When is JPEG 2000 used?
> Despite superior compression performance, JPEG 2000 is not universally used for web images (JPEG is simpler, faster, and universally supported). JPEG 2000 is the standard for:
> - **Medical imaging** (DICOM)
> - **Digital cinema** (DCP — Digital Cinema Package)
> - **Archival and digitization** (Library of Congress, National Archives)
> - **Satellite and aerial imagery** (remote sensing)

## Conclusion

> [!abstract] Key takeaways
> 1. **JPEG limitations**: blocking artefacts, ringing, no scalability — motivate a better approach
> 2. **Wavelets** offer time-frequency localization: they capture both where and at what scale signal features occur
> 3. **DWT** = iterated filter bank, applied to the low-pass subband; no redundancy (same number of coefficients as input)
> 4. **2D-DWT** is separable: applied to rows then columns, producing 4 subbands (LL, HL, LH, HH)
> 5. **Multi-level DWT** recursively decomposes the LL subband → produces a multi-resolution pyramid
> 6. **Sparsification**: 4–6 levels of 2D-DWT on a natural image creates a very sparse representation (histogram very peaked at 0)
> 7. **EZW**: exploits inter-scale dependencies (zero-trees) for progressive, embedded coding; excellent RD performance at all bitrates
> 8. **JPEG 2000**: combines 2D-DWT (CDF 9/7 or 5/3) with EBCOT's bitplane coding and PCRD-opt rate allocation; supports lossless + lossy, quality + resolution scalability, ROI coding
> 9. **Lifting scheme**: efficient integer implementation of DWT, enabling lossless wavelet transforms
> 10. Wavelets outperform JPEG especially at **low bitrates**, where JPEG shows severe blocking; JPEG 2000 degradation is graceful (blurry but artifact-free)

> [!tip] The bigger picture
> Transform coding (DCT → wavelet → learned transforms) follows a consistent progression:
> - **DCT (JPEG)**: block-local, real cosines, fixed basis — good but limited
> - **DWT (JPEG 2000)**: multi-scale, local in space and frequency, flexible basis — better
> - **Learned transforms (neural codecs)**: data-driven, non-linear, optimized end-to-end — state of the art
>
> Each step improves sparsification and exploits signal structure more effectively.
