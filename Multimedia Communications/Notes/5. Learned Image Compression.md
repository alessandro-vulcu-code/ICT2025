## Introduction: From Classical to Learned Compression

### What is Learned Image Compression (LIC)?

> [!important] Definition
> **Learned Image Compression (LIC)**, also called *Neural Image Compression (NIC)*, refers to the application of **neural networks and machine learning** to data compression tasks.

**Why "Learned"?**
- Traditional codecs (JPEG, JPEG 2000, …) use **hand-crafted rules**: fixed linear transforms (DCT, DWT), manually designed quantization tables, and human-engineered entropy coders
- LIC algorithms are learned **end-to-end from data** using generative models such as **Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)** and **GANs**; the transform itself is *born* from the data

> [!note] State of the Art
> While neural methods were once experimental (late 1980s), modern models now **outperform** the best handcrafted codecs (like JPEG 2000) in international challenges. JPEG AI (2025) is the first international standard built entirely on deep learning.

---

### Evolution of the Paradigm

> [!abstract] Classical Approach (JPEG / JPEG 2000)
> Relies on **hand-crafted linear transforms** (DCT, Wavelets) designed by experts to de-correlate pixels based on *general statistical assumptions*. The transform is fixed; only its parameters (quantization table, rate allocation) are adapted.

> [!abstract] Neural Approach
> Relies on **learned non-linear transforms**. The mapping from pixels to coefficients is optimized **directly from data**. Two key differences:
>
> - **Linearity**: Classical transforms are linear; NIC uses non-linear activations (ReLU, GDN) to capture complex structures that linear methods miss
> - **Adaptivity**: Classical standards adapt via parameters (e.g., custom Q-tables) but rely on fixed basis functions. LIC **learns the transform itself** directly from the data distribution

---

### The Linear Limit: KLT vs. Neural

> [!note] Recall: KLT
> The Karhunen-Loève Transform (KLT) is the *optimal linear transform* for Gaussian data because it perfectly decorrelates components.

| KLT (Linear Optimal) | Neural (Non-linear) |
|---|---|
| Data-dependent but **linear** | Learns a **non-linear manifold**, capturing high-order dependencies (edges, textures) that linear methods miss |
| Optimal *only* for Gaussian sources | Works for arbitrary distributions |
| Computationally heavy for large blocks | CNN architectures with weight-sharing process large receptive fields without block-based constraints |

> [!important] Key Insight
> **NIC can be viewed as a *Non-linear KLT* optimized for Rate-Distortion.** It is the generalization of the classical transform coding pipeline where the transform is no longer restricted to be linear.

---

### A Generalized Coding Architecture

Even with the transition to deep learning, the core structural flow of transform coding **remains remarkably consistent**:

$$x \text{ (Original)} \xrightarrow{\text{Analysis}} z = g_a(x) \xrightarrow{\text{Quantization}} \hat{z} = Q(z) \xrightarrow{\text{Synthesis}} \hat{x} = g_s(\hat{z}) \text{ (Decoded)}$$

- **Legacy Frameworks**: the Analysis and Synthesis steps rely on fixed linear operations like DCT or DWT
- **Learned Paradigms**: these stages are replaced by **non-linear neural networks** ($g_a$ and $g_s$) that are optimized directly from data

> [!tip] Terminology Bridge
> What we called "Transform Coefficients" in JPEG are called **"Latent Variables"** (or the **latent tensor**) in Neural Compression.

---

### Two Philosophies of R-D Optimization

The transition to learned compression marks a fundamental shift in how we approach Rate-Distortion optimization:

> [!note] Combinatorial Selection (Classical)
> Optimization is a **search process** over a discrete set of options:
> - We choose the best configuration from *predefined tools* (e.g., selecting quantization steps in JPEG or bit-plane truncation in EZW/EBCOT)
> - The "dictionary" of transforms and filters is **fixed**; we only optimize their usage

> [!important] Continuous Learning (Neural)
> Optimization is a **design process** over a continuous space:
> - We use **gradient descent** to physically shape the filters (weights) of the network by minimizing a differentiable loss function:
> $$\boxed{\mathcal{L} = D(x, \hat{x}) + \lambda R(\hat{z})}$$
> - The transform itself is "born" from the data and tailored to the specific bit-rate target ($\lambda$)
> - $\lambda$ controls the trade-off: **high $\lambda$ → high compression** (rate dominates), **low $\lambda$ → high quality** (distortion dominates)

## Basics: Deep Learning for Multimedia

### Fundamentals: The Artificial Neuron

> [!note] The artificial neuron
> The foundational building block of neural networks is the **artificial neuron**. It computes a weighted sum of incoming signals, adds a bias term, and applies a non-linear **activation function** $f(\cdot)$ to produce the output:
>
> $$y = f(w^T x + b) = f(w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \cdots + w_n x_n + b)$$

**Common activation functions:**

| Activation | Formula | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **ReLU** | $f(z) = \max(0, z)$ | 0 for negative inputs, linear for positive |
| **Sigmoid** | $f(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$ | Squashes output to $(0,1)$ |
| **Tanh** | $f(z) = \tanh(z)$ | Squashes output to $(-1, 1)$ |
| **GDN** | See dedicated section | Specifically designed for image compression |

> [!tip] Why non-linearity?
> Without the activation function $f(\cdot)$, a stack of neurons would collapse to a single linear transformation — no matter how deep the network. Non-linear activations are what give neural networks the ability to approximate arbitrarily complex functions.

### The Multilayer Perceptron (MLP)

In an **MLP**, layers are **Fully Connected**: every neuron connects to *all* neurons in the previous layer. For a network with one hidden layer:

$$\boldsymbol{h} = f(W_1\boldsymbol{x} + \boldsymbol{b}_1) \qquad \boldsymbol{y} = g(W_2\boldsymbol{h} + \boldsymbol{b}_2)$$

- $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{n_h \times n_x}$, $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y \times n_h}$ — weight matrices
- $\boldsymbol{b}_1, \boldsymbol{b}_2$ — bias vectors
- Computations are heavily vectorized → efficient **GPU acceleration** via matrix multiplication

![[Pasted image 20260408135252.png|400]]

---

### Gradient Descent and Backpropagation

> [!important] Learning as optimization
> Learning is an optimization problem. We define a **Loss Function** $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ that quantifies the prediction error and update the parameters $\theta$ (weights and biases) iteratively *against the gradient*:
>
> $$\boxed{\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t)}$$
>
> where $\eta > 0$ is the **learning rate** — controls how large each update step is.

> [!note] Backpropagation
> In deep architectures, finding which hidden weights caused an error is challenging. **Backpropagation** solves this by systematically applying the **calculus chain rule** from the output backwards, providing the exact gradient for every parameter.
>
> Without backpropagation, training deep networks would be computationally intractable.

---

### NN and Images: The Dimensionality Curse of MLPs

A naive approach would flatten a 2D image into a 1D vector and feed it to an MLP. For a $4 \times 4$ image → $\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{16}$. For a realistic $256 \times 256$ image → $\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{65536}$.

**Flattening treats all pixels as independent inputs**, ignoring their spatial topology. Near and far pixels are treated in the same way — this is wrong for images.

![[Pasted image 20260408135348.png]]

> [!warning] The Dimensionality Curse of MLPs
> **No Translation Invariance**: without a specific structure, the model is forced to redundantly learn to recognize the same pattern (e.g., an edge) independently at every possible location.
>
> **Parameter Explosion**: connecting every pixel to every neuron in the next layer leads to *millions of weights* even for small images. A 256×256 image connected to a layer of 1000 neurons requires 65,536,000 parameters — per layer!
>
> **Training Difficulty**: this over-parameterization makes the model highly prone to **overfitting** and computationally intractable to train.
>
> **Challenge**: we need an architecture that "understands" proximity and can **reuse learned patterns**.

---

### Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)

> [!important] Why CNNs?
> CNNs solve the scalability wall by embedding a **spatial prior** directly into their architecture, naturally exploiting local correlations.

**Key Architectural Features:**

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Local Connectivity & Weight Sharing** | Small filters slide across the image. Guarantees **translation invariance** and drastically reduces parameter count |
| **Strided Convolutions (Analysis)** | Using a stride $s > 1$ reduces spatial dimensions, compacting information into multiple channels (learned downsampling) |
| **Transposed Convolutions (Synthesis)** | Learned upsampling operations that reconstruct the full-resolution image from the compressed latent tensor |

---

#### Visualizing the Convolution

> [!example] How a convolution works
> A small filter (kernel) — e.g., $3 \times 3$ — **slides across** the input image. At each step:
> 1. The filter aligns with a local region of the image (**receptive field**)
> 2. An **element-wise multiplication** is computed between the filter weights and the image patch
> 3. All products are **summed** to produce a single output value in the **feature map**
>![[Pasted image 20260408135506.png]]
>
> The same filter weights are applied at *every position* of the image — this is **weight sharing**, which guarantees translation invariance.
>
> Using a **stride** $s > 1$ means the filter "skips" $s-1$ pixels between positions, reducing the spatial size of the output feature map and compacting the visual information.
> ![[Pasted image 20260408135520.png]]

---

#### Parallel Feature Extraction

A single kernel acts as a *specific detector* (e.g., horizontal edges). Natural images are too complex to be described by just one type of feature.

> [!note] Building the 3D Latent Tensor
> - **Parallel Kernels**: we apply **multiple independent kernels** to the same input area simultaneously
> - **Channels as Features**: each output "channel" (or feature map) represents the activation of one specific detector across the image
> - **Dimensionality**: this transforms the 2D input ($H \times W$) into a **3D Tensor** ($H' \times W' \times C$), where $C$ = number of channels (features)

![[Pasted image 20260408135620.png]]

In a typical neural codec (e.g., JPEG AI), the encoder maps an $H \times W$ image to a latent tensor of spatial dimensions $H/16 \times W/16$ (after 4 strided convolutions, each with stride 2) and $C = 160$ channels — a *compact, information-rich representation*.

---

### Generalized Divisive Normalization (GDN)

> [!important] GDN activation function
> Instead of simple activations (like ReLU), neural codecs use **GDN** — an activation function specifically designed for compression. For a feature $v_i$, the normalized output $w_i$ is:
>
> $$\boxed{w_i = \frac{v_i}{\sqrt{\beta_i + \sum_j \gamma_{ij} v_j^2}}}$$
>
> where $\beta_i$ and $\gamma_{ij}$ are **learned parameters**, and the sum is over neighbouring features/channels.

**Why does GDN work better for compression?**

| Property | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Lateral Inhibition** | The denominator measures *local energy*. If neighbouring features ($v_j$) are large, the response of $v_i$ is *suppressed* — mimicking competition between neurons |
| **Link to Perception** | Mathematically models **masking effects** in the human visual system (a strong stimulus hides weaker nearby stimuli) |
| **Gaussianization** | It **decorrelates** the signal and makes the latent distributions roughly Gaussian — which is **optimal for entropy coding** |

> [!tip] GDN vs ReLU for compression
> ReLU only rectifies; GDN actively *normalizes* features by their local context. This Gaussianization of the latent space is crucial: entropy coding (e.g., arithmetic coding) works best when the symbol distribution is close to Gaussian or Laplacian.

---

### Transposed Convolutions

> [!note] The decoder's challenge
> To reconstruct the image from the compressed latents, the Decoder needs to **increase the spatial resolution**. This is achieved via **Transposed Convolutions** — *Learned Upsampling*.
>
> Unlike fixed interpolation (e.g., bilinear), transposed convolutions **learn** the optimal basis functions to reconstruct the signal from the latent space.

**The mechanism (Broadcasting):**

1. **1-to-N Mapping**: a single value from the input latent tensor is multiplied by the kernel weights and "stamped" onto the output grid
2. **Fractional Stride**: using a stride $s > 1$ dictates how far apart these kernels are stamped on the output, effectively **increasing the spatial dimensions**
3. **Overlap & Sum**: where the stamped kernels overlap in the output grid, their values are summed together to form the final reconstructed pixels

> [!tip] Intuition
> A normal convolution *aggregates* a neighbourhood into one value (N-to-1). A transposed convolution *spreads* one value into a neighbourhood (1-to-N). They are exact mathematical transposes of each other — hence the name.

## The Neural Compression Paradigm

### The Core Idea: From Pixels to a Latent Tensor

> [!important] Key idea: global learned transform
> Instead of transforming **fixed 8×8 blocks** (like JPEG), neural compression transforms the **entire image** (or large regions) into a **deep 3D Latent Tensor**.

**How the tensor is built:**

- **Spatial Reduction**: strided convolutions compress the spatial dimensions ($W, H$). Typically, 4 stages of stride-2 convolutions reduce spatial size by a factor of 16
- **Channel Expansion**: to avoid losing information, the network learns multiple **parallel features** (Channels). Each channel represents a different structural aspect of the image

> [!example] JPEG AI example
> To achieve state-of-the-art compression, **JPEG AI** maps the input image into a latent tensor with:
> - **160 channels** for Luminance ($Y$)
> - **96 channels** for Chrominance ($C_b$, $C_r$)
>
> This is dramatically more channels than the 64 coefficients of an 8×8 DCT block — capturing far richer spatial and structural information.

---

### The Autoencoder Framework

> [!important] Definition: Autoencoder for compression
> In learned compression, the traditional transform coding pipeline is replaced by an **Autoencoder** architecture with three components:
>
> 1. **Analysis Transform ($g_a$)**: the *Encoder*. Uses CNNs (with GDN activations and strided convolutions) to map the image $x$ to the latent tensor $y = g_a(x)$
> 2. **Bottleneck**: this is where compression happens. The latents $y$ are **quantized** to $\hat{y} = Q(y)$ and entropy-coded
> 3. **Synthesis Transform ($g_s$)**: the *Decoder*. Uses CNNs (with inverse GDN and transposed convolutions) to reconstruct the image $\hat{x} = g_s(\hat{y})$ from the quantized latents

$$x \xrightarrow{g_a} y \xrightarrow{Q} \hat{y} \xrightarrow{\text{entropy code}} \text{bitstream} \xrightarrow{\text{entropy decode}} \hat{y} \xrightarrow{g_s} \hat{x}$$

---

### The Rate-Distortion VAE

> [!important] Connection to Variational Autoencoders
> Modern neural codecs are instances of **Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)** optimized for a specific objective.
>
> Compression aims to minimize the **Lagrangian**:
>
> $$\boxed{\mathcal{L} = \underbrace{D_{KL}[q(\hat{y}|x) \,\|\, p(\hat{y})]}_{\text{Rate }(R)} + \lambda\, \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\rho(x, \hat{x})]}_{\text{Distortion }(D)}}$$

**Interpreting the terms:**
- **Rate $R$**: the KL divergence between the learned posterior $q(\hat{y}|x)$ (what the encoder produces) and the prior $p(\hat{y})$ (what the entropy coder expects). Minimizing this = compressing the latents more efficiently
- **Distortion $D$**: the expected reconstruction error $\rho(x, \hat{x})$ — e.g., MSE, MS-SSIM, or a perceptual metric
- **$\lambda$**: controls the trade-off
  - High $\lambda$ → rate dominates → **high compression, lower quality**
  - Low $\lambda$ → distortion dominates → **high quality, lower compression**

> [!note] Training multiple points on the R-D curve
> By training the same architecture with different values of $\lambda$, one obtains a family of models covering the full rate-distortion curve — analogous to JPEG quality factors, but learned end-to-end.

---

### Why Compress in the Latent Space?

Instead of quantizing pixels directly, we quantize the output of the Analysis Transform ($y = g_a(x)$).

| In Pixel Space | In Latent Space |
|---|---|
| High redundancy — adjacent pixels are highly correlated | Features are **decorrelated** — latents are more statistically independent |
| Difficult to model probability distributions | Distributions are more predictable (Gaussian / Laplace) |
| Quantization errors are highly visible (blocking, ringing) | The network can hide quantization noise in less "perceptually important" channels |

> [!tip] Intuition
> The analysis transform $g_a$ is specifically trained to *remove redundancy* and *Gaussianize* the latent variables. Once in the latent space, standard entropy coders (arithmetic coding) work very efficiently, and the decoder ($g_s$) is trained to make quantization errors visually imperceptible.

---

### The Non-Differentiability Problem

> [!warning] A fundamental training obstacle
> The quantization operator $\hat{z} = Q(z) = \text{round}(z)$ is a **staircase function**.
>
> **The Gradient Issue:**
> - The derivative of $Q(z)$ is **zero almost everywhere** (flat steps)
> - Backpropagation *cannot pass through the quantizer* to update the encoder weights
>
> **The Mismatch**: we need "hard" quantization at *test time* (to get actual discrete bits), but we need "soft" or differentiable operations *during training*.

---

### Solution: Additive Uniform Noise

> [!important] The proxy operation
> To enable training, we replace rounding with **Additive Uniform Noise**:
>
> **During training:** $\hat{z} \approx z + u$, where $u \sim \mathcal{U}(-0.5, 0.5)$
>
> **During inference (test time):** $\hat{z} = \text{round}(z)$ (standard hard quantization)

**Why does this work?**

1. **Differentiability**: adding continuous noise makes the operation differentiable — gradients flow freely through the network
2. **Continuous relaxation**: it is a continuous relaxation of the discrete quantization process
3. **Mathematical link**: at high resolution, quantization noise acts like independent white noise, which is exactly $\mathcal{U}(-0.5, 0.5)$ — so the approximation is theoretically grounded

> [!note] Alternative approaches
> - **Straight-Through Estimator (STE)**: pretend the gradient of the quantizer is 1 (identity) during backpropagation — simple but crude
> - **Soft quantization**: use a smooth approximation (e.g., sigmoid-based) during training, switch to hard rounding at test time
> - **Additive noise** (used here): the most principled approach, connecting to the VAE framework naturally

---

### The Scale Hyperprior: Spatial Adaptability

> [!warning] Limitation of the basic VAE
> A fixed probability model assumes all latents are independent. In reality, latents exhibit **strong spatial correlation** in their variance (scale). The variance of a latent at position $(i,j)$ is typically similar to its neighbours — this structure is not captured by a fixed prior $p(\hat{y})$.

> [!important] The Hyperprior Concept (Ballé et al., 2018)
> We transmit additional **side information** $\hat{z}$ (the **Hyperprior**) to dynamically predict the probability distribution of the latents $\hat{y}$ at each spatial location.

**The Mechanism (A Second Autoencoder):**

$$x \xrightarrow{g_a} y \xrightarrow{Q} \hat{y} \xrightarrow{g_s} \hat{x}$$
$$\quad\quad\quad\quad \searrow_{h_a} z \xrightarrow{Q} \hat{z} \xrightarrow{h_s} \sigma \nearrow$$

- **Hyper-Encoder ($h_a$)**: operates in parallel to the main encoder ($g_a$). It analyzes the latents $y$ to extract a compressed **hyper-latent** $z$ — the hyperprior
- **Hyper-Decoder ($h_s$)**: decodes $\hat{z}$ to predict the **standard deviation** ($\sigma$) for the conditional prior: $p(\hat{y}|\hat{z}) = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$

> [!tip] Why this matters
> The hyperprior tells the entropy coder: *"at this spatial location, the latent is likely to be large/small"*. This allows the arithmetic coder to assign short codes to likely values and long codes to unlikely values — dramatically improving compression efficiency.
>
> **The total rate** now includes both the main latents $\hat{y}$ and the side information $\hat{z}$. The hyperprior is kept very compact, so the overhead is small compared to the gain in coding efficiency.

## JPEG-AI

### JPEG AI: Standardizing Neural Compression

> [!important] What is JPEG AI?
> Standardized as **ISO/IEC 29170-2**, **JPEG AI** is the **first international standard for image coding based entirely on Deep Learning** technologies.
>
> **Timeline:** developed between 2020 and 2024, finalized in early 2025.
>
> **Objective:** to outperform **HEVC (Intra)** and **VVC (Intra)** by **50% in coding efficiency** while supporting both **human and machine tasks**.

> [!note] The Paradigm Shift
> While legacy standards (JPEG, JPEG 2000) are built on hand-crafted linear transforms, JPEG AI is the industrial crystallization of the **Nonlinear Transform Coding** paradigm.

---

### Methodology: The Hierarchical VAE

The technical backbone of JPEG AI is a refined version of the **Scale Hyperprior Autoencoder**:

$$x \xrightarrow{\text{Analysis}} \hat{y} \text{ (Latents)} \xrightarrow{\text{Synthesis}} \hat{x}$$
$$\quad\quad\quad\quad \uparrow\downarrow \text{ Hyperprior } \hat{h} \xrightarrow{\sigma}$$

- **Analysis/Synthesis Transforms**: deep CNNs optimized for Rate-Distortion
- **Quantization**: uses rounding proxies (additive uniform noise) during training to handle gradients
- **Side Information**: the hyperprior $\hat{h}$ captures the **non-stationary statistics** of the image, acting as a dynamic "entropy-coding guide"

**Latent tensor dimensions:**
- **Luminance**: 160 channels
- **Chrominance**: 96 channels

These are spatially downsampled versions of the input ($\times 1/16$ in each dimension), providing a rich, compact representation.

---

### Multimodality: Human vs. Machine Vision

> [!important] The "AI-Ready" Design
> One of the most innovative features of JPEG AI is its **dual-use bitstream**. A single compressed file can be decoded in **two different ways**:
>
> 1. **Human Vision Decoder**: reconstructs standard pixels for visual consumption (optimized for high **MS-SSIM**)
> 2. **Computer Vision Task Decoder**: extracts features directly from the latent space for tasks like **Object Detection** or **Segmentation** — *without full pixel reconstruction*

> [!tip] Why this matters
> In modern AI pipelines (smart cities, autonomous vehicles), images are compressed, transmitted, and then fed to a neural network for analysis — they are *never viewed by a human*. Full pixel reconstruction is wasteful.
>
> JPEG AI allows: **compress once → decode for humans OR machines**. This provides a **massive reduction in computational overhead** for large-scale AI pipelines.

---

### Performance: The Rate-Distortion Leap

> [!important] Experimental results
> Experimental validation by the JPEG Committee shows that JPEG AI fundamentally shifts the R-D curve compared to classical codecs.
>
> **Coding Gain:**
> - Up to **27% bit-rate saving** compared to **VVC/H.266** (the current state-of-the-art video codec used in intra mode)
> - Over an **order of magnitude** savings compared to the original JPEG (1992) for the same perceived utility

> [!note] Visual Quality at Low Rates
> At typical coding rates ($\sim 0.5$ bpp), JPEG AI **perfectly preserves high-frequency textures** that are often smoothed or "washed out" by traditional methods. This is because the decoder is a neural network that can *hallucinate* plausible high-frequency detail, rather than mechanically reconstructing quantized DCT coefficients.

---

### Performance: Hardware Scalability

> [!note] Complexity Profiles
> Beyond bit-rate, a standard must be *deployable*. JPEG AI introduces a novel approach to computational complexity.
>
> Complexity is measured in **kilo-Multiplication Accumulator Count per pixel (kMAC/px)** — a standard metric for neural network deployment.
>
> The standard defines **multiple synthesis transforms** that decode the **same bitstream** at different quality/complexity tiers:

| Profile  | Complexity  | Target hardware                                              |
| -------- | ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Dec0** | 8 kMAC/px   | Low-end CPUs                                                 |
| **Dec1** | 23 kMAC/px  | Real-time decoding on mid-range smartphones                  |
| **Dec2** | 214 kMAC/px | Transformer-based, high-end GPUs for maximum visual fidelity |

> [!tip] Scalable decoding from a single bitstream
> This is a key innovation: the *same compressed file* can be decoded by a simple network (fast, low quality) or a complex one (slow, high quality), depending on the available hardware. The encoder produces one bitstream; the decoder is chosen at runtime.

## Conclusion

### Conclusions: The Paradigm Shift

> [!important] What makes LIC fundamentally different
> **Learned Image Compression (LIC)** represents a fundamental methodological departure from classical codec design:
>
> - **End-to-End Optimization**: instead of manually designing and patching disparate blocks (transforms, quantization, entropy), the *entire pipeline* is jointly optimized for the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L} = R + \lambda D$
>
> - **Data-Driven Transforms**: the architecture learns non-linear, content-adaptive basis functions rather than relying on fixed linear models (like the DCT)

> [!abstract] State of the Art: The Grand Trade-off
> **Pros:** Unprecedented Rate-Distortion efficiency, officially surpassing the best classical video codecs (VVC) in static image coding.
>
> **Cons:** Massive computational weight. It requires millions of MAC operations per pixel, strictly demanding parallel hardware accelerators (GPUs/NPUs). Classical codecs run efficiently on any CPU; LIC requires dedicated silicon.

---

### Current Challenges and Limitations

Despite the R-D superiority, neural codecs face critical engineering hurdles before ubiquitous adoption:

> [!warning] Key Bottlenecks

**Energy Consumption:**
- The high kMAC/pixel ratio translates directly to battery drain on mobile devices
- High-resolution video deployment is currently prohibitive on battery-powered devices

**Cross-Platform Determinism:**
- Classical codecs guarantee **bit-exact reconstruction**: decoding the same bitstream on any platform gives the *exact same pixels*
- Neural networks often rely on floating-point math, which can yield **varying rounding results** across different GPUs, leading to severe **decoder drift** (slightly different images on different devices)

**Out-of-Distribution Vulnerability:**
- Since transforms are learned from datasets (e.g., natural photographs), highly unusual signals (e.g., specific scientific imagery, severe sensor noise, medical images) might be:
  - Poorly modelled (high distortion)
  - Reconstructed with visual "**hallucinations**" — the decoder invents detail that was not in the original

---

### Future Horizons in Neural Compression

The field is rapidly expanding beyond simple mathematical Rate-Distortion metrics:

> [!tip] Emerging Research Vectors

**Generative Compression:**
- Integrating **GANs** and **Diffusion Models** to synthesize perceptually perfect textures at ultra-low bitrates
- Prioritizes *realism* over exact pixel-by-pixel fidelity
- At very low bitrates, "reconstructing" from a learned prior can look better than mechanically decompressing DCT coefficients

**Coding for Machines (Task-Oriented):**
- Compressing data **not for human eyes**, but for downstream AI tasks
- Latent tensors can be fed directly to classifiers or autonomous driving networks **without ever reconstructing the RGB image**
- Saves both bandwidth and computation

**Hardware-Algorithm Co-design:**
- As NPUs (Neural Processing Units) become ubiquitous in mobile SoCs (e.g., Apple Neural Engine, Qualcomm Hexagon), future codecs will be co-designed directly with silicon engineers
- Goal: execute non-linear transforms with near-zero computational overhead
