## Quality assessment

- The purpose of **Quality assessment** in the context of digital multimedia services is to associate a quantitative grade to an image, a video, a sound, or any other possible *stimulus* i.e., a **signal** that can be perceived by a **human observer**
- Since a stimulus is a *signal*, quality assessment can be seen as a *functional*, i.e. an operator associating a number to one or more multimedia signals
  - In this case we are performing an **objective quality assessment**
- However, what finally matters is the impact on the observers: quality assessment can be performed by asking people to evaluate the stimuli
  - In this case we are performing a **subjective quality assessment**

---

## Why is QA needed?

- Quality assessment plays a major role in the design of Multimedia services: it is the "benefit" term in the cost-benefit analysis of a service: How good is the "quality" of the delivered signal?
- The cost depends on the application
- In the case of compression / transmission systems, the cost is typically the coding rate
  - But it can also include the number of operations (**time complexity**), the amount of memory (**space complexity**) required, the **energy** consumption and the **latency**

[Figura: Rate-distortion curves for HDR MPEG, HDRV, JPEG HDR fullsamp, JPEG HDR precorrect, JPEG HDR postcorrect — SNR (dB) vs bits per pixel]

---

## Quality / rate trade-off

- The trade-off between coding rate and quality is of central importance in our context
- While the coding rate (and, to some extent, the complexity) are easy-to-measure quantities, quality is a whole different game
- In this class we will present the different ways to assess the quality of images and videos
- Mastering the Quality / Rate trade-off is needed in order to make decisions in the design of a Multimedia Service systems
  - E.g.: how to compare two compression methods? how to set up a streaming service? etc.

## Subjective assessment

- **PRO**: The most reliable way to measure the quality of experience of multimedia services.
- **CON**: Subjective tests are usually costly and time-consuming and require many users to provide reliable results.
- To obtain reliable and reproducible subjective results, it is fundamental to define and describe each of the following elements very carefully:
  - **Laboratory equipment**, including screen and viewing distance, illumination and reverberation characteristics of the room.
  - **Data set**, including the original and processed contents and their distribution
  - **Test methodology**, including the rating target (quality, comparison, or impairment), the scale (categorical or continuous) and the type of stimuli (single or double).
  - **Score processing**, including score normalization, outlier detection, mean score and confidence intervals computation, and significance tests.
- **Subjective experiments remain the gold standard for perceptual quality evaluation.**

## Spatial Information

- Spatial Information ($SI$): A measure that indicates the **amount of spatial detail** in a picture. It is usually higher for more spatially complex scenes.
- The $SI$ is based on the Sobel filter, i.e. a discrete analog of the spatial gradient
- The output of a Sobel Filter is large near object contours and small in flat areas
- Each video frame at time $n$ ($F_n$) is first filtered with the Sobel filter [$Sobel(F_n)$]
- The standard deviation over the pixels ($\sigma_{space}$) in each Sobel filtered frame is then computed. This operation is repeated for each frame in the video sequence and results in a time series of $SI$ of the scene.
- The maximum value in the time series ($max_{time}$) is chosen to represent the $SI$ content of the scene. This process can be represented in equation form as:

$$SI = max_{time}\{\sigma_{space}[Sobel(F_n)]\}$$

## Temporal Information

- Temporal information ($TI$): a measure that indicates the amount of temporal changes of a video sequence. It is usually large for high-motion sequences
- $TI$ is based upon the motion difference feature, $M_n(i,j)$, that is the difference between the pixel values at the same location in space but at successive times or frames. $M_n(i,j)$ as a function of time ($n$) is defined as:

$$M_n(i,j) = F_n(i,j) - F_{n-1}(i,j)$$

here $F_n(i,j)$ is the pixel luminance at the i$^{th}$ row and j$^{th}$ column of n$^{th}$ frame in time. The $TI$ measure is computed as the maximum over time ($max_{time}$) of the SD over space ($\sigma_{space}$) of $M_n(i,j)$ over all i and j:

$$TI = max_{time}\{\sigma_{space}[M_n(i,j)]\}$$

Diverse motion in adjacent frames will result in higher values of $TI$.

## Instructions for the assessment

- Assessors should be carefully introduced to the method of assessment, the types of impairment or quality factors likely to occur, the grading scale, the sequence and timing
- Training sequences demonstrating the range and the type of the impairments to be assessed should be used with illustrating images other than those used in the test, but of comparable sensitivity.

---

## Test session

- A session should not last more than half an hour
- At the beginning of the first session, about five "dummy presentations" should be introduced to stabilize the observers' opinion. The data issued from these presentations must not be considered in the results of the test
- The images and impairments should be presented in a pseudo-random sequence and, preferably in a different sequence for each session

[Figura: Schema temporale di una sessione di test — Training sequence(s), poi Stabilizing sequence(s) (results for these items are not processed), poi Break (to allow time to answer questions from observers), poi Main part of test session. Riferimento: BT.0500-01-2]

---

## The learning effect

- Participants may
  - change their criteria,
  - become more sensitive (training),
  - become less sensitive (tiredness)
- To control the learning effect
  - Run training session that should cover the range of stimuli so that a participant can determine his/her criteria
  - Keep the sessions short (<20 min, <40 min)
  - Pay participants
  - Randomize stimuli (as much as possible)

## Single-stimulus (SS) or Absolute category rating (ACR)

- In SS methods, a single image or sequence of images is presented and the assessor provides an index of the entire presentation.
- The test material might include only test sequences or both the test sequences and their corresponding reference sequence.
- In the latter case, the reference sequence is presented as a freestanding stimulus for rating like any other test stimulus.

[Figura: Screenshot di interfaccia software per valutazione soggettiva della qualità di immagini con scala 0–5]

---

## Absolute category rating with hidden reference (ACR-HR)

- The test sequences are presented one at a time and are rated independently on a category scale. The test procedure **must include** a reference version of each test sequence shown as any other test stimulus. This is termed a hidden reference condition.
- During the data analysis, a differential mean opinion score (DMOS) of quality will be computed between each test sequence and its corresponding (hidden) reference

---

## Double Stimulus Impairment Scale (DSIS)

- Reference and processed sequence/image are shown
- **Viewers rate degradation** on discrete scale: imperceptible, perceptible but not annoying, fair, poor, bad

[Figura: Schema temporale DSIS — Reference Video (A, 10s), pausa (1s), Test Video (B, 10s), pausa (1s), Reference Video (A*, 10s), pausa (1s), Test Video (B*, 10s), pausa (1s), Vote 1 (5s). TOT = 49s. Scala di voto a destra da bad a excellent (0–100).]

- The assessor is first presented with an unimpaired reference, then with the same image impaired. Following this, he is asked to vote on the second, keeping in mind the first.
- The assessor is presented with a series of images or sequences in random order and with random impairments covering all required combinations.

## How Can We Combine Subjective Opinions?

**During the experiment:**
- Different observers gave different scores
- Some artefacts were considered more annoying
- Preferences were not always consistent
- Even identical videos produced different votes

**How can we obtain a reliable quality score from subjective evaluations?**

Subjective quality assessment requires statistical processing!

---

## How to process the MOS

- Measured values are considered outcomes of a random process
  - Example: $N = 30$ observers rated an image with score

$$MOS = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i$$

[Figura: Istogramma con distribuzione normale stimata e media stimata per N=30 osservatori. Asse x: Score (0–100). Funzione gaussiana $\frac{1}{s\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{(x-m)^2}{2s^2}}$]

- Assuming that the measured distribution is normal
  - mean – estimates as an average of observer rates

**Average quality is not enough.**
We also need:
- variability
- confidence
- statistical reliability

---

## Standard deviation and standard error

- The **standard deviation** measures observer agreement
- Small standard deviation: observers mostly agree
- Large standard deviation: opinions are scattered

Formula: $s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i - m)^2}$

- The **standard error** measures the uncertainty of the estimated MOS.
- $SE = \frac{s}{\sqrt{N}}$

$N$ = sample size, $m$ = mean

[Figura: Tre istogrammi per n=5, n=50, n=1000 con distribuzione della popolazione (verde) e distribuzione della media (rosso)]

More observers:
- do NOT reduce disagreement (same $s$)
- but reduce uncertainty on the MOS estimate

---

## Confidence intervals

- Now that we know the uncertainty of the MOS estimate, we can compute a confidence interval
- If the same experiment is repeated with the same number of observers, in 95% of the cases the average value is expected to be within the range of the confidence interval

$$ci = [-1.96 \cdot SE; 1.96 \cdot SE]$$

[Figura: Tre istogrammi per n=5, n=50, n=1000 con regione di confidenza evidenziata in rosso]

## Comparison of the methods

- An important issue in choosing a test method is the fundamental difference between methods that use explicit references (e.g., DSIS), and methods that do not use any explicit reference (e.g., ACR, ACR-HR and PC). This second class of method does not test fidelity.
- The **DSIS** method should be used when testing the fidelity of transmission with respect to the source signal
  - DSIS has long been a key method specified in [ITU-R BT.500-14], for the assessment of TV pictures whose typical quality represents the extreme high levels of videotelephony and videoconferencing.
  - The specific comments of the DSIS scale (imperceptible or perceptible) are valuable when the viewer's detection of impairment is an important factor.
- **ACR** is easy and fast to implement, and the presentation of the stimuli is similar to that of the common use of the systems. Thus, ACR is well suited for qualification tests.

---

## Comparison of the methods

- **ACR-HR** has all the advantages of ACR with respect to presentation and speed.
  - The principal merit of ACR-HR over ACR is that the perceptual impact of the reference video can be removed from the subjective scores. This **reduces the impact of scene bias** (e.g., viewers liking or disliking a reference video), **reference video quality** (e.g., small differences in camera quality) and **monitor** (e.g., professional quality versus consumer grade) upon the final scores.
  - ACR-HR is well suited to large experiments, provided that all reference videos are at least "good" quality. However, ACR-HR may be insensitive to some impairments that are easily detected by direct differential methods (e.g., DSIS).
- The principal merit of the **PC** method is its **high discriminatory power**, which is of particular value when several of the test items are nearly equal in quality.
  - When a large number of items are to be evaluated in the same test, the procedure based on the PC method tends to be lengthy. In such a case, an ACR or DSIS test may be carried out first with a limited number of observers, followed by a PC test solely on those items that have received about the same rating.

## Notation

We start with *full-reference methods*, i.e., quality metrics that depend on both the reference (original) and test (compressed) image

- $I(n, m, c, t)$ is the intensity of component $c$ in position $n, m$ at time $t$ of the reference signal
- The test signal is $\hat{I}(n, m, c, t)$
- The color space is typically YCbCr, but in some cases RGB can be used

---

## MSE and PSNR

- The first image quality metric is the *mean square error* of the luminance component, $\text{MSE}_Y(t)$
- For a single image (image at time $t$) of a video sequence, we define:

$$\text{MSE}_Y(t) = \frac{1}{NM} \sum_{n,m} \left(I(n,m,1,t) - \hat{I}(n,m,1,t)\right)^2$$

- The logarithmic version of the MSE is the PSNR:

$$\text{PSNR}_Y(t) = 10\log_{10} \frac{255^2}{\text{MSE}_Y(t)} \approx 48dB - 10\log_{10} \text{MSE}_Y(t)$$

- PSNR>45dB: Excellent quality; between 40 and 45: very good; between 30-40: from good to very good; below 30: typically poor

---

## YCbCr-PSNR

- Similarly, one can define $\text{MSE}_{Cb}(t)$, $\text{MSE}_{Cr}(t)$, $\text{PSNR}_{Cb}(t)$, $\text{PSNR}_{Cr}(t)$
- For example,

$$\text{MSE}_{Cb}(t) = \frac{1}{\frac{N}{2}\frac{M}{2}} \sum_{n,m} \left(I(n,m,2,t) - \hat{I}(n,m,2,t)\right)^2$$

A global quality measure for color images is:

$$\text{PSNR}_{YCbCr}(t) = \frac{3}{4}\text{PSNR}_Y(t) + \frac{1}{8}\text{PSNR}_{Cb}(t) + \frac{1}{8}\text{PSNR}_{Cr}(t)$$

The smaller weights reflect the lesser impact of colors on human perception

---

## Video PSNR

- The PSNR of a video sequence is the average of individual images' PSNR. For a sequence of $T$ images

$$\text{PSNR}_Y = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \text{PSNR}_Y(t)$$

Similarly for $\text{PSNR}_{Cb}$, $\text{PSNR}_{Cr}$, $\text{PSNR}_{YCbCr}$

[Figura: Grafico PSNR per frame della sequenza "foreman" codificata con H.264 — per-frame PSNR_Y, distinguendo frame I, P, B. Average PSNR_Y ≈ 39.0 dB]

## Bjontegaard metrics

- Video codec produce rate/distortion curves
- A single-measure comparison can be achieved using the Bjontegaard metrics:

1. **Bjontegaard Delta Rate**: it is the average rate difference at the same quality, expressed as %
   - It is computed as the red area divided by the amplitude of the common PSNR interval

2. **Bjontegaard Delta PSNR**: it is the average PSNR difference at the same rate, expressed in dB
   - It is computed as the blue area divided by the amplitude of the common rate interval

[Figura: Curva rate-distortion per M-JP2K, MPEG-2, H.264 — PSNR (dB) vs rate (kbps), con area rossa (BD-Rate) e area blu (BD-PSNR) evidenziate]

---

## Bjontegaard metrics (computation)

- In practice, the BD metrics are computed as follows:
- For each curve, a small (4 or 5) number of points is considered
- This points are fitted into a parametric representation of the curve (3$^{rd}$ degree polynomial in the variable $t = \log R$
- The area is computed using the parametric representation of the two curves

[Figura: Curva rate-distortion con area rossa tra H.264 e MPEG-2]

## Why PSNR Is Not Enough

- PSNR measures pixel fidelity, not perceptual quality

**Important limitations**
- All pixels are treated equally
- Human visual perception is ignored
- Different artefacts may produce similar PSNR
- Temporal perception is poorly modeled

Similar PSNR does not imply similar perceived quality!
Example: blur vs low frame rate in our test

## SSIM – Structural Similarity Index

- In 2004 a new approach for image quality assessment has been presented, the Structural Similarity Index or SSIM.
- It is a Full reference metric, whose value lies between [0,1] (bad to good)
- The SSIM is designed to improve on traditional metrics like PSNR and MSE, which have proved to be inconsistent with human perception.
- It is based on human visual system.

## Structural Similarity (SSIM)

- **Main content**
- **Key idea**
- The human visual system is highly sensitive to:
  - structures
  - edges
  - local contrast

- **SSIM compares**
  - luminance
  - contrast
  - structural information
  - instead of pure pixel error.

| SSIM | Quality |
|---|---|
| 1.0 | identical |
| >0.95 | very high |
| 0.8–0.9 | noticeable degradation |

---

## SSIM – Structural Similarity Index

$$SSIM(x, y) = [l(x,y)]^\alpha [c(x,y)]^\beta [s(x,y)]^\gamma$$

- $l(x,y)$ accounts for luminance change. It is qualitatively consistent with Weber's law, which has been widely used to model light adaptation in the HVS
- $c(x,y)$ accounts for contrast change. It is consistent with the contrast-masking feature of the HVS
- $s(x,y)$ is the structure comparison function
- where $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are parameters used to adjust the relative importance of the three components

- Proposed in June 2016 by Netflix
- Full reference video metric
- **Combines multiple features**
  - detail preservation
  - structural similarity
  - motion information
  - perceptual learning

- VMAF values spans from quality 0–100, where 0 is worst, and 100 is best
  - VMAF 93 is a good value to target, but everything with an average over 90 will look good.

## Reduced Reference (RR) Metrics

**Key idea**
- Only partial information about the reference signal is available.

**Typical transmitted features**
- edge statistics
- frequency coefficients
- texture descriptors
- motion information

**Advantages**
- lower bandwidth requirements
- suitable for network monitoring
- practical for large-scale systems

**Limitations**
- less accurate than FR metrics
- requires side information transmission

**Typical applications**
- IPTV monitoring, adaptive streaming, network QoE estimation

---

## No Reference metrics

[Figura: Schema a blocchi — Input/Reference signal → Signal processing → Output/Processed signal → NR METRIC]

**Key idea**
- Quality is estimated without access to the original signal.

**Why NR metrics are important.** In many real systems:
- the reference video is unavailable
- only the received content can be analyzed

---

## No Reference metrics

**Typical scenarios**
- social media platforms
- smartphone cameras
- user-generated content
- video surveillance
- streaming analytic

**Main challenge - Distinguishing:**
- original scene characteristics from
- distortion artefacts

**Bottom takeaway**
- NR quality assessment is one of the most challenging problems in multimedia systems
