## The Human Visual System

### The Eye

Light is converted into neural impulses by two types of retinal receptors:

- **Cones** (6–7 million, center of retina): sensitive to color, good resolution, require high illumination.
- **Rods** (75–150 million): sensitive to light intensity, low resolution, very effective in low-light conditions.
![[Pasted image 20260227104651.png]]

### Light Perception

Perceived intensity follows a **logarithmic function** of physical intensity. The total dynamic range of the human visual system (HVS) spans approximately 10¹⁰ (100 dB), but it cannot operate across the full range simultaneously. Importantly, perceived brightness is not solely a function of physical light intensity — context and surrounding illumination also play a role.

### Contrast Sensitivity Function (CSF)

The CSF measures the HVS's ability to detect luminance differences across spatial frequencies. Key concepts:

- **Contrast**: difference in luminance between lightest and darkest parts of a pattern (expressed as a percentage).
- **Spatial frequency**: measured in cycles per degree of visual angle; low frequencies = large patterns, high frequencies = fine details.
- **Sensitivity**: reciprocal of the minimum detectable contrast.

The CSF is determined through psychophysical experiments with sine-wave gratings. It **peaks at mid-range frequencies (2–5 cycles/degree)** and declines at both extremes.
![[Pasted image 20260227104714.png]]
### Color Perception

Visible light spans 400–700 nm. Cones are spectrally tuned: 65% to red, 33% to green, 2% to blue (though blue cones are very sensitive). Color sensation arises from the **tristimulus response**, combining three primary colors.
![[Pasted image 20260227104731.png]]

### Color Spaces
![[Pasted image 20260227104825.png]]
**RGB** is represented as a unit cube: origin = black, opposite corner = white, main diagonal = grayscale. Primary colors sit at the cube's corners.

**HSV** is derived from RGB by balancing the cube on its black corner and rotating so the black-white diagonal is vertical:

- **Hue**: angle (0°–360°) around the vertical axis → color family.
- **Saturation**: distance from central axis → color purity.
- **Value**: vertical position → brightness.

**YCbCr (≈ YUV)** separates luminance from chrominance:

- **Y**: luminance (same as HSV Value).
- **Cb, Cr**: chrominance in Cartesian coordinates (equivalent to HSV Saturation and Hue).

The conversion from RGB to YCbCr follows the ITU-R BT.601 linear mapping:

```
[Y, Cb, Cr]ᵀ = M · [R, G, B]ᵀ + [0, 128, 128]ᵀ
```

Weights in Y reflect HVS sensitivity (maximum for green, minimum for blue). The transformation is invertible, so RGB can be perfectly reconstructed.

### Beyond Human Perception: Machine-Centric Multimedia

A growing share of multimedia traffic is consumed by **algorithms** (surveillance, IoT, industrial monitoring) rather than humans. Key differences:

- **HVS**: prioritizes semantic regions (e.g., faces), constrained by the CSF.
- **Machine models**: rely on high-frequency details and local textures critical for feature extraction.

This motivates **task-oriented communications**: instead of minimizing perceived distortion, the modern goal is to maximize task accuracy (e.g., object recognition) at minimum bitrate — enabling extreme compression ratios.

## The Sound Perception

### Pure Tone Perception

A pure tone $x(t) = a\sin(2\pi f_1 t)$ excites multiple auditory nerve fibers (power spreading). The auditory system is modeled as a **filterbank with M filters**, each corresponding to a nervous fiber. The ratio of output power to input power defines the **spreading function** $S_E(k) = A_k^2(f_1)$.

### Hearing Threshold & Critical Bands
![[Pasted image 20260227104904.png]]

The **hearing threshold** $S_a(f)$ is the minimum power required for a tone at frequency $f$ to be audible. It reaches its minimum between 1 and 4 kHz (the speech range).

A **critical band (CB)** defines a frequency neighborhood around $f_1$: for N sinusoids within the CB, only their **total power** needs to exceed $S_a(f_1)$ to be heard. The CB width increases with frequency.

### Masking Curves
![[Pasted image 20260227104933.png]]

**Frequency masking**: a loud tone at $f_0$ raises the audibility threshold for nearby frequencies. The masking function $S_m(f_0, \sigma^2, f)$ has a triangular shape peaking at $f = f_0$. A masked sound does not need to be louder than the masker to be inaudible. The slope is proportional to CB width and decreases for higher masker power.
![[Pasted image 20260227105041.png|500]]

**Time masking**: a loud sound masks quieter sounds both before (**pre-masking**: 2–5 ms) and after (**post-masking**: 100–200 ms) it occurs.
![[Pasted image 20260227105119.png|500]]

### Model Application

The psychoacoustical model identifies inaudible signal components, allowing **quantization noise** to be introduced where it will be masked. However, the model has limitations: it only handles pure tones or narrowband sounds and can assess at most ~3 simultaneous sounds. In practice, compression algorithm parameters are determined empirically through extensive listening tests.

## Image and Video Representation

### Gray-Level Images

A digital image is a discrete grid of **N × M pixels**. Each pixel at position $(m, n)$ maps to a scalar intensity value $f_{n,m} = f_k$, where $k = (n-1)M + m$ is the raster-scan index.
![[Pasted image 20260227105200.png]]
### RGB and YUV Representation

In **RGB**, color images consist of three grayscale planes (R, G, B), each at full resolution.

In **YUV (YCbCr)**, the image is decomposed into one luminance (Y) and two chrominance (Cb, Cr) components, exploiting the HVS's lower sensitivity to color detail.

### Color Subsampling
Chroma subsampling is described by the notation **J:a:b**:

- **J**: reference horizontal size (usually 4).
- **a**: number of chroma samples on the first row.
- **b**: number of additional chroma samples on the second row.
![[Pasted image 20260227105242.png]]
Common schemes:

|Format|Chroma resolution|Data saving|
|---|---|---|
|4:4:4|Full H & V|None|
|4:2:2|½ H, full V|33%|
|4:2:0|½ H, ½ V|50%|
|4:1:1|¼ H, full V|50%|

**YCbCr 4:2:0** is the most widely used format in digital video compression (JPEG, H.264, H.265). It keeps Y at full resolution and subsamples Cb, Cr by 2× both horizontally and vertically, achieving a **compression ratio of 0.5** (6/12) with high perceptual quality.

### Quantization

Pixel values are represented on a discrete set via **uniform quantization** (rounding). With $b$ bits per component: $L = 2^b$ levels. Typical values:

- 8 bpp → 256 gray levels.
- 24 bpp → ~16M colors.
- HDR: 32–64 bits per channel.

### Digital Video Representation

A video signal is a function $I(n, m, T, c) \to x \in {0, 1, \ldots, 2^b - 1}$, where $n, m$ are spatial coordinates, $T$ is time, and $c$ is the color channel. It combines spatial sampling, temporal sampling, color representation, and chroma subsampling.

## Compression
Why is possible to compress?
- **Statistical Rendundancy:** can be *spatial*, meaning that images are often locally homogeneous, and *temporal*, meaning that successive images are higly recommended;
- **Psycovisual rendundancy:** limits of the HSV (CSF, Masking, Contours). Goal is *visually lossless* coding;
- **NEW: Semantic rendundancy:** concepts and objects: images contain recognizable structures. The goal is "generative coding" (transmitting token/meaning instead of pixels);

## Rate
Compression ratio: $T = \dfrac{B_{in}}{B_{out}} = \dfrac{R_{in}}{R_{out}}$
Coding rate:
- Image: $R = \dfrac{B_{out}}{NM} \space [bpp]$
- Video, audio: $R = \dfrac{B_{out}}{T} \space [bps]$
We need to measure the size of the file (easy) and also the quality of the output (hard part).
How can we measure the quality of the output? An image is simply a matrix of pixel, so we can do the difference between the matrix of the original image and the matrix of the output image, and we get the **error matrix**.

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
100 & 99 & 98 \\
101 & 90 & 90
\end{bmatrix}
-
\begin{bmatrix}
99 & 100 & 98 \\
101 & 94 & 94
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & -1 & 0 \\
0 & -4 & -4
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Then we apply the **MSE**:
$$\mathcal{D}(f, \tilde{f}) = \frac{1}{NM} \|\mathcal{E}\|^2 = \frac{1}{NM} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \epsilon_{n,m}^2$$
where:
- $\epsilon(f, \tilde{f}) = f - \tilde{f}$ is **Error image**;

The **Peak signal-to-Noise ratio**: 
$$PSNR = 10 \cdot \log_{10} \left( \frac{255^2}{MSE} \right)$$
If we vectorize the matrices and compute the distance, we will see that MSE is just proportional to the distance calculated.

Then we have also **wighted PSNR**, where given a frequency weighting funciton (filter) *h*:
$$
WPSNR(f, \tilde{f}) = 10 \log_{10} \left( \frac{255^2}{\mathcal{D}_W(f, \tilde{f})} \right)
$$
which gives more emphasis to the lower frequency.

## Criteria for Image Quality Evaluation

### Perceptual Objective Criteria — SSIM

The **Structural Similarity Index (SSIM)** measures perceived quality between two image blocks $x$ and $y$ by combining three components:

$$\text{SSIM}(x, y) = [l(x,y)]^\alpha \cdot [c(x,y)]^\beta \cdot [s(x,y)]^\gamma$$

|Component|Formula|Description|
|---|---|---|
|Luminance|$l(x,y) = \dfrac{2\mu_x\mu_y + C_1}{\mu_x^2 + \mu_y^2 + C_1}$|Compares mean intensities|
|Contrast|$c(x,y) = \dfrac{2\sigma_x\sigma_y + C_2}{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + C_2}$|Compares standard deviations|
|Structure|$s(x,y) = \dfrac{\sigma_{xy} + C_3}{\sigma_x\sigma_y + C_3}$|Compares normalized correlation|

For simplicity, setting $\alpha = \beta = \gamma = 1$ and $C_3 = C_2/2$, the formula reduces to:

$$\text{SSIM} = \frac{(2\mu_x\mu_y + C_1)(2\sigma_{xy} + C_2)}{(\mu_x^2 + \mu_y^2 + C_1)(\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + C_2)}$$

The **SSIM between two images** is computed as the average of block-level SSIM values.

### Subjective Criteria (SC)

Subjective criteria are based on image quality assessments performed by **human observers**. They are considered the ground truth but are difficult to use in practice because:

- HVS modelling for objective criteria is complex.
- Results require statistical analysis.
- Evaluations are long, difficult, and costly.

For this reason, **non-perceptual objective criteria (NP-OC)** are often preferred in practice, due to their:

- Simplicity.
- Geometrical interpretation (norm-based).
- Analytical optimizability.
- (Partial) correlation with subjective criteria.
