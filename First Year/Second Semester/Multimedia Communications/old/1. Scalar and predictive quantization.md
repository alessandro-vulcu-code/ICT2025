## Definitions

### Scalar Quantization (SQ): a Staircase Function

**Scalar quantization** is, almost always, a *staircase function*. The function $Q(x)$ is piecewise constant: given a sample $x$, it is mapped to the level $\hat{x}^i$ corresponding to the cell $\Theta^i$ it falls into.

> [!definition] Definition: Scalar Quantization (SQ)
> A **scalar quantizer** is a function $Q$ from $\mathbb{R}$ to a discrete set called the *Dictionary* $\mathcal{C}$:
> $$Q : x \in \mathbb{R} \;\rightarrow\; y \in \mathcal{C} = \{\hat{x}^1, \hat{x}^2, \ldots, \hat{x}^L\} \subset \mathbb{R}$$
>
> - $\mathcal{C}$ – **Dictionary**: discrete subset of $\mathbb{R}$
> - $\hat{x}^i$ – **quantization level** or **code-word**
> - $e = x - Q(x)$ – **quantization noise** (quantization error)
> - $\Theta^i = \{x : Q(x) = \hat{x}^i\}$ – **decision region** or **cell**

**Regions and levels completely define the SQ.** Regions are typically defined as intervals:
$$\Theta^i = (t^i,\; t^{i+1})$$

Therefore, **thresholds** $t^i$ and **levels** $\hat{x}^i$ are an equivalent and complete description of the quantizer.

### Quantization as Coding / Decoding

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization.pdf#page=21| Encoder–decoder scheme of a scalar quantizer]]

The quantization process decomposes into two operations:

```
x(n) ──→ [ E ] ──i(n)──→ [ D ] ──→ x̂(n)
            ↑                  ↑
            Θⁱ                 C
```

- **Encoder (E)**: maps the sample $x(n)$ to the index $i(n) \in \mathbb{Z}$ of the quantization region.
- **Decoder (D)**: maps the index $i(n)$ to the corresponding level $\hat{x}^{i(n)}$.
- The index $i(n)$ is represented as a bit string via **lossless coding**.

> [!tip] Quantization vs Inverse Quantization
> In compression systems:
> - **Quantization**: $x \rightarrow i$ — performed at the **encoder**
> - **Inverse Quantization**: $i \rightarrow \hat{x}^i$ — performed at the **decoder**

---

### Rate of a Scalar Quantizer

> [!important] Definition: Rate $R$
> The **rate** $R$ is the **average number of bits** needed to represent one sample, i.e., the binarization of $i(n)$. It is **always obtained via lossless coding**.

In practice it is approximated as:
$$R = \log_2 L$$

This is a good approximation under two implicit assumptions:
1. Quantized data have a **uniform probability distribution** (pessimistic assumption).
2. The binary representation of $i(n)$ is optimal — an **entropy coder** is used.

---

### Distortion of a Scalar Quantizer

The **per-sample distortion** is the squared error:
$$d[x(n),\, Q[x(n)]] = |e(n)|^2 = |x(n) - Q[x(n)]|^2$$

For a **deterministic signal** $x(\cdot)$ of length $N$, the **Mean Square Error (MSE)** is:
$$D = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} d[x(n),\, Q[x(n)]]$$

For a **random signal**:
$$D = \mathbb{E}\!\left\{|X(n) - Q(X(n))|^2\right\} = \mathbb{E}\!\left\{|E(n)|^2\right\} = \sigma^2_Q$$

Distortion is therefore the **variance of the quantization noise process** $E(n) = X(n) - Q(X(n))$.

### Mathematical Reminders: Integer Mapping Functions

These standard functions are staircase functions widely used to implement quantizers.

| Function | Operator | Behavior | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **floor** | $\lfloor x \rfloor$ | Greatest integer $\leq x$, rounds toward $-\infty$ | $\lfloor 2.7 \rfloor = 2$, $\lfloor -2.7 \rfloor = -3$ |
| **ceil** | $\lceil x \rceil$ | Smallest integer $\geq x$, rounds toward $+\infty$ | $\lceil 2.1 \rceil = 3$, $\lceil -2.1 \rceil = -2$ |
| **fix** | $\text{fix}(x)$ | Drops fractional part, rounds toward $0$ (symmetric) | $\text{fix}(2.7) = 2$, $\text{fix}(-2.7) = -2$ |
| **round** | $\text{round}(x)$ | Nearest integer; $= \lfloor x + 0.5 \rfloor$ | round$(2.51) = 3$, round$(2.49) = 2$, round$(-2.3) = -2$ |

- `fix` is the standard integer cast in most programming languages.
- `round` is the basis of **uniform quantization** for signed data.

## Uniform Quantization

**Uniform Quantization (UQ)** is characterized by:
- Input range: $(0, A)$ for *unsigned* data, or $(-A/2, A/2)$ for *signed* data.
- Number of levels: $L$

The input range is divided into $L$ **equal-sized cells** of width:
$$\Delta = \frac{A}{L}$$

Each cell is represented by its **midpoint** (level = center of the cell):

$$\forall i,\quad \Delta^i = \Delta = \frac{A}{L}$$
$$t^i = t^{i-1} + \Delta \qquad \text{(thresholds)}$$
$$\hat{x}^i = \frac{t^i + t^{i-1}}{2} \qquad \text{(levels)}$$
$$\Theta^i = \left(\hat{x}^i - \frac{\Delta}{2},\;\; \hat{x}^i + \frac{\Delta}{2}\right)$$

---

### UQ for Unsigned Data

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization.pdf#page=31| UQ for unsigned data with L=8]]

For **unsigned data** with range $(0, A)$, thresholds are $0, \Delta, 2\Delta, \ldots, L\Delta$, i.e. $t^i = (i-1)\Delta$:

$$i = \left\lceil \frac{x}{\Delta} \right\rceil \qquad \text{encoder}$$
$$\hat{x}^i = i \cdot \Delta - \frac{\Delta}{2} \qquad \text{decoder}$$
$$Q(x) = \Delta \cdot \left\lceil \frac{x}{\Delta} \right\rceil - \frac{\Delta}{2}$$

---

### UQ for Signed Data: Mid-tread vs Mid-rise

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization.pdf#page=32| UQ for signed data: mid-tread]]

For **signed data** with range $(-A/2, A/2)$ one wants **zero to be a quantization level**.

> [!important] Mid-tread Quantizer (preferred)
> Zero is a **level**. Small fluctuations around zero are mapped to zero (effectively a "dead zone"), which is ideal for signals with noise near zero.
>
> We **always use an odd number of levels** for UQ of signed data.
>
> UQ for signed data amounts to **rounding**:
> $$i = \text{round}\!\left(\frac{x}{\Delta}\right) \qquad \text{encoder}$$
> $$\hat{x}^i = \Delta \cdot i \qquad \text{decoder}$$
> $$Q(x) = \Delta \cdot \text{round}\!\left(\frac{x}{\Delta}\right)$$
>
> $Q(x)$ is the **closest multiple of $\Delta$** to $x$.

> [!warning] Mid-rise Quantizer (problematic for noisy signals)
> Zero is a **threshold** (even number of levels). Any small value gets quantized to $\pm\Delta/2$ according to its sign. If the signal has small fluctuations due to additive noise, the quantizer **amplifies the noise** instead of suppressing it.

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization.pdf#page=34| Mid-tread vs mid-rise comparison on a noisy signal]]

> This comparison is conceptually very important: a "noisy" signal (small random oscillations around zero) is handled in the opposite way by the two quantizers. In the **mid-tread** case, all small fluctuations collapse to zero — the noise is *suppressed*. In the **mid-rise** case, fluctuations are amplified: the output alternates between $+\Delta/2$ and $-\Delta/2$, turning a near-zero signal into an oscillating signal of amplitude $\Delta$ — the noise is *enhanced*.

---

### Deadzone Quantization (DZQ)

**Deadzone Quantization** extends the mid-tread idea: the **central cell is larger than the others**, creating a wider "dead zone" around zero. This removes even more small-amplitude oscillations while keeping fine resolution on non-zero values. It is **widely used in data compression**.

Using a threshold parameter $\tau$:

$$i = \begin{cases} \text{sign}(x) \cdot \left\lceil \dfrac{|x| + \tau\Delta}{\Delta} \right\rceil & \text{if } |x| \geq \tau \\ 0 & \text{if } |x| < \tau \end{cases} \qquad \text{encoder}$$

$$\hat{x} = \begin{cases} \text{sign}(i) \cdot \Delta\!\left(|i| + \dfrac{1-\tau}{2}\right) & \text{if } i \neq 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } i = 0 \end{cases} \qquad \text{decoder}$$

---

### UQ Wrap-up

> [!summary] Summary
> **Uniform Quantization** is intuitive: split the input range into $L$ equal intervals and represent each one by its midpoint.
>
> **Implementation:**
> $$Q(x) = \Delta \cdot \text{round}\!\left(\frac{x}{\Delta}\right) \qquad \text{(mid-tread)}$$
> $$Q(x) = \Delta \cdot \left\lfloor \frac{x}{\Delta} \right\rfloor + \frac{\Delta}{2} \qquad \text{(mid-rise)}$$
>
> For a given range $A$ and number of levels $L$, UQ achieves the **optimal minimax quantizer**:
> - The maximum error is $\Delta/2$ (half the cell size).
> - Any modification to UQ can only increase the maximum error.
>
> UQ also allows an **analytical formula** for the Rate-Distortion curve.

## Examples

### Example 1: 1D Signal Quantization

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization.pdf#page=10| Example 1: quantization of a 1D signal]]

A 1D signal $x(n)$ is quantized with a uniform quantizer:
- **Input interval**: $(-A, A) = (-1.25,\; 1.25)$
- **Thresholds**: $-1.25,\; -0.75,\; -0.25,\; 0.25,\; 0.75,\; 1.25$
- **Levels** ($L = 5$): $-1,\; -0.5,\; 0,\; 0.5,\; 1$
- **Cell size**: $\Delta = \dfrac{2A}{L} = \dfrac{2.5}{5} = 0.5$

Each level is exactly at the **center of the corresponding cell** — the defining property of UQ. The quantization error $e(n) = x(n) - Q(x(n))$ is bounded within $[-\Delta/2,\; +\Delta/2] = [-0.25,\; +0.25]$.

In **Example 2** (same signal, $\Delta = 0.2$), more levels produce a smaller error and a better approximation of the original signal.

## Rate-Distortion Curve

### Probability Theory Refreshers

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization.pdf#page=3| Probability Theory Refreshers: uniform RV and LOTUS]]

Two results needed to derive the RD curve analytically.

#### 1. Uniform Random Variable

A continuous RV $X$ is **uniformly distributed** on $[a, b]$ if its PDF is:
$$X \sim \mathcal{U}(a, b) \;\Leftrightarrow\; f_X(x) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{b-a} & a \leq x \leq b \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Its variance is:
$$\text{Var}(X) = \frac{(b-a)^2}{12}$$

#### 2. Law of the Unconscious Statistician (LOTUS)

Let $X$ be a continuous RV with PDF $f_X(x)$. The expected value of a real-valued function $g(X)$ is:
$$E[g(X)] = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} g(x)\, f_X(x)\, dx$$

This allows computing the mean of $g(X)$ without first finding the PDF of $g(X)$ itself.

---

### Uniform Quantization: RD Curve Derivation

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization.pdf#page=5| UQ: RD curve derivation]]

**Hypotheses**: $X \sim \mathcal{U}\!\left(-\frac{A}{2}, \frac{A}{2}\right)$, $Q(x)$ is a uniform quantizer with $L$ levels.

**Goal**: Find $\sigma^2_Q = E\!\left[(X - \hat{X})^2\right]$ as a function of $A$ and $L$.

Using LOTUS with $g(u) = (u - Q(u))^2$:

$$\sigma^2_Q = E\!\left[(X-\hat{X})^2\right] = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} p_X(u)\,[u - Q(u)]^2\, du$$

Since $p_X(u) = 1/A$ on each cell and $Q(u) = \hat{x}^i$ for $u \in \Theta^i$:

$$= \sum_{i=1}^{L} \int_{\Theta^i} \frac{1}{A}[u - \hat{x}^i]^2\, du = \frac{1}{A} \sum_{i=1}^{L} \int_{\hat{x}^i - \Delta/2}^{\hat{x}^i + \Delta/2} [u - \hat{x}^i]^2\, du$$

Substituting $t = u - \hat{x}^i$:

$$= \frac{1}{A} \sum_{i=1}^{L} \int_{-\Delta/2}^{\Delta/2} t^2\, dt = \frac{1}{A} \cdot L \cdot \frac{\Delta^3}{12} = \frac{\Delta^2}{12} = \frac{1}{12} \frac{A^2}{L^2}$$

> [!important] RD Curve for UQ of a Uniform RV
> $$D = \frac{\Delta^2}{12} = \frac{A^2}{12L^2} = \frac{A^2/12}{2^{2R}} = \sigma^2_X\, 2^{-2R}$$
> (using $\sigma^2_X = A^2/12$ for $X \sim \mathcal{U}(-A/2, A/2)$ and $L = 2^R$)
>
> The **SNR** in dB:
> $$\text{SNR} = 10\log_{10} \frac{E\{X^2\}}{D} = 10\log_{10} 2^{2R} \approx 6R \;\text{[dB]}$$
>
> **Each additional bit of rate adds approximately 6 dB of SNR.**

---

### High-Resolution (HR) Uniform Quantization

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_2.pdf#page=9| High-Resolution UQ: generic RV (slides 35–36/78)]]

What if $X$ is a **generic** RV (not necessarily uniform)?

**Hypothesis**: $L \to +\infty$ (high resolution).

In HR, within any cell $\Theta^i$, $p_X$ can be **approximated as constant** (the value changes across cells, but is flat within each one). Therefore:
- The quantization noise in each $\Theta^i$ is $\mathcal{U}(-\Delta/2, \Delta/2)$
- By the total probability law: $E \sim \mathcal{U}(-\Delta/2, \Delta/2)$

The distortion result still holds:
$$D = \frac{\Delta^2}{12} = \frac{A^2}{12}\, 2^{-2R}$$

The SNR generalizes to:
$$\text{SNR} \approx 6R - 10\log_{10} \frac{\gamma^2}{3}$$

where $\gamma^2 = \dfrac{X^2_{\max}}{\sigma^2_X} = \dfrac{A^2/4}{\sigma^2_X}$ is the **load factor**: ratio between *peak power* and *average power*.

The distortion can be rewritten as:
$$D = \frac{A^2}{3}\, 2^{-2R} = \frac{\gamma^2}{3}\,\sigma^2_X\, 2^{-2R} = K_X\,\sigma^2_X\, 2^{-2R}$$

> The load factor $\gamma^2$ captures the mismatch between the quantizer range and the actual signal variance. A high $\gamma^2$ means the quantizer range is much wider than the signal's typical amplitude — many cells are rarely used, which is inefficient.

## Optimal Scalar Quantization

### Problem Statement

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_2.pdf#page=12| Optimal quantization: problem statement (slide 38/78)]]

For a given PDF $p_X(x)$, find the quantizer that **minimizes distortion for a given rate** $R$. This is equivalent to determining optimal thresholds $t^i$ and levels $\hat{x}^i$.

Two approaches:
- **Analytical solution in high resolution**: $D = c_X\, \sigma^2_X\, 2^{-2R}$
- **Low resolution** (HR hypothesis not met): Lloyd-Max algorithm (local optimum)

---

### Optimal Quantization in High Resolution

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_2.pdf#page=13| Optimal quantization in HR: RD curve (slide 39/78)]]

Under the **high-resolution hypothesis** ($L \to \infty$, $\max_i \Delta^i \to 0$, $p_X(u) \approx P_i$ constant within each $\Theta^i$), it can be shown that the optimal quantizer achieves:

> [!important] Optimal RD Curve (High Resolution)
> $$\sigma^2_Q = c_X\, \sigma^2_X\, 2^{-2R}$$
> where
> $$c_X = \frac{1}{12}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}} p_U^{1/3}(t)\, dt\right]^3, \qquad U = \frac{X}{\sigma_X}$$

---

### Shape Factor

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_2.pdf#page=14| Shape factor: definition and values (slide 40/78)]]

In the RD curve $\sigma^2_Q = c_X\, \sigma^2_X\, 2^{-2R}$, the term $c_X$ is called the **shape factor**: it depends only on the **shape** of the PDF, not on its variance. (The shape of $X$ is the same as that of the normalized variable $U = X/\sigma_X$.)

| PDF | Shape factor $c_X$ |
|:---:|:---:|
| Uniform | $1$ |
| Gaussian | $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\pi \approx 2.72$ |

> The shape factor quantifies how much harder it is to quantize a given distribution compared to the uniform case. A Gaussian signal requires ~2.72× more distortion power than a uniform signal at the same rate and variance, because its PDF has heavy tails that are costly to represent with a finite number of levels.

---

### Non-HR Optimal Quantization: Lloyd-Max Algorithm

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_2.pdf#page=15| Non-HR optimal quantization: motivation (slide 41/78)]]

The HR hypothesis is **not always met** — in practice, quantization often operates at low or very low rate. In this regime:
- There is **no analytical solution**.
- It is possible to derive **necessary conditions** for optimality, independent of resolution.
- These conditions define an iterative algorithm converging to a **local optimum**: the **Lloyd-Max Algorithm (LMA)**.

#### Algorithm Structure

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_2.pdf#page=16| Lloyd-Max algorithm: overview (slide 42/78)]]

1. Choose an initial dictionary $\mathcal{C}^{(0)} = \{\hat{x}^i_0\}_{i=1,\ldots,L}$
2. Find the **best thresholds** for the given dictionary
3. Find the **best dictionary** for the given thresholds
4. Iterate steps 2–3 until a stop condition is met

#### Step 0: Initialization

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_2.pdf#page=17| Lloyd-Max: initial dictionary (slide 43/78)]]

- **Uniform initialization**: $\hat{x}^n = -A_0 + n\Delta$, $\forall n = 1, \ldots, L$
- **Random initialization**: code-words drawn from the same distribution as the data

#### Step 2: Nearest Neighbor Rule (best thresholds)

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_2.pdf#page=18| Lloyd-Max: nearest neighbor rule (slide 44/78)]]

Given $\{\hat{x}^i\}_{i=1,\ldots,L}$, assign each input to the **nearest code-word**:
$$k = \arg\min_n\, |x - \hat{x}^n| \;\Rightarrow\; Q(x) = \hat{x}^k$$

This is equivalent to placing thresholds at the **midpoint between consecutive levels**:
$$t^i = \frac{\hat{x}^i + \hat{x}^{i+1}}{2}, \qquad i \in \{1, \ldots, L-1\}$$

#### Step 3: Centroid Condition (best dictionary)

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_2.pdf#page=19| Lloyd-Max: centroid condition (slide 45/78)]]

Given thresholds $\{t^0, \ldots, t^L\}$, minimize distortion over levels:

$$\sigma^2_Q = \sum_{i=1}^{L} \int_{t^{i-1}}^{t^i} (u - \hat{x}^i)^2\, p_X(u)\, du$$

Setting $\partial\sigma^2_Q / \partial\hat{x}^i = 0$:

$$\hat{x}^i = \frac{\int_{t^{i-1}}^{t^i} u\, p_X(u)\, du}{\int_{t^{i-1}}^{t^i} p_X(u)\, du} = \frac{\int_{\Theta^i} u\, p_X(u)\, du}{\int_{\Theta^i} p_X(u)\, du} = E\!\left[X \mid X \in \Theta^i\right]$$

> [!important] Centroid Condition
> The optimal representative of a quantization region $\Theta^i$ is its **centroid**: the **conditional mean** of $X$ given $X \in \Theta^i$.

#### Stop Condition

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_2.pdf#page=20| Lloyd-Max: stop condition (slide 46/78)]]

After each iteration $k$, compute the new distortion $\sigma^2_{Q,(k)}$. The optimization steps guarantee $\sigma^2_{Q,(k)} \leq \sigma^2_{Q,(k-1)}$.

**Relative distortion reduction** criterion:
$$\frac{\sigma^2_{Q,(k-1)} - \sigma^2_{Q,(k)}}{\sigma^2_{Q,(k-1)}} < \varepsilon$$

Or stop after a fixed number of iterations: $k = K$.

#### Convergence

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_2.pdf#page=21| Lloyd-Max: convergence (slide 47/78)]]

> [!warning] Convergence of Lloyd-Max
> - Convergence to the **global optimum is not guaranteed**.
> - The final result depends on the **initialization**.
> - However, at each step **distortion cannot increase**.

---

### Lloyd-Max Algorithm for Data

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_2.pdf#page=22| Lloyd-Max for data (slide 48/78)]]

In practice, the signal PDF is unknown — only a dataset is available. The LMA adapts as follows.

Let $\mathcal{X} = \{u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_M\}$ be the data to quantize (or a training set):

1. Initialize ($k=0$): $\mathcal{C}^{(k)} = \{\hat{x}^i_0\}_{i=1,\ldots,L}$
2. **Nearest neighbor rule**: assign each data point to its closest code-word:
$$W^i_k = \left\{u_m \in \mathcal{X} : \forall j \neq i,\; \|u_m - \hat{x}^i_k\| \leq \|u_m - \hat{x}^j_k\|\right\}$$
3. **Centroid rule**: update each code-word to the mean of its cluster:
$$\hat{x}^i_{k+1} = \frac{1}{|W^i_k|} \sum_{u_m \in W^i_k} u_m$$
4. Iterate steps 2–3 until convergence

> This is equivalent to the **k-means clustering algorithm** applied to 1D data. Each quantization level is a cluster centroid and each cell is a Voronoi region.

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_2.pdf#page=23| Scalar quantization summary (slide 49/78)]]

> [!summary] RD Curve Summary

> | UQ, uniform RV | $D = \sigma^2_X\, 2^{-2R}$ |

## Predictive Scalar Quantization

### Motivation

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=2| Predictive quantization: principles (slide 51/78)]]

Scalar quantization alone has a fundamental limitation: it treats each sample **independently**, without exploiting the statistical structure of the signal. This works well only for **sparse data** (where most values are near zero), but most natural signals — images, audio, speech — are **non-sparse** and heavily correlated.

> [!important] Why Predictive Quantization?
> - Quantization alone is **not effective** for compression of non-sparse data
> - SQ uses a **too simple model**: it neglects dependencies among samples
> - **Idea**: exploit sample correlation by **prediction** — subtract a predicted value before quantizing
> - **Goal**: reduce the signal's variance by increasing sparsity of the residual

---

### Sparse Signals: A Preview

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=3| Sparse signals: definition and motivation (slide 52/78)]]

> [!definition] Sparse Signal
> A signal is **sparse** if most of its elements are zero or close to zero.

Key observations:
- A near-zero sample can be **"neglected"** (quantized as zero) without introducing large distortion.
- The variance of a sparse signal is **low** — which is desirable since $D = c_X \sigma^2_X 2^{-2R}$.
- Natural signals are **not sparse in their original form**, but can become sparse after appropriate processing.
- The two main tools for **signal sparsification** are **prediction** and **linear transforms**.

> This sets the stage for the entire predictive quantization framework: if we can transform the signal into a sparse representation, quantization becomes vastly more efficient.

---

### Coding Scheme (Open Loop)

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=4| Open-loop predictive coding scheme (slide 53/78)]]

The basic idea is to subtract a **prediction** $v(n)$ from $x(n)$ before quantizing:

```
x(n) ──→ [−] ──→ y(n) ──→ [Q] ──→ ŷ(n) ──→ [+] ──→ x̂(n)
           ↑                                    ↑
          v(n)                                 v(n)
```

- $v(n)$ is the **predictor**: an estimate of $x(n)$ computed from past samples
- $y(n) = x(n) - v(n)$ is the **prediction error** (residual)
- The quantizer $Q$ operates on $y(n)$, which has **lower variance** than $x(n)$
- At the decoder, the reconstructed value is $\hat{x}(n) = \hat{y}(n) + v(n)$

If the prediction is good, $v(n) \approx x(n)$, so $y(n) \approx 0$ — the residual is near-zero, i.e. **sparse**.

---

### Prediction Gain

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=5| Prediction gain derivation (slide 54/78)]]

Since the quantizer operates on $y(n) = x(n) - v(n)$, the quantization error and prediction error are related:

$$q(n) = y(n) - \hat{y}(n) = x(n) - v(n) - \hat{x}(n) + v(n) = \bar{q}(n)$$

The goal of Predictive Quantization (PQ) is therefore to **minimize the distortion of $y$**, i.e., to make $\sigma^2_Y$ as small as possible.

The total SNR of the system is:

$$\text{SNR}_P = 10\log_{10}\frac{\sigma^2_X}{D} = 10\log_{10}\frac{\sigma^2_X}{\sigma^2_Y} + 10\log_{10}\frac{\sigma^2_Y}{D} = G_P + G_Q$$

> [!important] Prediction Gain $G_P$
> $$G_P = 10\log_{10}\frac{\sigma^2_X}{\sigma^2_Y}$$
>
> **Prediction is effective if and only if the prediction error has a smaller variance than the original signal**, i.e., $\sigma^2_Y < \sigma^2_X$, which gives $G_P > 0$.

---

### Example: AR(1) Process

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=6| Example: AR(1) process and prediction gain (slide 55/78)]]

Let $X(n) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ with autocorrelation $E[X(n)X(m)] = \sigma^2 \rho^{|n-m|}$, and let the predictor be the previous sample: $V(n) = X(n-1)$.

Then $Y(n) = X(n) - X(n-1)$ is a zero-mean Gaussian RV with variance:

$$\sigma^2_Y = E\left[(X(n) - X(n-1))^2\right] = 2\sigma^2 - 2\sigma^2\rho = 2\sigma^2(1-\rho)$$

The prediction gain is:

$$G_P = 10\log_{10}\frac{\sigma^2_X}{\sigma^2_Y} = 10\log_{10}\frac{\sigma^2}{2(1-\rho)\sigma^2} = 10\log_{10}\frac{1}{2(1-\rho)}$$

> **$G_P > 0 \Leftrightarrow \rho > \frac{1}{2}$**: prediction only helps when consecutive samples are more than 50% correlated. For natural images, $\rho$ is typically very high (close to 1), yielding large gains.

### Linear Predictors

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=8| Linear predictors: structure and optimality (slide 56/78)]]

**Linear predictors** are simple, computationally efficient, and — crucially — **optimal for Gaussian random variables**.

A linear predictor of order $P$ is defined as:

$$v(n) = -\sum_{i=1}^{P} a_i x_{n-i} \qquad \text{(filter with } P \text{ parameters)}$$

The prediction error signal is:

$$y(n) = x(n) - v(n) = \sum_{i=0}^{P} a_i x_{n-i} \qquad \text{with } a_0 = 1$$

> $y$ is therefore the output of filtering $x$ with the **analysis filter**:
> $$A(z) = 1 + a_1 z^{-1} + \ldots + a_P z^{-P}$$

The optimal filter minimizes $\sigma^2_Y$ — the **variance of the prediction error**.

---

### Predictor Selection: Wiener-Hopf Equations

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=9| Predictor selection: derivation (slide 57/78)]]
[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=10| Predictor selection: matrix form (slide 58/78)]]
[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=11| Predictor selection: solution (slide 59/78)]]

**Problem**: Find the coefficient vector $\underline{a} = [a_1, \ldots, a_P]^T$ minimizing:

$$\sigma^2_Y = E\{Y^2(n)\} = E\left\{\left[X(n) + \sum_{i=1}^{P} a_i X(n-i)\right]^2\right\}$$

Expanding:

$$\sigma^2_Y = \sigma^2_X + 2\underline{r}^T\underline{a} + \underline{a}^T R_X \underline{a}$$

where:
$$\underline{r} = [r_X(1) \ldots r_X(P)]^T, \qquad r_X(k) = E\{X(n)X(n-k)\}$$

$$R_X = \begin{bmatrix} r_X(0) & r_X(1) & \cdots & r_X(P-1) \\ r_X(1) & r_X(0) & \cdots & r_X(P-2) \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots \\ r_X(P-1) & r_X(P-2) & \cdots & r_X(0) \end{bmatrix}$$

Setting $\partial\sigma^2_Y/\partial\underline{a} = 0$:

$$2\underline{r} + 2R_X \underline{a} = 0$$

> [!important] Wiener-Hopf Solution
> $$\underline{a}^{\text{opt}} = -R_X^{-1}\underline{r}$$
> $$\sigma^2_Y = \sigma^2_X + \underline{r}^T \underline{a}^{\text{opt}}$$

In practice, the autocorrelation $r_X$ is estimated from data:
$$\hat{r}_X(k) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1-k} X(n)X(n+k)$$

### Example: Predictive Quantization on the Peppers Image

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=12| Predictive quantization example: peppers image (slide 60/78)]]

For the **grayscale peppers image**, a second-order linear predictor is applied:

$$\hat{f}_{n,m} = a f_{n-1,m} + b f_{n,m-1}$$

The predictor is a weighted average of the **top neighbor** and the **left neighbor**.

| $a$ | $b$ | $\sigma^2_Y$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| 0 | 0 | 2902.7 |
| 1/2 | 1/2 | 78.7 |
| 0.449 | 0.546 | 78.4 |

> - **No prediction** ($a=b=0$): distortion is $\sigma^2_Q = 2902.7$ — the raw signal variance.
> - **Simple average** ($a=b=1/2$): distortion drops to $78.7$ — a reduction factor **greater than 37×**.
> - **Optimal predictor**: distortion $78.4$ — only a marginal improvement over the simple average.
>
> This illustrates that for natural images, a very simple predictor already captures most of the exploitable correlation. The optimal predictor provides negligible additional benefit.

### Impact of Filter Order on Prediction Gain

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=13| Impact of filter order on prediction gain (slide 61/78)]]

In **global DPCM**, the predictor $v(n)$ uses a linear combination of $N$ causal neighbors.

> [!note] Filter Order Trade-offs
> - **Order Selection**: Increasing from order 2 to 4 typically improves the Prediction Gain ($G_P = \sigma^2_X / \sigma^2_Y$), producing a more "whitened" residual.
> - **Screening Effect**: The benefit of including "distant" pixels diminishes rapidly. Immediate neighbors capture most of the mutual information — distant pixels are "screened" by the closer ones.
> - **Complexity vs. Performance**: Higher orders increase computational overhead with negligible PSNR gains for most natural textures.

### Rate-Distortion Analysis: Effect of Filter Order

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=14| RD analysis: filter order — peppers image (slide 62/78)]]
[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=15| RD analysis: filter order — lena image (slide 63/78)]]

Experiments on two 512×512 grayscale images compare orders 2, 3, and 4:

- **Peppers** (homogeneous image with some background noise): the three order curves are close together, showing small marginal gains from higher order. The image's spatial correlation is largely captured already at order 2.
- **Lena** (clean image with diverse textures, edges, and smooth areas): higher order provides more separation in the RD curves, especially at moderate bitrates. The richer structure gives the predictor more to gain from longer context.

> In both cases, the improvements from order 3 to 4 are smaller than those from order 2 to 3 — consistent with the screening effect.

### Local Adaptation: The Block-wise Approach

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=16| Local adaptation: block-wise approach (slide 64/78)]]

Images are **non-stationary** signals: a single global filter is suboptimal across varying textures (e.g., edges vs. smooth areas).

**Strategy**: Divide the image into $M \times M$ blocks and compute the **locally optimal filter** for each block.

> [!warning] Side Information Trade-off
> The total rate is increased by $\dfrac{N \cdot B}{M^2}$, where $N$ is the filter order and $B$ is the bits per coefficient (e.g., 16).
>
> - **Small Blocks**: Excellent tracking of local statistics, but **massive overhead** from filter coefficients.
> - **Large Blocks**: Minimal overhead, but the predictor **fails to adapt** to local features.

This is a fundamental trade-off between **coding efficiency** and **side information cost**.

---

### Rate-Distortion Analysis: Effect of Block Size

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=17| RD analysis: block size — peppers image (slide 65/78)]]
[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=18| RD analysis: block size — lena image (slide 66/78)]]

With order-4 predictor, varying the block size (512×512, 256×256, 64×64, 32×32):

- **Peppers**: Very little difference across block sizes — the image is homogeneous enough that a single global filter is nearly optimal. The overhead of per-block coefficients is a net loss at small block sizes.
- **Lena**: Moderate gains from smaller blocks at low-to-medium rates, but the benefit saturates. At high rates, smaller blocks slightly hurt performance due to increased side information.

> The takeaway is that block adaptation is most beneficial for images with high local variation, but the gain must exceed the side information cost to be worthwhile.

### The Drift Problem in Coding/Decoding

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=19| Coding/decoding scheme: wrong version (slide 67/78)]]
[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=20| Coding/decoding scheme: wrong version with drift label (slide 67/78)]]

A critical implementation issue arises from a naive implementation of the encoder-decoder pair.

> [!warning] Drift Problem
> If the **encoder** computes the prediction $v(n)$ from the **original (non-quantized)** past samples $x(n-1), x(n-2), \ldots$, but the **decoder** computes $v(n)$ from the **reconstructed (quantized)** samples $\hat{x}(n-1), \hat{x}(n-2), \ldots$, then:
>
> $$x \neq \hat{x} \implies v_{\text{encoder}} \neq v_{\text{decoder}}$$
>
> This mismatch **accumulates over time** — a phenomenon called **drift** — causing the reconstructed signal to diverge increasingly from the original.

---

### Drift Example (Wrong Scheme)

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=21| Predictive quantization drift example: wrong scheme (slide 68/78)]]

Simple predictive scheme: prediction = previous value; 3-bit quantizer with levels $\{-9, -6, -3, 0, 3, 6, 9\}$; encoder uses non-quantized values for prediction.

| Step | Original | Enc. Prediction | Error | Quant. Error | Dec. Prediction | Rec. Value |
|:----:|:--------:|:---------------:|:-----:|:------------:|:---------------:|:----------:|
| 1 | 10 | — | — | 9 | — | 9 |
| 2 | 11 | 10 | 1 | 0 | 9 | 9 |
| 3 | 12 | 11 | 1 | 0 | 9 | 9 |
| 4 | 13 | 12 | 1 | 0 | 9 | 9 |
| 5 | 14 | 13 | 1 | 0 | 9 | 9 |
| 6 | 18 | 14 | 4 | 3 | 9 | 12 |
| 7 | 21 | 18 | 3 | 3 | 12 | 15 |
| 8 | 18 | 21 | −3 | −3 | 15 | 12 |

> The decoder's prediction steadily lags behind the encoder's — the error compounds at each step. By step 7, the reconstructed value is 15 instead of 21. The drift becomes more severe as the signal changes rapidly.

---

### Correct Coding/Decoding Scheme

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=22| Correct coding/decoding scheme (slide 69/78)]]

The fix is conceptually simple but important: **both encoder and decoder must use the same data** for prediction.

> [!important] Correct Scheme
> The predictor is fed with the **quantized (reconstructed) past samples** $\hat{x}(n-1), \hat{x}(n-2), \ldots$ **at both encoder and decoder**. This ensures that $v_{\text{encoder}}(n) = v_{\text{decoder}}(n)$ at every step.
>
> The encoder therefore must:
> 1. Compute the prediction error $Y = X - V$
> 2. Quantize: $\hat{Y} = Q(Y)$
> 3. **Reconstruct** $\hat{X} = \hat{Y} + V$ — using the *quantized* error
> 4. Store $\hat{X}$ in a buffer and use it for future predictions

The reconstructed value $\hat{X}$ acts as a local feedback loop in the encoder, mirroring the decoder's state exactly.

---

### Drift Example (Correct Scheme)

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=23| Predictive quantization drift example: correct scheme (slide 70/78)]]

Same signal and quantizer, but now encoder uses quantized values for prediction:

| Step | Original | Enc. Prediction | Error | Quant. Error | Dec. Prediction | Rec. Value |
|:----:|:--------:|:---------------:|:-----:|:------------:|:---------------:|:----------:|
| 1 | 10 | — | — | 9 | — | 9 |
| 2 | 11 | 9 | 2 | 3 | 9 | 12 |
| 3 | 12 | 12 | 0 | 0 | 12 | 12 |
| 4 | 13 | 12 | 1 | 0 | 12 | 12 |
| 5 | 14 | 12 | 2 | 3 | 12 | 15 |
| 6 | 18 | 15 | 3 | 3 | 15 | 18 |
| 7 | 21 | 18 | 3 | 3 | 18 | 21 |
| 8 | 18 | 21 | −3 | −3 | 21 | 18 |

> The encoder and decoder predictions are now **identical** at every step. The reconstruction tracks the original signal accurately, without any accumulation of error.

### Coding Scheme: Summary

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=24| Coding scheme: summary (slide 71/78)]]

> [!summary] DPCM Coding Rules
> 1. The encoder computes the prediction error $Y = X - V$
> 2. The predictor $V$ must be computed from data **available at the decoder as well**
> 3. Thus the encoder must compute the **reconstructed value** $\hat{X} = \hat{Y} + V$
> 4. This value is stored in a buffer and used to compute the prediction of future values

This architecture is known as **DPCM (Differential Pulse Code Modulation)** and is the foundation of many audio and image compression standards.

### Direct vs. Predictive Quantization: Rate Estimation Caveat

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=25| Direct vs. PQ comparison with log₂L rate estimate — lena (slide 72/78)]]
[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=26| Direct vs. PQ comparison with log₂L rate estimate — peppers (slide 73/78)]]

When the rate is estimated as $R = \log_2 L$ (a fixed-length code), comparing direct quantization (UQ) and predictive quantization (PQ) on the Lena and Peppers images yields a **surprising result**: PQ does **not** consistently outperform direct UQ — and for peppers, it is often **worse**.

> [!warning] Why This Seems Paradoxical
> - The theoretical analysis assumed the predictor is computed from the **original signal**.
> - In practice, the predictor must use the **quantized past samples**, introducing an additional error source.
> - At **low bitrates**: the predictor is fed heavily quantized data — the prediction is very noisy.
> - At **high bitrates**: the quantizer must reproduce the **additive noise** present in the image (for peppers), which it is not designed to do efficiently.
> - Moreover, estimating rate as $\log_2 L$ is **too pessimistic**: it assumes a uniform distribution of quantization indices, which is very far from reality for PQ residuals.

---

### PQ Prediction Error Distribution

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=27| PQ prediction error distribution and histogram (slide 74/78)]]
[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=28| PQ prediction error: entropy coding argument (slide 75/78)]]

For the Lena image with $\Delta = 8$ and $L = 19$ levels, the estimated rate is $\log_2 19 \approx 4.25$ bits/sample.

However, examining the **histogram of quantization indices** reveals a dramatically **non-uniform distribution**:
- $\approx 84\%$ of the quantization indices are **zero**
- Indices outside $(-3, 3)$ account for **less than 1%** of the total

> [!important] Key Insight: Entropy Coding is Essential
> The uniform-length code grossly overestimates the actual information content of the residual. A **variable-length code** (entropy coder) assigning short codewords to the frequent index 0 and longer codewords to rare indices would dramatically reduce the actual bitrate.
>
> The conclusion: **entropy coding is not optional in PQ — it is what makes the system competitive**.

### Direct vs. Predictive Quantization with Entropy Coding

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=29| Direct vs. PQ with entropy coding — lena (slide 76/78)]]
[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=30| Direct vs. PQ with entropy coding — peppers (slide 77/78)]]

When the **actual coding rate** (using entropy coding) is used on the x-axis, the picture changes completely:

- **Lena**: PQ + entropy coding is **dramatically better** than UQ + entropy coding:
  - $\Delta\text{PSNR} = 20\,\text{dB}$ at 1 bpp
  - Or equivalently: an **86% rate reduction** at 30 dB PSNR

- **Peppers**: Similar results — PQ + entropy coding significantly outperforms direct quantization across all rates.

> This is the definitive demonstration of the value of predictive quantization: the combination of **prediction + entropy coding** is a powerful and practical compression strategy. Neither technique alone is sufficient; together, they exploit both the signal's correlation structure and the resulting sparsity of the residual.

### Quantization: Wrap-Up

[[L02_scalar_predictive_quantization_3.pdf#page=31| Quantization wrap-up (slide 78/78)]]

> [!summary] Final Summary
>
> - **UQ**: $D = K_X \sigma^2_X 2^{-2R}$ for uniform RV or in high resolution
> - **Optimal SQ**: similar behavior with shape factor $c_X$
> - Both UQ and optimal SQ applied **directly** to images or sound are **catastrophic**: quality drops and rate is not reduced much
> - **Predictive Quantization** can dramatically improve performance, but with three non-negotiable conditions:
>   1. **Never** compute the prediction on original data — always use **quantized** past samples, or the decoder will drift
>   2. The input signal must be **correlated enough** ($G_P > 0$, i.e., $\rho > 1/2$)
>   3. **Entropy coding** must be used to achieve good performance
>
> The best encoding strategy is **entropy coding** — the subject of the next lesson.
