\section{Core Idea}

\textbf{Quality assessment} assigns a quantitative grade to a multimedia stimulus as perceived by human observers. In multimedia services, this grade is needed because quality is the \textbf{benefit} in the tradeoff against rate, latency, complexity, memory, and energy.

\begin{calloutbox}{red!70!black}{Quality assessment}
\textbf{Statement:} quality assessment maps one or more multimedia signals to a number describing perceived quality.

\textbf{Meaning:} if humans produce the number, the method is subjective. If an algorithm produces it, the method is objective. Subjective evaluation remains ground truth.
\end{calloutbox}

\textbf{QoS} (\textit{Quality of Service}) describes measurable system/network properties: bitrate, delay, jitter, loss, resolution, frame rate. \textbf{QoE} (\textit{Quality of Experience}) describes final user perception, including content, display, context, expectations, and interaction.

\begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.833\linewidth]{ch11/rate-quality-tradeoff.png}\end{center}
Rate-quality curves show why quality assessment is necessary: codec/service choices must compare quality gain against bitrate cost.

\subsection{Subjective Testing}

Subjective testing directly measures human opinion, but must control:

\begin{itemize}
  \item lab illumination and display;
  \item viewing distance;
  \item monitor characteristics;
  \item observer acuity and color vision;
  \item content selection;
  \item stimulus order;
  \item training and fatigue;
  \item score processing and outlier handling.
\end{itemize}

\begin{calloutbox}{red!70!black}{Subjective assessment}
Subjective experiments are expensive and slow, but they are the gold standard because objective metrics are only useful when they correlate with human perception.
\end{calloutbox}

\section{Theory and Formulas}

\subsection{Spatial Information}

For each frame $F_n$, apply Sobel filtering and compute spatial standard deviation:

\[
SI=\max_{time}\{\sigma_{space}[Sobel(F_n)]\}
\]

High $SI$ means many edges/textures. Low $SI$ means smooth content.

\subsection{Temporal Information}

Frame difference:

\[
M_n(i,j)=F_n(i,j)-F_{n-1}(i,j)
\]

Temporal Information:

\[
TI=\max_{time}\{\sigma_{space}[M_n(i,j)]\}
\]

High $TI$ means strong motion or temporal changes.

\subsection{MOS}

If $N$ observers give scores $x_i$:

\[
MOS=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_i
\]

\begin{calloutbox}{red!70!black}{MOS limitation}
MOS estimates average perceived quality. It does not describe observer disagreement or statistical confidence.
\end{calloutbox}

\begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.933\linewidth]{ch11/mos-histogram.png}\end{center}
Subjective scores are random variables; MOS is estimated from their distribution.

\subsection{Variability, Standard Error, Confidence}

Standard deviation:

\[
s=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i-m)^2}
\]

Standard error:

\[
SE=\frac{s}{\sqrt{N}}
\]

Approximate 95\% confidence interval around MOS:

\[
ci=[-1.96SE,\;1.96SE]
\]

\begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.867\linewidth]{ch11/standard-error-sampling.png}\end{center}
More observers reduce uncertainty of MOS estimate, not intrinsic disagreement.

\begin{calloutbox}{red!70!black}{Standard error}
$s$ measures spread among observers. $SE$ measures uncertainty of mean estimate. Increasing $N$ decreases $SE$ but does not force observers to agree.
\end{calloutbox}

\subsection{Human Visual System Effects}

Objective metrics should consider visual perception:

\begin{itemize}
  \item local contrast matters;
  \item same luminance can look different on different backgrounds;
  \item edges can create overshoot/undershoot perception;
  \item motion and geometry illusions show that perception is not pixel-wise.
\end{itemize}

\begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.533\linewidth]{ch11/mach-band-effect.png}\end{center}
Mach bands show perceived brightness overshoot near intensity transitions.

\begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.867\linewidth]{ch11/simultaneous-contrast.png}\end{center}
Simultaneous contrast: identical gray patches appear different depending on background.

\subsection{MSE and PSNR}

For luminance component at frame $t$:

\[
MSE_Y(t)=\frac{1}{NM}\sum_{n,m}
\left(I(n,m,1,t)-\hat{I}(n,m,1,t)\right)^2
\]

PSNR:

\[
PSNR_Y(t)=10\log_{10}\frac{255^2}{MSE_Y(t)}
\approx 48\text{dB}-10\log_{10}MSE_Y(t)
\]

Typical interpretation:

\begin{center}\fitwidth{%
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|}
\hline
\textbf{PSNR} & \textbf{Quality} \\ \hline
$>45$ dB & excellent \\ \hline
40-45 dB & very good \\ \hline
30-40 dB & good to very good \\ \hline
$<30$ dB & usually poor \\ \hline
\end{tabular}}
\end{center}

Weighted YCbCr PSNR:

\[
PSNR_{YCbCr}(t)=
\frac{3}{4}PSNR_Y(t)
+\frac{1}{8}PSNR_{Cb}(t)
+\frac{1}{8}PSNR_{Cr}(t)
\]

Video sequence average:

\[
PSNR_Y=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}PSNR_Y(t)
\]

\begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.833\linewidth]{ch11/psnr-frame-plot.png}\end{center}
Per-frame PSNR varies across I/P/B frames, so sequence averages hide temporal behavior.

\subsection{Bjontegaard Metrics}

Rate-distortion curves are compared with:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{BD-Rate}: average bitrate difference at same quality, in percent;
  \item \textbf{BD-PSNR}: average PSNR difference at same bitrate, in dB.
\end{itemize}

Typical procedure:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item measure 4 or 5 RD points per codec;
  \item fit polynomial, often in $t=\log R$;
  \item integrate curve difference on common interval;
  \item divide by interval length.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.867\linewidth]{ch11/bjontegaard-area.png}\end{center}
Bjontegaard area summarizes full RD-curve difference into one number.

\subsection{Why PSNR Is Not Enough}

PSNR treats all pixel errors equally. It ignores:

\begin{itemize}
  \item masking;
  \item structure and edges;
  \item temporal sensitivity;
  \item artifact type;
  \item viewing conditions.
\end{itemize}

\begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.933\linewidth]{ch11/similar-psnr-different-quality.png}\end{center}
Different distortions can have similar PSNR but very different perceived quality.

\begin{calloutbox}{red!70!black}{PSNR limitation}
Similar PSNR does not imply similar QoE. Blur, blocking, ringing, frame drops, and motion judder can have different annoyance at same pixel-error level.
\end{calloutbox}

\subsection{SSIM}

\textbf{SSIM} (\textit{Structural Similarity Index}) is full-reference and compares luminance, contrast, and structure:

\[
SSIM(x,y)=[l(x,y)]^\alpha[c(x,y)]^\beta[s(x,y)]^\gamma
\]

Range:

\begin{center}\fitwidth{%
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|}
\hline
\textbf{SSIM} & \textbf{Meaning} \\ \hline
1.0 & identical \\ \hline
$>0.95$ & very high \\ \hline
0.8-0.9 & noticeable degradation \\ \hline
\end{tabular}}
\end{center}

Components:

\begin{itemize}
  \item $l(x,y)$: luminance similarity, related to light adaptation;
  \item $c(x,y)$: contrast similarity, related to contrast masking;
  \item $s(x,y)$: structure similarity.
\end{itemize}

SSIM better follows HVS than PSNR, but remains limited for video temporal perception and complex artifacts.

\subsection{VMAF}

\textbf{VMAF} (\textit{Video Multi-Method Assessment Fusion}) is full-reference video metric from Netflix.

It fuses:

\begin{itemize}
  \item VIF/detail preservation;
  \item DLM/structural information;
  \item temporal information;
  \item learned mapping from subjective data;
  \item spatial and temporal pooling.
\end{itemize}

Values run from 0 to 100. Around 90+ often indicates good perceptual quality; 93 is common target.

\begin{center}\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ch11/vmaf-pipeline.png}\end{center}
VMAF combines perceptual features and learned fusion to predict subjective quality.

\subsection{AI-Based Metrics}

Modern metrics use deep networks and subjective datasets:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{LPIPS}: perceptual feature distance;
  \item \textbf{DISTS}: deep image structure/texture similarity;
  \item learned NR models such as CLIP-IQA-like approaches.
\end{itemize}

AI metrics can model complex artifacts and generated media, but depend strongly on training data and validation.

\subsection{Reduced-Reference Metrics}

RR metrics transmit selected reference features:

\begin{itemize}
  \item edge statistics;
  \item frequency features;
  \item texture descriptors;
  \item motion descriptors.
\end{itemize}

Use cases: IPTV monitoring, adaptive streaming, network QoE monitoring.

Limitation: less accurate than FR and requires side information.

\subsection{No-Reference Metrics}

NR metrics infer quality without original signal. Examples:

\begin{center}\fitwidth{%
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|}
\hline
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Main idea} \\ \hline
BRISQUE & natural scene statistics \\ \hline
NIQE & deviation from natural image statistics \\ \hline
PIQE & block-wise distortion estimation \\ \hline
Deep NR & learned perceptual prediction \\ \hline
\end{tabular}}
\end{center}

NR is essential for social media, user-generated content, mobile capture, surveillance, and streaming analytics. It is hardest because metric must distinguish real scene properties from distortions.

\section{Visual Schemes}

\subsection{Subjective Quality Experiment}

\begin{center}\footnotesize\itshape Diagram (Mermaid source):\end{center}
\begin{verbatim}
flowchart LR
    A[Stimulus set] --> B[Lab setup]
    B --> C[Observer screening]
    C --> D[Training]
    D --> E[Randomized test session]
    E --> F[Scores]
    F --> G[Outlier processing]
    G --> H[MOS + CI]
\end{verbatim}

Subjective tests require controlled environment, controlled stimuli, and statistical score processing.

\subsection{Objective Metric Pipeline}

\begin{center}\footnotesize\itshape Diagram (Mermaid source):\end{center}
\begin{verbatim}
flowchart LR
    A[Reference signal] --> B{Reference available?}
    C[Processed signal] --> B
    B -- full --> D[FR metric]
    B -- partial --> E[RR metric]
    B -- none --> F[NR metric]
    D --> G[Predicted quality]
    E --> G
    F --> G
\end{verbatim}

Reference availability determines objective metric family.

\subsection{QoE Optimization Loop}

\begin{center}\footnotesize\itshape Diagram (Mermaid source):\end{center}
\begin{verbatim}
flowchart LR
    A[Codec / network parameters] --> B[Delivered media]
    B --> C[QoS: rate delay loss frame rate]
    B --> D[Perceived quality]
    D --> E[QoE score]
    E --> F[Optimization]
    C --> F
    F --> A
\end{verbatim}

Systems should optimize user-perceived QoE, not only raw QoS or PSNR.

\begin{calloutbox}{red!70!black}{Main takeaway}
Multimedia quality is ultimately human perception. PSNR and QoS are useful engineering signals, but codec and service decisions should be validated against subjective QoE or objective metrics trained to predict it.
\end{calloutbox}
