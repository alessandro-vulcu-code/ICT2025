> [!Example] Sudden Throughput Drop in ABR
> **Problem:** Consider an ABR system with $L = 2$, $M = 1$, and $N = 6$, where throughput suddenly drops during segment download.
>
> **Approach:** Track segment download and playout timelines and observe $B(t)$.
>
> **Solution:** Segments 1-3 download quickly. Segment 4 takes longer because throughput drops. During this period the buffer decreases, reaches zero, and causes rebuffering. After recovery, playout continues with segments 5 and 6.
>
> **Result:** the buffer rises to about $2T_S$, declines through $T_S$, reaches $0$, triggers rebuffering, then recovers.
>
> **Takeaway:** sudden throughput drops can cause starvation even if previous segments were downloaded quickly.

> [!Example] Exercise 1
> **Problem:** Consider an adaptive streaming system with:
>
> - segment duration $T_S = 1$ s;
> - number of quality levels $K = 3$;
> - bitrates $R_C(1) = 500\,\text{kbps}$, $R_C(2) = 1\,\text{Mbps}$, $R_C(3) = 2\,\text{Mbps}$.
>
> The initial buffering stops after $t_{\text{ST}}$ seconds. This is the duration of initial buffering, not the amount of downloaded playable video.
>
> Set $t=0$ when the first bit is received. The server-client connection has throughput:
>
> $$
> \forall t \in (0,T),\quad S(t) = S_1 = 0.4\,\text{Mbps}
> $$
>
> $$
> \forall t > T,\quad S(t) = S_2 = 0.5\,\text{Mbps}
> $$
>
> Find the minimum initial buffering that assures no stalls when $q(n)=1$, meaning the client always requests quality level 1.
>
> **Approach:** Compare buffer slopes during rebuffering and playout before and after $T$.
>
> **Solution:** For $t<T$, $S < R_C$. For $t>T$, $S = R_C$. During rebuffering:
>
> $$
> B'_{\text{rebuff}}(t) = \frac{S}{R_C}
> $$
>
> During playout:
>
> $$
> B'_{\text{playout}}(t) = \frac{S}{R_C} - 1
> $$
>
> If $t_{\text{ST}} > T$, the slope of the buffer is:
>
> $$
> B'(t) =
> \begin{cases}
> \dfrac{S_1}{R_C} = 0.8 & \forall t \in (0,T) \\
> \dfrac{S_2}{R_C} = 1 & \forall t \in (T,t_{\text{ST}}) \\
> \dfrac{S_2}{R_C} - 1 = 0 & \forall t > t_{\text{ST}}
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> In this case, the buffer never depletes because the slope is positive or zero. There is no rebuffering.
>
> If $t_{\text{ST}} = T$, the buffer reaches $0.8T$ before playout starts, then stays constant.
>
> If $t_{\text{ST}} < T$, during $(0,t_{\text{ST}})$ the buffer fills with slope:
>
> $$
> B' = \frac{S_1}{R_C} = 0.8
> $$
>
> so:
>
> $$
> B(t_{\text{ST}}) = 0.8\,t_{\text{ST}}
> $$
>
> For $t > t_{\text{ST}}$ until the buffer is empty, the slope is:
>
> $$
> B' = \frac{S_1}{R_C} - 1 = -0.2
> $$
>
> hence:
>
> $$
> B(t) = B(t_{\text{ST}}) + B'(t-t_{\text{ST}})
> = 0.8\,t_{\text{ST}} - 0.2(t-t_{\text{ST}})
> = t_{\text{ST}} - 0.2t
> $$
>
> The buffer decreases linearly until it reaches zero or until $t=T$, when the slope becomes zero. The zero time $t^*$ is:
>
> $$
> B(t^*) = 0
> \Leftrightarrow t_{\text{ST}} - 0.2t^* = 0
> \Leftrightarrow t^* = 5t_{\text{ST}}
> $$
>
> To avoid rebuffering, the buffer must not empty before the slope changes at $T$:
>
> $$
> T < 5t_{\text{ST}}
> $$
>
> The minimum is obtained at:
>
> $$
> T = 5t_{\text{ST}}
> $$
>
> therefore:
>
> $$
> t_{\text{ST}} = \frac{T}{5}
> $$
>
> **Result:** the minimum initial buffering is $t_{\text{ST}} = T/5$.
>
> **Takeaway:** initial buffering must be long enough for the buffer to survive the low-throughput interval before capacity becomes equal to the selected coding rate.

> [!Example] Exercise 2 Question 1
> **Problem:** Determine when playout starts if $q(n)=3$ for all $n$.
>
> **Approach:** Initial buffering ends after $L=2$ segments have been downloaded.
>
> **Solution:** The number of bits needed is:
>
> $$
> N_b = T_S R_C(q(1)) + T_S R_C(q(2))
> $$
>
> More generally:
>
> $$
> N_b = T_S \sum_{n=1}^{L} R_C(q(n))
> $$
>
> Here $q(1)=q(2)=3$, and $R_C(3)=2\,\text{Mbps}$. Each segment takes 2 Mbits, so:
>
> $$
> N_b = 2 + 2 = 4\,\text{Mbits}
> $$
>
> In the first 2 s:
>
> $$
> N_1 = 2 \cdot 1.5 = 3\,\text{Mbits}
> $$
>
> In the next 1 s:
>
> $$
> N_2 = 1 \cdot 0.1 = 0.1\,\text{Mbits}
> $$
>
> Remaining bits:
>
> $$
> N_b - N_1 - N_2 = 0.9\,\text{Mbits}
> $$
>
> Starting from $t=3$, throughput is $S_3=1.8\,\text{Mbps}$, so the remaining download time is:
>
> $$
> \frac{0.9}{1.8} = 0.5\,\text{s}
> $$
>
> **Result:** playout starts at:
>
> $$
> t_{\text{ST}} = 3.5\,\text{s}
> $$
>
> **Takeaway:** startup time depends on the cumulative bits required for the first $L$ requested segments and the piecewise throughput profile.

> [!Example] Exercise 2 Question 2
> **Problem:** Find frequency and duration of rebuffering for $q(n)=3$.
>
> **Approach:** After $t_{\text{ST}}=3.5$, throughput is constant at $S_3=1.8\,\text{Mbps}$ and $R_C=2\,\text{Mbps}$.
>
> **Solution:** Since $R_C>S$, the buffer decreases. During playout:
>
> $$
> B'(t) = \frac{S_3}{R_C} - 1 = \frac{1.8}{2} - 1 = -\frac{1}{10}
> $$
>
> Starting from $B(t_{\text{ST}})=2$, the buffer empties after:
>
> $$
> \frac{B(t_{\text{ST}})}{|B'|} = \frac{2}{1/10} = 20\,\text{s}
> $$
>
> During these 20 s of playout, the client receives:
>
> $$
> 20 \cdot 1.8 = 36\,\text{Mbits}
> $$
>
> corresponding to:
>
> $$
> \frac{36}{R_C} = \frac{36}{2} = 18\,\text{s}
> $$
>
> of playable video. Together with the initial 2 s, received and played video equals $2+18=20$ s, so the buffer becomes empty at:
>
> $$
> t_{\text{rebuff}} = 3.5 + 20 = 23.5\,\text{s}
> $$
>
> Rebuffering then starts. The current segment has been fully downloaded, so only the next segment is needed:
>
> $$
> N_{\text{rebuff}} = T_S R_C(q(n)) = 2\,\text{Mbits}
> $$
>
> The source states this takes:
>
> $$
> \frac{N_{\text{rebuff}}}{S} = \frac{2}{1.5} = \frac{10}{9} \approx 1.1\overline{1}\,\text{s}
> $$
>
> (note: possible OCR artifact, because for $t>3$ the stated throughput profile gives $S_3=1.8\,\text{Mbps}$, which would produce $2/1.8=10/9$ s; the final numerical value $10/9$ matches $S=1.8$ rather than $1.5$. The original expression is preserved.)
>
> After rebuffering, the buffer has 1 s of playable video. It empties again after 10 s, then the next rebuffering lasts again $10/9$ s.
>
> **Result:** the rebuffering period is:
>
> $$
> T_{\text{rebuff}} = 11.\overline{1}\,\text{s}
> $$
>
> and stall duration is:
>
> $$
> \Delta = \frac{10}{9}\,\text{s}
> $$
>
> **Takeaway:** because $S_3<R_C(3)$, maximum quality creates periodic rebuffering after startup.

> [!Example] Exercise 2 Question 3
> **Problem:** Show that the periodic strategy using level 2 every fifth segment avoids rebuffering.
>
> **Approach:** Analyze one 5-segment cycle after startup.
>
> **Solution:** Initial rebuffering ends at $t=3.5$ s because the first two segments are requested with $k=3$, as before. Therefore:
>
> $$
> B(3.5)=2
> $$
>
> The third segment is requested at $k=2$. Its download time is:
>
> $$
> T_D(3) = \frac{T_S R_C(2)}{S_3} = \frac{1}{1.8}\,\text{s} = \frac{5}{9}\,\text{s}
> $$
>
> During this time, the buffer slope is:
>
> $$
> B'(t)=\frac{S}{R_C}-1 = 0.8\,\text{s/s}
> $$
>
> The slope is positive, so the buffer fills. After downloading the segment:
>
> $$
> B = B(3.5) + B'(t)T_D(3)
> = 2 + \frac{5}{9}\cdot\frac{4}{5}
> = 2.\overline{4}\,\text{s}
> $$
>
> Now four segments, from 4 to 7, are downloaded at level $k=3$. Since $S_n$ is constant, download time is:
>
> $$
> T_D = 4T_D(n,3)
> = 4 \frac{T_S R_C(3)}{S_n}
> = 4 \cdot \frac{2}{1.8}\,\text{s}
> = \frac{40}{9}\,\text{s}
> = 4.\overline{4}\,\text{s}
> $$
>
> During these four downloads the buffer is depleted. When segment 7 finishes, the buffer is:
>
> $$
> B(t) = 2.\overline{4} - \frac{4.\overline{4}}{10} = 2\,\text{s}
> $$
>
> The system has returned to the same buffer level at the start of the cycle, so the pattern repeats every 5 segments.
>
> **Result:** the buffer oscillates between $2$ and $2.\overline{4}$ seconds and never reaches zero.
>
> **Takeaway:** periodic quality reduction provides enough buffer gain to compensate for four high-quality segments.

> [!Example] Exercise 2 Question 4
> **Problem:** Decide whether periodic switching is better than always using $q=2$.
>
> **Approach:** Compare QoE for the two no-stall strategies.
>
> **Solution:** For the first strategy, there is no stall, so:
>
> $$
> J_1 =
> \sum_{n=1}^{N}
> [\lambda_1 q_1(n) - \lambda_2 |q_1(n)-q_1(n-1)|]
> - \lambda_3 T_1
> $$
>
> Here $q_1=3$ for $4N/5$ segments and $q_1=2$ for $N/5$ segments. Also, $|q_1(n)-q_1(n-1)|=1$ for $2N/5$ segments. Therefore:
>
> $$
> J_1 =
> \frac{12}{5}N\lambda_1
> + \frac{2}{5}N\lambda_1
> - \frac{2}{5}N\lambda_2
> - \lambda_3 T_1
> $$
>
> $$
> J_1 =
> N\left(\frac{14}{5}\lambda_1 - \frac{2}{5}\lambda_2\right)
> - \lambda_3 T_1
> $$
>
> In the second case there is no stall either. The QoE is:
>
> $$
> J_2 =
> \sum_{n=1}^{N}
> [\lambda_1 q_2(n) - \lambda_2 |q_2(n)-q_2(n-1)|]
> - \lambda_3 T_2
> $$
>
> Since $q_2=2$ always and there are no changes:
>
> $$
> J_2 = 2N\lambda_1 - \lambda_3 T_2
> $$
>
> For large $N$:
>
> $$
> J_1 - J_2
> \approx
> N\left(\frac{4}{5}\lambda_1 - \frac{2}{5}\lambda_2\right)
> $$
>
> This is positive if and only if:
>
> $$
> \lambda_1 > \frac{\lambda_2}{2}
> $$
>
> **Result:** periodic switching is better than constant level 2 only when the reward for higher quality is large enough compared with the penalty for quality changes.
>
> **Takeaway:** the best strategy depends on subjective weights $\lambda_i$, which are usually evaluated through subjective tests.
