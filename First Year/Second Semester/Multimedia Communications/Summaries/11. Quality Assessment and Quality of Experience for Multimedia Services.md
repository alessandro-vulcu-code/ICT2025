## Overview

This note covers the assessment of multimedia quality and the relationship between quality of
service, quality of experience, subjective quality evaluation, objective quality evaluation,
modern perceptual metrics, and interactive quality evaluation.

The main topics are:

- Introduction to **Multimedia Quality Assessment**.
- **QoS** versus **QoE**.
- **Subjective Quality Assessment**.
- **Objective Quality Assessment**.
- Modern perceptual metrics.
- Interactive quality evaluation.

## Quality Assessment

> [!Important] Quality Assessment
> **Statement:** In digital multimedia services, **quality assessment** associates a quantitative
> grade with an image, a video, a sound, or any other possible *stimulus*, that is, a **signal**
> perceived by a **human observer**.
>
> **Interpretation:** Quality assessment turns the perceived quality of multimedia content into a
> measurable quantity that can be used when designing, comparing, or optimizing multimedia services.

Since a stimulus is a signal, quality assessment can be seen as a *functional*: an operator that
associates a number with one or more multimedia signals.

- If the number is obtained by applying an algorithm to the signals, the process is an
  **objective quality assessment**.
- If the number is obtained by asking people to evaluate the stimuli, the process is a
  **subjective quality assessment**.

What finally matters is the impact on observers, so subjective assessment remains the reference
against which objective methods are judged.

### Why Quality Assessment Is Needed

Quality assessment plays a major role in the design of multimedia services because it is the
"benefit" term in a cost-benefit analysis: it answers the question, "How good is the quality of the
delivered signal?"

The cost depends on the application. For compression and transmission systems, the cost is
typically the **coding rate**, but it can also include:

- the number of operations, or **time complexity**;
- the amount of memory required, or **space complexity**;
- **energy** consumption;
- **latency**.
![[Pasted image 20260515104515.png]]
*Figure 1: Rate-distortion curves for HDR MPEG, HDRV, JPEG HDR fullsamp, JPEG HDR precorrect, and JPEG HDR postcorrect, with SNR in dB plotted against bits per pixel.*

### Quality Rate Trade-Off

The trade-off between coding rate and quality is central in multimedia communications.

- Coding rate and, to some extent, complexity are easy to measure.
- Quality is much harder to measure because it depends on perception.
- The course presents different ways to assess image and video quality.
- Mastering the quality/rate trade-off is necessary to make design decisions in multimedia
  service systems.

Typical design questions include:

- How should two compression methods be compared?
- How should a streaming service be configured?
- How much rate is worth spending for a given increase in perceived quality?

### Taxonomy of Quality Assessment Methods
![[Pasted image 20260515104621.png]]
*Figure 2: Taxonomy tree for image quality assessment, divided into subjective and objective methods. Subjective methods include single stimulus methods such as ACR/MOS, double stimulus methods with reference and impaired stimuli, and pairwise comparison or A/B comparison. Objective methods include full-reference, reduced-reference, and no-reference metrics.*

The taxonomy separates **subjective** and **objective** approaches.

Subjective methods include:

- **Single stimulus** methods, including ACR and MOS-based evaluation.
- **Double stimulus** methods, where reference and impaired versions are shown.
- **Pairwise comparison**, where two versions are compared directly.

Objective methods include:

- **Full-reference (FR)** metrics, where the original signal is available.
- **Reduced-reference (RR)** metrics, where partial side information is available.
- **No-reference (NR)** metrics, where no original signal is available.

## Subjective Quality Evaluation

### Subjective Assessment

Subjective assessment directly measures human judgment.

- **Pro:** It is the most reliable way to measure the quality of experience of multimedia services.
- **Con:** Subjective tests are usually costly and time-consuming, and require many users to
  provide reliable results.

To obtain reliable and reproducible subjective results, the following elements must be defined very
carefully:

- **Laboratory equipment**, including screen and viewing distance, illumination, and room
  reverberation characteristics.
- **Data set**, including the original and processed contents and their distribution.
- **Test methodology**, including the rating target, the scale, and the type of stimuli.
- **Score processing**, including score normalization, outlier detection, MOS and confidence
  interval computation, and significance tests.

> [!Important] Subjective Experiments
> **Statement:** **Subjective experiments remain the gold standard for perceptual quality
> evaluation.**
>
> **Interpretation:** Objective metrics are useful only if they correlate well with what human
> observers actually perceive.

### Lab Environment

The laboratory environment affects subjective quality scores. General viewing conditions include:

- room illumination;
- chromaticity of the background;
- peak luminance;
- monitor contrast ratio;
- ratio between the luminance of the background behind the picture monitor and the peak luminance
  of the picture.

Standardized conditions include:

- viewing distance;
- observation angle;
- room environment color scheme.

### Monitor Characteristics

Using monitors with different characteristics produces different subjective image qualities.
Relevant characteristics include:

- monitor processing;
- resolution;
- contrast;
- brightness;
- motion interpolation;
- refresh rate;
- OLED black levels;
- local dimming.

### Observers

Observers may be **expert** or **non-expert**, depending on the goals of the assessment.

Typical laboratory studies use between 15 and 40 observers. Before a session, observers should be
screened for:

- **normal visual acuity**, using a Snellen chart or Landolt chart;
- **normal color vision**, using selected color vision charts.

Experimenters should describe the characteristics of their assessment panels in as much detail as
possible, so that the influence of the panel can be investigated in later studies.

### Stimuli

The selection of stimuli depends on the goal of the subjective experiment. Examples include
comparing compression algorithms or evaluating the performance of an enhancement method.

For **video stimuli**, it is useful to compare the relative **Spatial Information** and
**Temporal Information** of the available sequences. If only a small number of test sequences is
used, they should span a large portion of the *spatio-temporal information* space.

### Spatial Information and Temporal Information

Spatial Information and Temporal Information help characterize video content.

Intuitively:

- high $SI$ means textured or detail-rich content;
- low $SI$ means smooth or cartoon-like content;
- high $TI$ means sports or action scenes;
- low $TI$ means news or interview content.

| Content | SI | TI |
|---|---|---|
| Football match | Medium | High |
| Anime | Medium | Low |
| Nature documentary | High | Medium |
| Video call | Low | Low |

> [!Important] Spatial Information
> **Statement:** **Spatial Information** ($SI$) measures the amount of spatial detail in a picture.
> It is usually higher for more spatially complex scenes.
>
> $$
> SI = \max_{time}\{\sigma_{space}[Sobel(F_n)]\}
> $$
>
> **Interpretation:** The Sobel filter emphasizes contours and edges. The maximum spatial standard
> deviation across frames summarizes the highest spatial complexity observed in the sequence.

The $SI$ computation is based on the Sobel filter, a discrete analogue of the spatial gradient.
The output of a Sobel filter is large near object contours and small in flat areas.

For each video frame at time $n$, denoted by $F_n$:

1. Filter the frame with the Sobel filter, obtaining $Sobel(F_n)$.
2. Compute the standard deviation over the pixels, $\sigma_{space}$.
3. Repeat this for every frame, obtaining a time series of spatial activity.
4. Select the maximum value over time.
![[Pasted image 20260515104905.png|600]]
*Figure 7: Pipeline for computing Spatial Information, showing $F_n$ followed by Sobel filtering, variance or standard deviation computation, maximum selection, and $SI$. The visual includes an original grayscale image, a colored Sobel-filtered heat map, and a graph of $\sigma_n^2$ over frames 0 to 250.*

For the example video sequence, $SI = 161.6$.

> [!Important] Temporal Information
> **Statement:** **Temporal Information** ($TI$) measures the amount of temporal change in a video
> sequence. It is usually large for high-motion sequences.
>
> $$
> M_n(i,j) = F_n(i,j) - F_{n-1}(i,j)
> $$
>
> $$
> TI = \max_{time}\{\sigma_{space}[M_n(i,j)]\}
> $$
>
> **Interpretation:** $TI$ measures frame-to-frame luminance differences and summarizes the largest
> spatial variation of these temporal differences.

Here, $F_n(i,j)$ is the pixel luminance at the $i^{th}$ row and $j^{th}$ column of the $n^{th}$
frame in time. Diverse motion in adjacent frames produces higher $TI$ values.

![[Pasted image 20260515105231.png]]
*Figure 8: Motion Difference for a given $n$, shown as a color map with scale from -200 to +200, and a graph of the standard deviation as a function of frame index from 0 to 250. The example reports $TI = 39.20$.*

### Instructions and Test Session

Assessors should be carefully introduced to:

- the method of assessment;
- the types of impairment or quality factors likely to occur;
- the grading scale;
- the sequence and timing of the experiment.

Training sequences should demonstrate the range and type of impairments to be assessed. They should
use illustrative images other than those used in the actual test, but with comparable sensitivity.

A test session should not last more than half an hour. At the beginning of the first session, about
five **dummy presentations** should be introduced to stabilize the observers' opinion. The data
from these presentations must not be considered in the results.

The images and impairments should be presented in a pseudo-random sequence and, preferably, in a
different sequence for each session.

![[Pasted image 20260515105459.png]]
*Figure 9: Temporal scheme of a test session from BT.0500-01-2, including training sequences, stabilizing sequences whose results are not processed, a break for observers' questions, and the main part of the test session.*

The **learning effect** must also be controlled. Participants may:

- change their criteria;
- become more sensitive due to training;
- become less sensitive due to tiredness.

To control this effect:

- run a training session that covers the range of stimuli, so participants can determine their
  criteria;
- keep sessions short, for example below 20 minutes or below 40 minutes;
- pay participants;
- randomize stimuli as much as possible.

### Subjective Test Methodologies

#### Single-Stimulus or Absolute Category Rating

In **single-stimulus (SS)** methods, also called **Absolute Category Rating (ACR)** methods, a
single image or sequence of images is presented, and the assessor provides an index for the entire
presentation.

The test material may include:

- only test sequences;
- both test sequences and their corresponding reference sequences.

In the second case, the reference sequence is presented as a free-standing stimulus and rated like
any other test stimulus.
![[Pasted image 20260515105531.png|500]]
*Figure 10: Software interface for subjective image quality evaluation with a 0 to 5 rating scale.*

#### Absolute Category Rating with Hidden Reference

In **ACR-HR**, test sequences are presented one at a time and rated independently on a category
scale. The procedure **must include** a reference version of each test sequence, shown as any other
test stimulus. This is called a **hidden reference** condition.

During data analysis, a **Differential Mean Opinion Score (DMOS)** is computed between each test
sequence and its corresponding hidden reference.

#### Double Stimulus Impairment Scale

In the **Double Stimulus Impairment Scale (DSIS)** method, the reference and processed
sequence/image are shown. Viewers rate degradation on a discrete scale:

- imperceptible;
- perceptible but not annoying;
- fair;
- poor;
- bad.

![[Pasted image 20260515105605.png]]
*Figure 11: DSIS timing scheme with Reference Video A for 10 s, pause for 1 s, Test Video B for 10 s, pause for 1 s, Reference Video A* for 10 s, pause for 1 s, Test Video B* for 10 s, pause for 1 s, and Vote 1 for 5 s, with total duration 49 s and a voting scale from bad to excellent.*

The assessor first sees an unimpaired reference, then the same image impaired. The assessor then
votes on the second presentation while keeping the first in mind. Images or sequences are shown in
random order and with random impairments covering all required combinations.

#### Pairwise Comparison

In the **pairwise comparison (PC)** method, two video sequences are shown one after another, and the
observer selects the sequence with higher quality.

Example task: for video quality ranking among $n$ sequences, "You will see two video sequences one
after another. Select the sequence of higher quality."

Figure 12: Two side-by-side images of a corridor with a fire extinguisher, used as an A/B comparison between an original version and a compressed or modified version.

Results are stored in a **comparison matrix**:

$$
C =
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 3 & 1 \\
3 & 0 & 1 \\
1 & 3 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
\quad
\begin{array}{l}
C1 \\
C2 \\
C3
\end{array}
$$

The entry $C_{ij} = n$ means that condition $C_j$ was preferred over $C_i$ exactly $n$ times.

### Processing Subjective Scores

During subjective experiments:

- different observers give different scores;
- some artefacts are considered more annoying;
- preferences are not always consistent;
- even identical videos can produce different votes.

Therefore, subjective quality assessment requires statistical processing.

> [!Important] Mean Opinion Score
> **Statement:** Measured scores are considered outcomes of a random process. If $N$ observers rate
> an image with scores $x_i$, the **Mean Opinion Score (MOS)** is:
>
> $$
> MOS = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i
> $$
>
> **Interpretation:** MOS estimates the average perceived quality, but it does not describe
> disagreement, uncertainty, or statistical reliability by itself.

![[Pasted image 20260515112817.png]]
*Figure 21: Histogram for $N = 30$ observers with estimated normal distribution and estimated mean. The x-axis is the score from 0 to 100, and the Gaussian model is $\frac{1}{s\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{(x-m)^2}{2s^2}}$.*

Assuming the measured distribution is normal, the mean is estimated as the average of observer
ratings.

Average quality is not enough. The analysis also needs:

- variability;
- confidence;
- statistical reliability.

> [!Important] Standard Deviation and Standard Error
> **Statement:** The **standard deviation** measures observer agreement, while the **standard
> error** measures the uncertainty of the estimated MOS.
>
> $$
> s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i - m)^2}
> $$
>
> $$
> SE = \frac{s}{\sqrt{N}}
> $$
>
> **Interpretation:** More observers do not reduce disagreement among observers, but they do reduce
> the uncertainty of the MOS estimate.

Here, $N$ is the sample size and $m$ is the mean.
![[Pasted image 20260515112908.png]]
*Figure 22: Three histograms for $n = 5$, $n = 50$, and $n = 1000$, showing the population distribution in green and the distribution of the mean in red.*

More observers:

- do **not** reduce disagreement, so the same $s$ remains;
- do reduce uncertainty on the MOS estimate.

> [!Important] Confidence Interval
> **Statement:** Once the uncertainty of the MOS estimate is known, a confidence interval can be
> computed. If the same experiment is repeated with the same number of observers, in 95% of cases
> the average value is expected to fall within the confidence interval.
>
> $$
> ci = [-1.96 \cdot SE;\; 1.96 \cdot SE]
> $$
>
> **Interpretation:** The confidence interval expresses how reliable the MOS estimate is, given the
> number of observers and the variability of their scores.

![[Pasted image 20260515113323.png]]
*Figure 23: Three histograms for $n = 5$, $n = 50$, and $n = 1000$, with the confidence region highlighted in red.*

### Comparison of Subjective Methods

| Method | Reference visible? | Speed | Accuracy | Typical use cases |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ACR (Absolute Category Rating) | No | High | Medium | Large-scale streaming evaluation |
| ACR-HR | Hidden reference | High | High | Compression quality studies |
| DSIS (Double Stimulus Impairment Scale) | Yes | Medium | Very High | Codec validation |
| PC (Pairwise Comparison) | Relative comparison | Low | Excellent | Fine perceptual ranking |

The trade-off is:

- fast methodologies scale better;
- more accurate methodologies require more time and attention;
- no single methodology is optimal in every scenario.

![[Pasted image 20260515113344.png]]
*Figure 24: Decision flowchart for selecting subjective assessment methods. If very accurate comparison is needed, choose Pairwise Comparison. Otherwise, if a reference video is available, choose DSIS or ACR-HR. If fast large-scale testing is needed, choose ACR. Otherwise, use hybrid approaches.*

The choice of methodology depends on:

- accuracy requirements;
- testing time;
- observer fatigue;
- availability of a reference signal.

An important issue is the difference between methods that use explicit references, such as DSIS,
and methods that do not use any explicit reference, such as ACR, ACR-HR, and PC. Methods without an
explicit reference do not test fidelity.

The **DSIS** method should be used when testing the fidelity of transmission with respect to the
source signal.

- DSIS has long been a key method specified in ITU-R BT.500-14 for the assessment of TV pictures
  whose typical quality represents the extreme high levels of videotelephony and videoconferencing.
- The specific comments of the DSIS scale, such as imperceptible or perceptible, are valuable when
  the viewer's detection of impairment is important.

**ACR** is easy and fast to implement, and the presentation of stimuli resembles ordinary system
use. Therefore, ACR is well suited for qualification tests.

**ACR-HR** has all the advantages of ACR in terms of presentation and speed. Its main advantage over
ACR is that the perceptual impact of the reference video can be removed from subjective scores.
This reduces the influence of:

- **scene bias**, such as viewers liking or disliking a reference video;
- **reference video quality**, such as small camera-quality differences;
- **monitor** characteristics, such as professional versus consumer-grade displays.

ACR-HR is well suited to large experiments, provided that all reference videos are at least of
"good" quality. However, ACR-HR may be insensitive to impairments that are easily detected by direct
differential methods such as DSIS.

The main advantage of **PC** is its **high discriminatory power**, which is valuable when several
test items are nearly equal in quality. If many items must be evaluated in one test, PC becomes
lengthy. In that case, an ACR or DSIS test may first be carried out with a limited number of
observers, followed by a PC test only on items that received similar ratings.

## Objective Quality Evaluation

Objective quality assessment algorithms predict visual quality by comparing a distorted signal with
a reference, typically by modeling the human visual system.

Objective image quality assessment is based on mathematical models that predict perceived quality
between a distorted image and a reference image. These methods consider characteristics of the
human visual system to incorporate perceptual aspects in the quality measures. Modern methods
increasingly rely on machine learning and large subjective datasets.

### Perceptual Effects of the Human Visual System

Perceived brightness is not a simple function of light intensity.

In the **Mach band** pattern, the visual system tends to undershoot or overshoot around the
boundaries of regions with different intensities.

![[Pasted image 20260515114134.png]]
*Figure 27: Grayscale Mach band gradient comparing actual stepped illumination with perceived brightness, including overshoot and undershoot at transitions.*

In **simultaneous contrast**, a region's perceived brightness does not depend only on its intensity;
it also depends on background intensity.

![[Pasted image 20260515114152.png]]
*Figure 28: Three identical gray squares on black, dark gray, and light gray backgrounds, where the same square appears lighter on a dark background and darker on a light background.*

Dynamic luminance effects show that the HVS can fill in non-existing information or wrongly
perceive geometrical properties of objects.

![[Pasted image 20260515114212.png]]
*Figure 29: Rotating optical illusion made of black-and-white spiral sectors that appear to rotate.*

![[Pasted image 20260515114232.png]]
*Figure 30: Hermann grid illusion, showing a black grid with bright corridors where dark illusory points appear at intersections.*

![[Pasted image 20260515114249.png]]
*Figure 31: Anomalous motion illusion with a circular colored pattern whose blue, yellow, and green elements appear to rotate.*

Objective algorithms, or **metrics**, are desired when they estimate subjective MOS with high
correlation.

Examples include:

- full-reference metrics: **PSNR**, **SSIM**, **VMAF**;
- no-reference metrics: **NIQE**, **BRISQUE**, **CLIP-IQA**;
- reduced-reference metrics: **RRED**.

### Full Reduced and No Reference Scenarios

![[Pasted image 20260515114324.png]]
*Figure 33: Full-reference block diagram where an input/reference signal passes through signal processing to produce an output/processed signal, and both the reference and output feed an FR metric.*

Objective metrics are categorized by reference availability:

- **Full-reference (FR)**: the original signal is available.
- **Reduced-reference (RR)**: partial information about the original signal is available.
- **No-reference (NR)**: the original signal is not available.

### Full-Reference Notation

Full-reference methods depend on both the reference, or original, image and the test, or compressed,
image.

Notation:

- $I(n,m,c,t)$ is the intensity of component $c$ at position $(n,m)$ and time $t$ in the reference
  signal.
- $\hat{I}(n,m,c,t)$ is the corresponding value in the test signal.
- The color space is typically YCbCr, although RGB can be used in some cases.

### MSE and PSNR

> [!Important] Mean Square Error
> **Statement:** For a single image at time $t$ in a video sequence, the luminance-component
> **mean square error** is:
>
> $$
> \text{MSE}_Y(t) = \frac{1}{NM} \sum_{n,m}
> \left(I(n,m,1,t) - \hat{I}(n,m,1,t)\right)^2
> $$
>
> **Interpretation:** MSE measures pixel-wise squared error, but it does not model perceptual
> relevance.

The logarithmic version of MSE is **PSNR**:

> [!Important] Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio
> **Statement:** The luminance PSNR at time $t$ is:
>
> $$
> \text{PSNR}_Y(t) =
> 10\log_{10} \frac{255^2}{\text{MSE}_Y(t)}
> \approx 48dB - 10\log_{10} \text{MSE}_Y(t)
> $$
>
> **Interpretation:** PSNR is easy to compute and widely used, but it measures signal fidelity more
> than perceived quality.

Typical PSNR interpretation:

- $\text{PSNR} > 45$ dB: excellent quality.
- 40 to 45 dB: very good quality.
- 30 to 40 dB: from good to very good quality.
- below 30 dB: typically poor quality.

For chroma components, one can similarly define $\text{MSE}_{Cb}(t)$, $\text{MSE}_{Cr}(t)$,
$\text{PSNR}_{Cb}(t)$, and $\text{PSNR}_{Cr}(t)$.

For example:

$$
\text{MSE}_{Cb}(t) =
\frac{1}{\frac{N}{2}\frac{M}{2}}
\sum_{n,m} \left(I(n,m,2,t) - \hat{I}(n,m,2,t)\right)^2
$$

A global quality measure for color images is:

$$
\text{PSNR}_{YCbCr}(t) =
\frac{3}{4}\text{PSNR}_Y(t)
+ \frac{1}{8}\text{PSNR}_{Cb}(t)
+ \frac{1}{8}\text{PSNR}_{Cr}(t)
$$

The smaller chroma weights reflect the lesser impact of color components on human perception.

For a video sequence, PSNR is averaged across the $T$ individual images:

$$
\text{PSNR}_Y = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \text{PSNR}_Y(t)
$$

The same averaging applies to $\text{PSNR}_{Cb}$, $\text{PSNR}_{Cr}$, and
$\text{PSNR}_{YCbCr}$.

![[Pasted image 20260515114654.png]]

### Bjontegaard Metrics

Video codecs produce rate-distortion curves. A single-measure comparison can be obtained with
**Bjontegaard metrics**.

1. **Bjontegaard Delta Rate (BD-Rate)** is the average rate difference at the same quality,
   expressed as a percentage. It is computed as the red area divided by the amplitude of the common
   PSNR interval.
2. **Bjontegaard Delta PSNR (BD-PSNR)** is the average PSNR difference at the same rate, expressed
   in dB. It is computed as the blue area divided by the amplitude of the common rate interval.

![[Pasted image 20260515114717.png]]
![[Pasted image 20260515114740.png]]

*Figure 35: Rate-distortion curves for M-JP2K, MPEG-2, and H.264, with PSNR in dB plotted against rate in kbps and red and blue areas indicating BD-Rate and BD-PSNR computations.*

In practice, BD metrics are computed as follows:

1. For each curve, a small number of points, usually 4 or 5, is considered.
2. These points are fitted into a parametric representation of the curve, using a third-degree
   polynomial in the variable $t = \log R$.
3. The area is computed from the parametric representation of the two curves.

### Why PSNR Is Not Enough

PSNR measures pixel fidelity, not perceptual quality.

Important limitations:

- all pixels are treated equally;
- human visual perception is ignored;
- different artefacts may produce similar PSNR;
- temporal perception is poorly modeled.

> [!Important] PSNR Limitation
> **Statement:** Similar PSNR does not imply similar perceived quality.
>
> **Interpretation:** Two videos or images may have nearly identical pixel errors but very different
> visual annoyance, such as blur versus low frame rate in the subjective test.

### SSIM Structural Similarity Index

In 2004, the **Structural Similarity Index (SSIM)** was introduced as a new approach for image
quality assessment.

- SSIM is a full-reference metric.
- Its value lies in the interval $[0,1]$, from bad to good.
- SSIM improves on traditional metrics such as PSNR and MSE, which are often inconsistent with
  human perception.
- SSIM is based on the human visual system.
![[Pasted image 20260515115320.png]]
*Figure 37: Reference "Barbara" image and three versions with additive Gaussian noise of increasing variance, 64, 200, and 600, with PSNR values 30.05 dB, 25.15 dB, and 20.42 dB, showing that PSNR decreases reliably when the same degradation increases.*

![[Pasted image 20260515115335.png]]
*Figure 38: Reference "Barbara" image and three distorted versions with similar PSNR values, 20.42 dB, 20.42 dB, and 20.76 dB, corresponding to additive Gaussian noise, blur, and additive Gaussian noise in high frequencies, showing very different visual perception despite similar PSNR.*

Modern objective metrics attempt to model:

- human visual perception;
- structural similarity;
- temporal sensitivity;
- perceptual importance.

The evolution is:

$$
\text{MSE} \rightarrow \text{PSNR} \rightarrow \text{SSIM} \rightarrow \text{VMAF}
\rightarrow \text{AI-based metrics}
$$

Modern metrics try to estimate **QoE**, not only signal fidelity.

The main idea of SSIM is that the human visual system is highly sensitive to:

- structures;
- edges;
- local contrast.

Instead of pure pixel error, SSIM compares:

- luminance;
- contrast;
- structural information.

| SSIM | Quality |
|---|---|
| 1.0 | Identical |
| > 0.95 | Very high |
| 0.8 to 0.9 | Noticeable degradation |

> [!Important] Structural Similarity Index
> **Statement:** SSIM is defined as:
>
> $$
> SSIM(x,y) = [l(x,y)]^\alpha [c(x,y)]^\beta [s(x,y)]^\gamma
> $$
>
> **Interpretation:** SSIM combines luminance, contrast, and structure comparisons, with parameters
> $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$ controlling their relative importance.

The components are:

- $l(x,y)$ accounts for luminance change and is qualitatively consistent with Weber's law, which
  models light adaptation in the HVS.
- $c(x,y)$ accounts for contrast change and is consistent with contrast masking in the HVS.
- $s(x,y)$ is the structure comparison function.

### VMAF

**Video Multi-Method Assessment Fusion (VMAF)** is a full-reference video metric proposed by
Netflix in June 2016.

VMAF combines multiple features:

- detail preservation;
- structural similarity;
- motion information;
- perceptual learning.

VMAF values span from 0 to 100, where 0 is worst and 100 is best. A VMAF value of 93 is a good
target, and content with an average over 90 usually looks good.

![[Pasted image 20260515115553.png]]

The model is trained using subjective data.*

### AI-Based Quality Metrics

Modern quality metrics use:

- deep neural networks;
- learned perceptual representations;
- large subjective datasets.

Examples include:

- **LPIPS**;
- **DISTS**;
- learned no-reference metrics.

AI-based metrics can:

- model complex artefacts;
- predict perceptual annoyance;
- estimate quality without reference videos.

The bottom takeaway is that quality assessment is increasingly data-driven.

### Reduced-Reference Metrics

![[Pasted image 20260515115621.png]]
*Figure 45: Reduced-reference block diagram where the input/reference signal goes through signal processing to produce an output/processed signal, while features extracted from the reference and the processed output feed an RR metric.*

The key idea of **Reduced-Reference (RR)** metrics is that only partial information about the
reference signal is available.

Typical transmitted features include:

- edge statistics;
- frequency coefficients;
- texture descriptors;
- motion information.

Advantages:

- lower bandwidth requirements;
- suitability for network monitoring;
- practicality for large-scale systems.

Limitations:

- RR metrics are less accurate than full-reference metrics;
- RR metrics require side-information transmission.

Typical applications include IPTV monitoring, adaptive streaming, and network QoE estimation.

### No-Reference Metrics
![[Pasted image 20260515115703.png]]
*Figure 46: No-reference block diagram where an input/reference signal passes through signal processing to produce an output/processed signal, and only the output/processed signal feeds an NR metric.*

The key idea of **No-Reference (NR)** metrics is that quality is estimated without access to the
original signal.

NR metrics are important because, in many real systems:

- the reference video is unavailable;
- only the received content can be analyzed.

Typical scenarios include:

- social media platforms;
- smartphone cameras;
- user-generated content;
- video surveillance;
- streaming analytics.

The main challenge is distinguishing:

- original scene characteristics;
- distortion artefacts.

NR quality assessment is one of the most challenging problems in multimedia systems.

Examples of no-reference metrics:

| Metric | Main idea |
|---|---|
| BRISQUE | Natural scene statistics |
| NIQE | Deviation from natural image statistics |
| PIQE | Block-wise distortion estimation |
| Deep NR Metrics | Learned perceptual prediction |

Modern AI-based no-reference quality assessment uses:

- convolutional neural networks;
- transformers;
- perceptual embeddings;
- multimodal learning.

Goals:

- predict MOS directly;
- estimate perceptual annoyance;
- operate in real time.

Current trends:

- NR video quality for streaming;
- mobile QoE prediction;
- generative AI quality assessment;
- perceptual metrics for AI-generated media.

AI is transforming quality assessment from signal analysis to perceptual prediction.

## Subjective Versus Objective Assessment

Full-reference, reduced-reference, and no-reference methods can be compared as follows:

| Type | Reference availability | Accuracy | Complexity | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FR | Full reference | Very High | Medium | Codec evaluation |
| RR | Partial reference | Medium | Medium | Network monitoring |
| NR | No reference | Lower / evolving | High | Real-time QoE |

Subjective and objective assessment have complementary properties:

| Subjective | Objective |
|---|---|
| **Accurate** | **Fast** |
| **Human perception** | **Automatic** |
| **Expensive** | **Scalable** |
| **Time-consuming** | **Real-time capable** |
| **Ground truth** | **Approximation** |

## Final Takeaways

The main content of the lecture is that multimedia quality is ultimately about human perception.

- **QoE** is fundamentally subjective.
- Human observers do not always agree.
- MOS requires statistical processing.
- PSNR alone is insufficient.
- Different artefacts affect perception differently.
- Modern systems optimize perceptual quality.
- AI-based metrics are becoming increasingly important.

> Multimedia quality is ultimately about human perception.
