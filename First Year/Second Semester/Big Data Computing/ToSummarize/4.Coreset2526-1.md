## Slide 14 — Metric Space

Typically, the input of a clustering problem consists of a set of points from a metric space

> **Definition**
>
> A **metric space** is an ordered pair $(M, d)$ where $M$ is a set and $d(\cdot)$ is a **metric** on $M$, i.e., a function
>
> $$d : M \times M \to \mathbb{R}$$
>
> such that for every $x, y, z \in M$ the following holds
>
> - $d(x, y) \geq 0$;
> - $d(x, y) = 0$ if and only if $x = y$;
> - $d(x, y) = d(y, x)$; (symmetry)
> - $d(x, z) \leq d(x, y) + d(y, z)$; (triangle inequality)

**Remark:** *The choice of the function may have a significant impact on the effectiveness of the cluster analysis.*

---

## Slide 16 — Distance functions

**Minkowski distances.** Let $X, Y \in \mathbb{R}^n$, with $X = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)$ and $Y = (y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_n)$. For $r > 0$ the $L_r$-distance between $X$ and $Y$ (a.k.a. $L_r$-norm)

$$d_{L_r}(X, Y) = \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i - y_i|^r\right)^{1/r}.$$

- $r = 2$ (*Euclidean distance*): standard distance in $\mathbb{R}^n$ (also denoted by $||\cdot||$)
- $r = 1$ (*Manhattan distance*): used in grid-like environments
- $r = \infty$ (*Chebyshev distance*): maximum absolute differences of coordinates (i.e., the limit for $r$ that tends to $\infty$).

## Slide 19 — Distance functions — Angular distance

**Angular distance.** It is used when points are *vectors* in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Given $X = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)$ and $Y = (y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_n)$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, their angular distance is the angle between them, that is,

$$d_{\text{angular}}(X, Y) = \arccos\!\left(\frac{X \cdot Y}{||X|| \cdot ||Y||}\right) = \arccos\!\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i y_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i)^2}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i)^2}}\right) \in [0, \pi]$$

**Example:** For $X = (1, 2, -1)$ and $Y = (2, 1, 1)$

$$d_{\text{angular}}(X, Y) = \arccos\!\left(\frac{3}{6}\right) = \arccos\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{\pi}{3}$$

**Obs:** In [LRU14] it is called *cosine distance*, but in the literature the term has a different meaning.

---

## Slide 20 — Distance functions — Angular distance (cont.)

For non-negative coordinates, $d_{\text{angular}}(X, Y) \in [0, \pi/2]$, while for arbitrary coordinates the range is $[0, \pi]$.

_[Figura: vettori in $\mathbb{R}^n$ con angoli $\theta$ e $\theta'$; annotazione: "$\theta \in [0, \pi/2]$ (coordinates $\geq 0$)", "$\theta' \in [0, \pi]$ (arbitrary coordinates)".]_

To normalize values in $[0, 1]$, $d_{\text{angular}}(X, Y)$ is often divided by $\pi$ or $\pi/2$ depending on whether vectors have arbitrary or non-negative coordinates. Also, to satisfy the second property of metric spaces, scalar multiples of a vector are regarded as the same vector.

**Remark.** *This distance is used in information retrieval for documents. A document $X$ over a vocabulary of $n$ words can be represented as an $n$-vector, where $x_i$ is the number of occurrences of the $i$-th word of the vocabulary in $X$.*

---

## Slide 21 — Distance functions — Hamming distance

- **Hamming distance.** Used when points are *binary vectors* over some $n$-dimensional space: $X, Y \in \{0, 1\}^n$. The Hamming distance between two vectors is the number of coordinates in which they differ.

$$d_{\text{Hamming}}(X, Y) = |\{i | x_i \neq y_i\}|$$

Example: for $X = (\underline{0}, 1, 1, \underline{0}, 1)$ and $Y = (1, 1, 1, 0, \underline{0})$

$$d_{\text{Hamming}}(X, Y) = \mathbf{2}$$

**Observation:** $d_{\text{Hamming}}(X, Y) = d_{L1}(X, Y)$

---

## Slide 22 — Distance functions — Jaccard distance

- **Jaccard distance.** Used when points are *sets* (e.g., documents seen as bags of words). Let $S$ and $T$ be two sets over the same ground set of elements. The Jaccard distance between $S$ and $T$ is

$$d_{\text{Jaccard}}(S, T) = 1 - \frac{|S \cap T|}{|S \cup T|} = \frac{|S \cup T| - |S \cap T|}{|S \cup T|}.$$

Note that the distance ranges in $[0, 1]$, and it is 0 iff $S = T$ and 1 iff $S$ and $T$ are disjoint. The value $|S \cap T|/|S \cup T|$ is referred to as the *Jaccard similarity* of the two sets.

Example:

_[Figura: diagramma di Venn con insiemi $S$ e $T$ che si sovrappongono. $S$ ha 6 punti verdi, $T$ ha 12 punti in totale di cui 6 rossi nella parte comune. Annotazione: $d_{\text{Jaccard}}(S,T) = 1 - 6/18 = 2/3$.]_

## Slide 23 — Which distance should we use?

**Minkowski and Angular distances** are used when an object is characterized by the numerical values of its features (e.g., frequency).

- **Minkowski distance:** it aggregates the gap in each dimension between two objects. Examples: GPS coordinates; measurements of physical/chemical processes.

- **Angular distance:** it measures the ratios among features' values, rather than their absolute values. Example: distance between documents of different lengths, where each document is characterized by the relative frequency of words from a given vocabulary.

---

## Slide 24 — Which distance should we use? (cont.)

**Hamming and Jaccard distances** are used when an object is characterized by having or not having some features (no multiplicity).

- **Hamming distance:** it measures the total number of differences between two objects. Examples: fixed-length binary words, genetic distance.

- **Jaccard distance:** it measures the ratio between the number of differences between two sets and the total number of points in the sets. Example: the topics describing a document.

**Observation:** All of the above distances satisfy the four requirements for a metric space.

## Slide 25 — Types of clusterings

Given a set of points in a metric space, a clustering problem often specifies an objective function to optimize.

The objective function also allows to compare different solutions.

Clustering problems can be categorized based on whether or not

- A target number $k$ of clusters is given in input.
- For each cluster a center must be identified.
- Disjoint clusters are sought.

---

## Slide 26 — Background: optimization problem

> **Combinatorial optimization problem**
>
> A (COMBINATORIAL) OPTIMIZATION PROBLEM is defined by
>
> - A set $\mathcal{I}$ of **instances** (i.e., possible inputs)
> - A set $\mathcal{S}$ of **solutions** (i.e., possible outputs)
> - For each $i \in I$, a set $\mathcal{S}_i \subseteq \mathcal{S}$ of **feasible solutions**
> - **objective function** $\Phi : \mathcal{S} \to \mathbb{R}$
>
> Given $i \in I$ the problem requires to find an OPTIMAL SOLUTION, i.e., a feasible solution $s^* \in \mathcal{S}_i$ such that
>
> - $\Phi(s^*) = \min\{\Phi(s) : s \in \mathcal{S}_i\}$ &emsp; (*minimization problem*)
>
> or
>
> - $\Phi(s^*) = \max\{\Phi(s) : s \in \mathcal{S}_i\}$ &emsp; (*maximization problem*)
>
> Obs. Technically, if $\mathcal{S}_i$ is not finite, min and max become inf and sup.

## Slide 28 — Background: approximation algorithm

> **Approximation algorithm**
>
> Let $\Pi$ be an optimization problem. For $c \geq 1$, a $c$-approximation algorithm $A$ for $\Pi$ is an algorithm that for each instance $i \in \mathcal{I}$ returns a feasible solution $A(i) \in \mathcal{S}_i$ such that
>
> - $\Phi(A(i)) \leq c \cdot \min\{\Phi(s) : s \in \mathcal{S}_i\}$ &emsp; (*minimization problem*)
>
> or
>
> - $\Phi(A(i)) \geq (1/c) \cdot \max\{\Phi(s) : s \in \mathcal{S}_i\}$ &emsp; (*maximization problem*)
>
> We refer to $c$ as **approximation ratio**, and to $A(i)$ as **$c$-approximate solution** for $i$, or $c$-approximation of the optimal solution for $i$.

---

## Slide 29 — Observations

Approximation algorithms play a **crucial role** in big data computing for the following reasons

- For several important optimization problems finding optimal solutions for large inputs is very time consuming. E.g.,
  - NP-hard problems for which it is likely that exponential time is required.
  - Problems for which polynomial-time algorithms exist but require superlinear number of operations (e.g., quadratic, cubic).
- Allowing for approximations widens the spectrum of possible solutions and makes it easier to find one.
- In real-world instances, often affected by noise or uncertainty, the true optimum may not be well defined.

## Slide 30 — Center-based clustering

Let $P$ be a set of $N$ points in metric space $(M, d)$, and let $k$ be the target number of clusters, $1 \leq k \leq N$.

A **$k$-clustering** of $P$ is a tuple $\mathcal{C} = (C_1, C_2, \ldots, C_k; S)$ which comprises

- A partition of $P = C_1 \cup C_2 \cup \cdots \cup C_k$ into $k$ clusters, with $C_i \cap C_j = \emptyset$, $\forall i, j$
- A set $S = \{c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_k\}$ of $k$ **centers**, where $c_i \in C_i$ is the center of cluster $C_i$, for $1 \leq i \leq k$.

**Remark:** in the above definition, centers are points of $P$. In some cases (e.g., for Euclidean spaces), centers outside $P$ may be allowed.

## Slide 32 — Popular center-based clustering problems

There are 3 popular problems that require to determine center-based clusterings for a given input $P$, which optimize one of 3 objectives.

Specifically:

- **k-center clustering:** aims at *minimizing the maximum distance of any point from the center of its cluster*

- **k-means clustering:** aims at *minimizing the sum of the squared distances of the points from the centers of their respective clusters* (a.k.a. Sum of Squared Errors (SSE))

- **k-median clustering:** aims at *minimizing the sum of the distances of the points from the centers of their respective clusters*

**Observation:** for k-means and k-median, minimizing the sum of (squared) distances is equivalent to minimizing the average (squared) distance.

## Slide 35 — Formal definitions of the problems

For any point $x \in P$ and any subset $S \subseteq P$ we define

$$d(x, S) = \min_{y \in S} d(x, y)$$

i.e., the distance between $x$ and the closest point of $S$

> **Definition**
>
> Let $(M, d)$ be a metric space. The **k-center/k-means/k-median clustering** problems are optimization problems that, given a finite pointset $P \subseteq M$ and an integer $k \leq |P|$, require to return the subset $S \subseteq P$ of $k$ centers which minimizes the following respective objective functions:
>
> - $\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S) = \max_{x \in P} d(x, S)$ &emsp; (k-center clustering)
> - $\Phi_{\text{kmeans}}(P, S) = \sum_{x \in P} (d(x, S))^2$ &emsp; (k-means clustering).
> - $\Phi_{\text{kmedian}}(P, S) = \sum_{x \in P} (d(x, S))$ &emsp; (k-median clustering).

---

## Slide 36 — Terminology and notations

For the k-center/means/median clustering problems we have that:

INSTANCE $(P, k)$

FEASIBLE SOLUTIONS: All subsets $S \subseteq P$ of size $k$

OPTIMAL SOLUTION: $S^*$ (i.e., the feasible solution which minimizes the appropriate objective function)

We also define:

- $\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k) = \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S^*)$.

- $\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kmeans}}(P, k) = \Phi_{\text{kmeans}}(P, S^*)$.

- $\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kmedian}}(P, k) = \Phi_{\text{kmedian}}(P, S^*)$.

Why do we require only the centers as output?

Can a suitable partition be recovered from the centers?

## Slide 37 — Answer to the above questions (scritta a mano)

ANSWER TO THE ABOVE QUESTIONS: Given an instance $(P, k)$ and a set $S$ of $k$ centers (solution) the "best" $k$-clustering of $P$ around the $k$ centers of $S$ is obtained by assigning each point to the cluster associated with the closest center.

The assignment can be computed efficiently both in the sequential and distributed (e.g., MapReduce) settings

## Slide 44 — Farthest-First Traversal: algorithm

- Popular 2-approximation sequential algorithm developed by T.F. Gonzalez [Theoretical Computer Science, 38:293-306, 1985]
- Simple, fast implementation if input fits in main memory.
- Powerful primitive for extracting samples for further analyses

**Input** Set $P$ of $N$ points from a metric space $(M, d)$, integer $k \geq 1$

**Output** A set $S \subseteq P$ of $k$ centers which is a good solution to the k-center problem on $P$ (i.e., $\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S)$ "close" to $\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k)$)

---

## Slide 45 — Farthest-First Traversal: algorithm (pseudocodice)

```
S ← {c₁}  // c₁ ∈ P arbitrary point
for i ← 2 to k do
    Find the point cᵢ ∈ P − S that maximizes d(cᵢ, S)
    S ← S ∪ {cᵢ}
return S
```

*(annotazione a mano):* Does it resemble an algorithm that you have already seen?

**Observation:** *The assignment of each point to the closest center can be easily maintained in every iteration of the for-loop.*

## Slide 49 — Farthest-First Traversal: analysis

> **Theorem**
>
> Let $S$ be the set of centers returned by running Farthest-First Traversal on $P$. Then:
>
> $$\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S) \leq 2 \cdot \Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k).$$
>
> That is, Farthest-First Traversal is a 2-approximation algorithm.

*(annotazioni a mano):*

radius of the $k$-clustering induced by $S$ $\qquad$ radius of the optimal $k$-clustering

## Slide 50 — Proof of theorem — setup (scritta a mano)

$S = \{c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_k\}$ $\quad$ $c_i$ = $i$-th center selected by FFT

Recall that $\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S) = \max_{x \in P} d(x, S)$

Define $q \in P$ as the point such that

$$\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S) = d(q, S)$$

Consider the set $\{c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_k, c_{k+1} = q\}$ $\quad$ $k+1$ points!

CLAIM: $\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S) = d(q, S) \leq d(c_i, c_j)$ $\forall 1 \leq i < j \leq k+1$

We will prove the claim later on

---

## Slide 51 — Proof — figura (scritta a mano)

_[Figura: i cinque cluster con i centri $c_1, \ldots, c_5$ e il punto $q$ (aggiunto). Linee tratteggiate verdi collegano tutte le coppie di centri e $q$. Annotazioni:]_

- $q$ is the most distant point from its center
- any two points in $\{c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4, c_5, q\}$ are at distance $\geq d(q, \{c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4, c_5\}) = d(q, c_2)$

---

## Slide 52 — Proof — soluzione ottimale (scritta a mano)

Let $S^* = \{c_1^*, c_2^*, \ldots, c_k^*\}$ be the OPTIMAL SOLUTION

$\Rightarrow$

* $\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k) = \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S^*) \leq \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S)$

* $\forall x \in P \quad d(x, S^*) \leq \Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k)$

Define

$$C_t^* = \{x \in P : c_t^* \text{ is the closest out of } S^* \text{ to } x\}$$

$\Rightarrow P = C_1^* \cup C_2^* \cup \cdots \cup C_k^*$ $\quad$ break ties arbitrarily

$=$ optimal $k$-clustering of $P$

---

## Slide 53 — Proof — Pigeonhole (scritta a mano)

By PIGEONHOLE PRINCIPLE there must exist 2 points of $\{c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_k, c_{k+1} = q\}$ which belong to the same optimal cluster $C_t^*$, say $c_a, c_b$ with $a < b$

_[Figura: cluster ottimale $C_t^*$ con punto $c_t^*$ al centro e due punti $c_a$, $c_b$ all'interno. Distanze da $c_a$ e $c_b$ al centro $c_t^*$ annotate come $\leq \Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}$.]_

By Triangle inequality

$$d(c_a, c_b) \leq d(c_a, c_t^*) + d(c_t^*, c_b) \leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k)$$

---

## Slide 54 — Proof — conclusione (scritta a mano)

Therefore

$$\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S) = d(q, S) \quad \text{by the choice of } q$$
$$\leq d(c_a, c_b) \quad \text{by the CLAIM}$$
$$\leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k)$$

At this point we only need to prove that the CLAIM holds, that is

$$d(q, S) \leq d(c_i, c_j) \quad 1 \leq i < j \leq k+1$$

Recall: $S = \{c_1, \ldots, c_k\}$ and $q = c_{k+1}$

---

## Slide 55 — Proof of the Claim (scritta a mano)

Fix $i < j$ arbitrarily ($1 \leq i < j \leq k+1$)

$$d(q, S) = d\!\left(q, \{c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_k\}\right)$$
$$\leq d\!\left(q, \{c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_{j-1}\}\right) \quad \text{since } j \leq k$$
$$\leq d\!\left(c_j, \{c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_{j-1}\}\right) \quad \text{by the selection protocol used by FFT}$$
$$\leq d(c_j, c_i) \quad \text{since } i < j \Rightarrow c_i \in \{c_1, \ldots, c_{j-1}\}$$

$\Rightarrow$ CLAIM holds $\Rightarrow$ Theorem holds $\quad \square$

## Slide 66 — MapReduce-Farthest-First Traversal

Let $P$ be a set of $N$ points ($N$ large!) from a metric space $(M, d)$, and let $k > 1$ be an integer.

**Algorithm MR-Farthest-First Traversal**

- **Round 1:**
  - Map Phase: Partition $P$ arbitrarily into $\ell$ subsets of equal size $P_1, P_2, \ldots, P_\ell$.
  - Reduce Phase: for every $i \in [1, \ell]$ separately, run Farthest-First Traversal on $P_i$ to determine a set $T_i \subseteq P_i$ of $k$ centers.

- **Round 2:**
  - Map Phase: empty.
  - Reduce Phase: gather the coreset $T = \cup_{i=1}^\ell T_i$ (of size $\ell \cdot k$) and return a set $S = \{c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_k\}$ of $k$ centers, computed by running Farthest-First Traversal on $T$ using a single reducer.

## Slide 67 — Analysis of MR-Farthest-First Traversal (scritta a mano)

> **Theorem**
>
> The 2-round MR-Farthest-First Traversal algorithm can be implemented using local space $M_L = O\!\left(\sqrt{Nk}\right)$ and aggregate space $M_A = O(N)$.

Proof: Assume an even partitioning in the MapPhase of Round 1

$M_L$

Round 1: Map Ph.: $O(1)$

Reduce Ph.: $O(N/\ell)$ since each partition has size $O(N/\ell)$ and FFT requires linear space in its input size

$\Rightarrow M_L = O(\max\{N/\ell,\ \ell \cdot k\})$

## Slide 70 — Coreset quality lemma

Let $T$ be the union of the coresets $T_i$ computed by MR-Farthest-First Traversal on input $P$. The following lemma establishes the quality of $T$.

> **Lemma**
>
> For every $x \in P$ we have
>
> $$d(x, T) \leq 2 \cdot \Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k).$$

*(annotazione a mano a sottolineare:)* R1

**Observation.** The lemma gives a quantitative characterization of how well the coreset $T$ represents the input $P$, with respect to the $k$-center objective.

---

## Slide 71 — Proof of lemma (scritta a mano)

Proof

Recall that $P = P_1 \cup P_2 \cup \cdots \cup P_\ell$ and that

$T = T_1 \cup T_2 \cup \cdots \cup T_\ell$ with $T_r \leftarrow \text{FFT}(P_r, k)$

For each index $r$, $1 \leq r \leq \ell$ define

$q_r$ = point of $P_r$ farthest from $T_r$

CLAIM: $d(q_r, T_r) \leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k)$

If the CLAIM is true, then we have that $\forall x \in P$ (e.g. say $x \in P_r$, for some index $r$):

$$d(x, T) \leq d(x, T_r) \leq d(q_r, T_r) \leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k)$$

---

## Slide 72 — Proof of the Claim (scritta a mano)

PROOF OF THE CLAIM. Fix $r$ arbitrarily and repeat the same argument used in the analysis of FFT. Let

$S^* = \{c_1^*, c_2^*, \ldots, c_k^*\}$ set of OPTIMAL CENTERS FOR $P$

Define $C_t^*$ = optimal cluster centered at $c_t^*$, $1 \leq t \leq k$

$\Rightarrow P = C_1^* \cup C_2^* \cup \cdots \cup C_k^*$

Let $T_r = \{c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_k\}$ and consider

$\{c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_k, c_{k+1} = q_r\}$

Recall that $q_r$ is the farthest most point of $P_r$ from $T_r$.

Hence, this set contains $k+1$ points of $P_r$, thus $k+1$ points of $P$

---

## Slide 73 — Proof of the Claim — conclusione (scritta a mano)

By repeating the same argument used in the analysis of FFT we can show that

* $d(q_r, T_r) \leq d(c_i, c_j)$ $\quad \forall\ 1 \leq i < j \leq k+1$

* There must exist 2 points $c_a, c_b$ with $1 \leq a < b \leq k+1$ which belong to the same optimal cluster $C_t^*$ of $P$. $\Rightarrow$

$$d(q_r, T_r) \leq d(c_a, c_b)$$
$$\leq d(c_a, c_t^*) + d(c_t^*, c_b)$$
$$\leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k) \quad \square$$

## Slide 76 — Analysis of MR-Farthest-First Traversal — Approximation theorem

> **Theorem**
>
> Let $S$ be the set of $k$ centers returned by running MR-Farthest-First Traversal on $P$. Then:
>
> $$\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S) \leq 4 \cdot \Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k).$$
>
> That is, MR-Farthest-First Traversal is a 4-approximation algorithm.

*(annotazione a mano):* This theorem shows that in order to make the solution of k-center feasible for massive datasets, we must tolerate a slightly worse accuracy (i.e., factor 4 approximation rather than factor 2)

---

## Slide 77 — Proof of theorem (scritta a mano)

Proof of theorem

From the previous lemma we know that

(A) $\forall x \in P\ \exists y \in T$ such that $d(x, y) \leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k)$

Recall that the final solution $S$ is computed by running $\text{FFT}(T, k)$. Let $\bar{y} \in T$ be the point of $T$ which is farthest most from $S$. By using exactly the same argument used to prove the CLAIM in the previous lemma, we can show that

$$d(\bar{y}, S) \leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k) \quad \text{(Exercise)}$$

---

## Slide 78 — Proof of theorem — conclusione (scritta a mano)

Hence

(B) $\forall y \in T\ \exists c \in S : d(y, c) \leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k)$

By combining (A) and (B) we have

_[Figura: schema con tre cerchi concentrici $P \supset T \supset S$. Punto $x$ nel cerchio esterno a distanza $\leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P,k)$ da $y \in T$ (A), e $y$ a distanza $\leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P,k)$ da $c \in S$ (B).]_

$$d(x, S) \leq d(x, c)$$
$$\leq d(x, y) + d(y, c) \quad \text{by triangle ineq.}$$

---

## Slide 79 — Proof — fine (scritta a mano)

$$\leq 4\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k) \quad \text{by (A)+(B)} \quad \square$$

## Slide 81 — Observations on MR-Farthest-First Traversal

- Farthest-First Traversal provides good coresets $T_i$'s, hence a good final coreset $T$ since it ensures that any point not belonging to $T$ is well represented by some coreset point.
- MR-Farthest-First Traversal is able to handle very large pointsets and the the final approximation is not too far from the best achievable one.
- Observe that for any optimization problem, no MR algorithm can attain better accuracy than the best sequential algorithm, since all MR algorithms become sequential algorithms when executed on one processing element.

---

## Slide 82 — Low-dimensional pointsets

When $P$ has low dimensionality the quality of the solution returned by MR-Farthest-First Traversal can be made arbitrarily close to 2 by selecting a slightly larger coreset, while still ensuring sublinear local space and linear aggregate space.

*(annotazione a mano):* If in each partition $P_r$ we use FFT to extract $\bar{k} > k$ centers, the distance of each point $x \in P$ from the closest center in $T$ decreases SHARPLY as $\bar{k}$ increases, especially for low dimensional datasets $\Rightarrow$ the approximation guarantee of MR-FFT gets close to 2, with a moderate increase in the coreset size ($|T|$)
