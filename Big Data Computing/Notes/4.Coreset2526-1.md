## Metric Spaces

> [!Important] Definition — Metric Space
> A **metric space** is an ordered pair $(M, d)$ where $M$ is a set and $d$ is a **metric** on $M$:
> $$d : M \times M \to \mathbb{R}$$
> satisfying for every $x, y, z \in M$:
> 1. $d(x, y) \geq 0$
> 2. $d(x, y) = 0 \iff x = y$
> 3. $d(x, y) = d(y, x)$ (symmetry)
> 4. $d(x, z) \leq d(x, y) + d(y, z)$ (triangle inequality)
>
> **Intuizione:** the choice of $d$ has significant impact on the effectiveness of cluster analysis.

### Triangle Inequality and Big Data

![[Pasted image 20260428111303.png]]
$$d(x,y) - d(y,z) \leq d(x,z) \leq d(x,y) + d(y,z)$$

When $d(y,z)$ is small: $d(x,z) \approx d(x,y)$, meaning $z$ and $y$ are almost indistinguishable w.r.t. their similarity to $x$ → **one of the two can be discarded in a coreset**.

## Distance Functions

### Minkowski Distances

For $X, Y \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the **$L_r$-distance** (Minkowski distance of order $r > 0$):

$$d_{L_r}(X, Y) = \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i - y_i|^r\right)^{1/r}$$

| $r$ | Name | Note |
|-----|------|------|
| 1 | *Manhattan distance* | Grid-like environments |
| 2 | *Euclidean distance* ($\|\cdot\|$) | Standard distance in $\mathbb{R}^n$ |
| $\infty$ | *Chebyshev distance* | $\max_i |x_i - y_i|$ |

> [!Example] Minkowski distances — $A=(2,1)$, $B=(7,5)$
> $$d_{L1}(A,B) = (7-2)+(5-1) = 9$$
> $$d_{L2}(A,B) = \sqrt{5^2+4^2} = 6.403\ldots$$
> $$d_{L\infty}(A,B) = \max(5,4) = 5$$
>

### Angular Distance

Used when points are **vectors** in $\mathbb{R}^n$; measures the angle between them:

$$d_{\text{angular}}(X, Y) = \arccos\!\left(\frac{X \cdot Y}{\|X\| \cdot \|Y\|}\right) = \arccos\!\left(\frac{\sum_{i} x_i y_i}{\sqrt{\sum_i x_i^2}\sqrt{\sum_i y_i^2}}\right)$$

- Non-negative coordinates: $d_{\text{angular}} \in [0, \pi/2]$
- Arbitrary coordinates: $d_{\text{angular}} \in [0, \pi]$
- Often normalized to $[0,1]$ by dividing by $\pi/2$ or $\pi$.
- Scalar multiples of a vector are treated as the same vector.

> [!Example] Angular distance — $X=(1,2,-1)$, $Y=(2,1,1)$
> $$d_{\text{angular}}(X,Y) = \arccos\!\left(\frac{1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 1 + (-1)\cdot 1}{\sqrt{6}\cdot\sqrt{6}}\right) = \arccos\!\left(\frac{3}{6}\right) = \frac{\pi}{3}$$

**Application:** information retrieval — a document over vocabulary of $n$ words is an $n$-vector where $x_i$ = frequency of the $i$-th word. Angular distance measures similarity independent of document length.

### Hamming Distance

Used for **binary vectors** $X, Y \in \{0,1\}^n$:

$$d_{\text{Hamming}}(X, Y) = |\{i \mid x_i \neq y_i\}|$$

**Obs.:** $d_{\text{Hamming}}(X,Y) = d_{L1}(X,Y)$ for binary vectors.

> [!Example] Hamming distance
> $X = (0, 1, 1, 0, 1)$, $Y = (1, 1, 1, 0, 0)$: differences at positions 1 and 5 → $d_{\text{Hamming}} = 2$.

### Jaccard Distance

Used when points are **sets**:

$$d_{\text{Jaccard}}(S, T) = 1 - \frac{|S \cap T|}{|S \cup T|}$$

- Range: $[0,1]$; equals 0 iff $S = T$; equals 1 iff $S \cap T = \emptyset$.
- $\frac{|S \cap T|}{|S \cup T|}$ is the **Jaccard similarity**.
![[Coreset1-007.jpg]]

### Choosing the Right Distance

| Distance | Use when... | Example |
|----------|-------------|---------|
| **Minkowski** | Objects characterized by numerical feature values | GPS coords, physical measurements |
| **Angular** | Ratios among feature values matter, not absolute values | Documents of different lengths |
| **Hamming** | Binary features (have/don't have), fixed-length | Binary words, genetic distance |
| **Jaccard** | Set membership (no multiplicity), ratio of difference to total | Topics describing a document |

All four distances satisfy the metric space axioms.

## Clustering Problems

### Combinatorial Optimization Background

> [!Important] Definition — Combinatorial Optimization Problem
> Defined by:
> - $\mathcal{I}$: set of **instances**
> - $\mathcal{S}$: set of **solutions**
> - $\mathcal{S}_i \subseteq \mathcal{S}$: **feasible solutions** for instance $i$
> - **Objective function** $\Phi : \mathcal{S} \to \mathbb{R}$
>
> Goal: find $s^* \in \mathcal{S}_i$ minimizing (or maximizing) $\Phi$.

![[Coreset1-008.jpg]]

### Approximation Algorithms

> [!Important] Definition — $c$-Approximation Algorithm
> For $c \geq 1$, a **$c$-approximation algorithm** $A$ returns a feasible solution $A(i)$ such that:
> - **Minimization:** $\Phi(A(i)) \leq c \cdot \min\{\Phi(s) : s \in \mathcal{S}_i\}$
> - **Maximization:** $\Phi(A(i)) \geq (1/c) \cdot \max\{\Phi(s) : s \in \mathcal{S}_i\}$
>
> $c$ is the **approximation ratio**; $A(i)$ is a **$c$-approximate solution**.
>
> **Why crucial for big data:**
> - Many clustering problems are NP-hard → exact solutions infeasible.
> - Approximations widen the solution space.
> - Real-world data affected by noise: true optimum may not be well-defined.

### Center-Based Clustering

Let $P$ be a set of $N$ points in metric space $(M, d)$, and $k$ the target number of clusters.

> [!Important] Definition — $k$-Clustering
> A **$k$-clustering** of $P$ is a tuple $\mathcal{C} = (C_1, C_2, \ldots, C_k; S)$ where:
> - $P = C_1 \cup \cdots \cup C_k$ is a partition into $k$ disjoint clusters.
> - $S = \{c_1, \ldots, c_k\}$ is a set of **centers**, $c_i \in C_i$.
>
> *(Note: centers are typically points of $P$; for Euclidean spaces, centers outside $P$ may be allowed.)*

![[Coreset1-009.jpg]]

**Three popular center-based problems:**

| Problem | Objective | Also known as |
|---------|-----------|---------------|
| **k-center** | Minimize *max* distance of any point from its cluster center | Radius minimization |
| **k-means** | Minimize *sum of squared* distances from centers | SSE minimization |
| **k-median** | Minimize *sum* of distances from centers | — |

> **Obs.:** for k-means and k-median, minimizing the *sum* of (squared) distances is equivalent to minimizing the *average* (squared) distance.

### Formal Definitions

For $x \in P$ and $S \subseteq P$: $d(x, S) = \min_{y \in S} d(x, y)$.

> [!Important] Objective Functions
> $$\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S) = \max_{x \in P} d(x, S)$$
> $$\Phi_{\text{kmeans}}(P, S) = \sum_{x \in P} (d(x, S))^2$$
> $$\Phi_{\text{kmedian}}(P, S) = \sum_{x \in P} d(x, S)$$
>
> **Instance:** $(P, k)$. **Feasible solutions:** all $S \subseteq P$ with $|S| = k$.
>
> **Optimal values:** $\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter/kmeans/kmedian}}(P,k) = \Phi(P, S^*)$.

- The **radius** of a clustering = $\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P,S)$ = max distance of any point from its closest center.
![[Coreset1-010.jpg]]
![[Coreset1-011.jpg]]

### Recovering the Partition from Centers

Given $S$, the optimal partition is obtained by assigning each point to its **closest center**:

| Setting | Complexity |
|---------|-----------|
| Sequential | $O(N \cdot k)$ |
| MapReduce | $R=1$, $M_L = O(k)$, $M_A = O(N)$ — $S$ stored as global variable; best center computed per point in Map Phase |

### Observations on Clustering Objectives

- All three problems are **NP-hard** → approximations are necessary.
- **k-center:** guarantees every point is close to a center; sensitive to noise/outliers.
- **k-means/median:** guarantees the *average* point is close to a center; k-means is more sensitive to noise than k-median, but faster approximations exist.
- k-center and k-median are **facility-location problems** (minimize max/avg distance from customer to open facility).

## k-Center Clustering

### Farthest-First Traversal (FFT)

A popular **2-approximation** sequential algorithm (Gonzalez, 1985).

**Input:** Set $P$ of $N$ points, integer $k \geq 1$.  
**Output:** $S \subseteq P$ of $k$ centers, good solution to k-center.

```
S ← {c₁}   // c₁ ∈ P arbitrary
for i ← 2 to k do
    cᵢ ← argmax_{p ∈ P−S} d(p, S)
    S ← S ∪ {cᵢ}
return S
```

- **Time complexity:** $O(N \cdot k)$ (maintain for each point its closest center and distance; update incrementally at each iteration).
- **Practical note:** requires $k-1$ scans of $P$ → impractical for massive $P$ and large $k$.

![[Coreset1-013.jpg]]
![[Coreset1-014.jpg]]
### FFT Analysis — 2-Approximation Theorem

> [!Important] Theorem — FFT is a 2-Approximation
> Let $S$ be the centers returned by FFT on $P$. Then:
> $$\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S) \leq 2 \cdot \Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k)$$
>
> **Dimostrazione:**
>
> Let $S = \{c_1,\ldots,c_k\}$ where $c_i$ is the $i$-th center selected by FFT. Define $q \in P$ as the point maximizing $d(q, S)$, so $\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P,S) = d(q,S)$. Consider $\{c_1, \ldots, c_k, c_{k+1} = q\}$: a set of $k+1$ points.
>
> **Claim:** $d(q, S) \leq d(c_i, c_j)$ for all $1 \leq i < j \leq k+1$.
>
> *Proof of Claim:* Fix $i < j$ arbitrarily. Then:
> $$d(q, S) = d(q,\{c_1,\ldots,c_k\}) \leq d(q, \{c_1,\ldots,c_{j-1}\})$$
> (first: since $j \leq k$, $\{c_1,\ldots,c_{j-1}\} \subseteq S$, so distance to $S$ is $\leq$ distance to subset). Then:
> $$\leq d(c_j, \{c_1,\ldots,c_{j-1}\}) \leq d(c_j, c_i)$$
> The second inequality uses FFT's greedy selection protocol (at step $j$, $c_j$ is the farthest point from $\{c_1,\ldots,c_{j-1}\}$); the third uses $i < j \Rightarrow c_i \in \{c_1,\ldots,c_{j-1}\}$. ✓
>
> **Main argument:** Let $S^* = \{c_1^*, \ldots, c_k^*\}$ be the optimal solution inducing clusters $C_1^*, \ldots, C_k^*$. Note that $\forall x \in P$: $d(x, S^*) \leq \Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P,k)$.
>
> By the **Pigeonhole Principle**, among the $k+1$ points $\{c_1,\ldots,c_k, q\}$, at least two — say $c_a, c_b$ with $a < b$ — fall in the same optimal cluster $C_t^*$.
>
> By triangle inequality:
> $$d(c_a, c_b) \leq d(c_a, c_t^*) + d(c_t^*, c_b) \leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P,k)$$
>
> Therefore:
> $$\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P,S) = d(q,S) \underset{\text{Claim}}{\leq} d(c_a,c_b) \leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P,k) \quad \square$$

## k-Center for Big Data — Composable Coresets

FFT requires $k-1$ scans of $P$ → impractical for massive datasets. Solution: **composable coreset** technique.

![[Pasted image 20260428112839.png]]
### MR-Farthest-First Traversal

**Algorithm MR-FFT** — 2 rounds:

**Round 1:**
- *Map Phase:* partition $P$ arbitrarily into $\ell$ subsets $P_1, \ldots, P_\ell$ of equal size $N/\ell$.
- *Reduce Phase:* for each $i \in [1,\ell]$, run $\text{FFT}(P_i, k)$ → $T_i \subseteq P_i$, $|T_i| = k$.

**Round 2:**
- *Map Phase:* empty.
- *Reduce Phase:* gather $T = \bigcup_{i=1}^\ell T_i$ ($|T| = \ell \cdot k$); run $\text{FFT}(T, k)$ with a single reducer → return $S = \{c_1, \ldots, c_k\}$.

### Space Analysis of MR-FFT

> [!Important] Theorem — Space Complexity of MR-FFT
> MR-FFT can be implemented with:
> $$M_L = O\!\left(\sqrt{Nk}\right), \quad M_A = O(N)$$
>
> **Dimostrazione:**
>
> Assume even partitioning in the Map Phase of Round 1.
>
> *$M_L$:*
> - Round 1, Map: $O(1)$
> - Round 1, Reduce: $O(N/\ell)$ (each partition has $N/\ell$ points; FFT uses linear space)
> - Round 2, Reduce: $O(\ell \cdot k)$
>
> $$M_L = O\!\left(\max\!\left\{\frac{N}{\ell},\ \ell \cdot k\right\}\right)$$
>
> The minimum of $\max\{N/\ell, \ell k\}$ is achieved at $\ell = \sqrt{N/k}$, giving:
> $$M_L = O\!\left(\sqrt{Nk}\right)$$
>
> *$M_A$:* each round involves $O(N)$ data (at most 1 pair per point). $\quad\square$
>
> **Obs.:** if $k = o(N)$, then $M_L = o(N)$ → meets MR design goals. For $k = O(1)$: $M_L = O(\sqrt{N})$.

![[Pasted image 20260428112904.png]]
### Coreset Quality Lemma

> [!Important] Lemma — Coreset Quality
> Let $T = \bigcup_{i=1}^\ell T_i$ be the coreset built by MR-FFT. For every $x \in P$:
> $$d(x, T) \leq 2 \cdot \Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k)$$
>
> **Dimostrazione:**
>
> Recall $P = P_1 \cup \cdots \cup P_\ell$ and $T = T_1 \cup \cdots \cup T_\ell$ with $T_r = \text{FFT}(P_r, k)$.
>
> For each $r$, $1 \leq r \leq \ell$, define $q_r$ = point of $P_r$ farthest from $T_r$.
>
> **Claim:** $d(q_r, T_r) \leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k)$.
>
> *Proof of Claim:* Let $S^* = \{c_1^*, \ldots, c_k^*\}$ be the optimal centers for $P$, inducing optimal clusters $C_t^*$. Let $T_r = \{c_1,\ldots,c_k\}$ and consider $\{c_1,\ldots,c_k, c_{k+1} = q_r\}$: these are $k+1$ points of $P_r \subseteq P$.
>
> By the same FFT Claim argument: $d(q_r, T_r) \leq d(c_i, c_j)$ for all $1 \leq i < j \leq k+1$.
>
> By the **Pigeonhole Principle**, two points $c_a, c_b$ ($a < b$) fall in the same optimal cluster $C_t^*$ of $P$. Therefore:
> $$d(q_r, T_r) \leq d(c_a, c_b) \leq d(c_a, c_t^*) + d(c_t^*, c_b) \leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k) \quad \checkmark$$
>
> If the Claim holds, for any $x \in P$ (say $x \in P_r$):
> $$d(x, T) \leq d(x, T_r) \leq d(q_r, T_r) \leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P,k) \quad \square$$
>
> **Intuizione:** the coreset $T$ "covers" all of $P$ within twice the optimal radius (labeled $R_1$ in the analysis).

### 4-Approximation Theorem for MR-FFT

> [!Important] Theorem — MR-FFT is a 4-Approximation
> Let $S$ be the centers returned by MR-FFT. Then:
> $$\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S) \leq 4 \cdot \Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k)$$
>
> **Dimostrazione:**
>
> From the Coreset Quality Lemma:
>
> (A) $\forall x \in P\ \exists y \in T : d(x,y) \leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P,k)$
>
> Recall $S = \text{FFT}(T, k)$. Let $\bar{y} \in T$ be the point of $T$ farthest from $S$. By the same Claim argument used in the Coreset Quality Lemma (applied now to $T$ and $S$):
> $$d(\bar{y}, S) \leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k)$$
>
> Hence:
>
> (B) $\forall y \in T\ \exists c \in S : d(y, c) \leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P,k)$
>
> Combining (A) and (B) via triangle inequality: for any $x \in P$ take $y \in T$ from (A) and $c \in S$ from (B):
> $$d(x, S) \leq d(x, c) \leq d(x, y) + d(y, c) \leq 4\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P,k) \quad \square$$
>
> **Obs.:** Moving to big data requires tolerating a slightly worse accuracy (factor 4 vs factor 2). $R_1 = d(x,y) \leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}$ from (A), $R_2 = d(y,c) \leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}$ from (B).

![[Pasted image 20260428112951.png]]
### Observations on MR-FFT

- FFT produces **good coresets** $T_i$: every point not in $T$ is well-represented by some coreset point.
- MR-FFT handles very large pointsets with approximation not too far from the best achievable.
- **Fundamental bound:** no MR algorithm can attain better accuracy than the best sequential algorithm (any MR algorithm becomes sequential on one processing element).
- **Low-dimensional improvement:** if $P$ has low dimensionality, extracting $\bar{k} > k$ centers per partition (instead of $k$) makes the approximation ratio approach 2, with moderate increase in coreset size $|T|$.

## Why Random Sampling Is Not a Good Coreset

Let $T \subseteq P$ of size $\sqrt{Nk}$ be selected uniformly at random with replacement. Is $\text{FFT}(T, k)$ a good solution?

**Answer: No.** Counterexample with $k = 2$:

![[Pasted image 20260428113024.png]]

- $|P_2| = m = O(1)$ (very small).
- $\Pr[T \text{ contains a point of } P_2] \leq \sqrt{Nk} \cdot \frac{m}{N} \xrightarrow{N\to\infty} 0$

So for large $N$: almost certainly $T \subseteq P_1$.
- Any clustering based on $T$ (hence on $P_1$ only) has radius $\geq \Delta_2$.
- But $\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, 2) \leq \Delta_1$.

Making $\Delta_2 \gg \Delta_1$ makes the approximation ratio **arbitrarily bad**.

**Conclusion:** uniform random sampling does not provide a good coreset for k-center because it misses small but geometrically important parts of $P$.

## Exercises

1. Let $T \subseteq P$ be a coreset of $|T| > k$ points such that $d(x, T) \leq \varepsilon \cdot \Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k)$ for every $x \in P$, for some $\varepsilon \in (0,1)$. Let $S$ be the $k$ centers from running FFT on $T$. Prove an upper bound to $\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S)$ as a function of $\varepsilon$ and $\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k)$.

2. Let $P$ be a set of points in $(M,d)$ and $T \subseteq P$. For any $k < |T|, |P|$, show that $\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(T, k) \leq 2\Phi^{\text{opt}}_{\text{kcenter}}(P, k)$. Is the bound tight?

3. Consider $P$ of $N$ points represented by $(i, x_i)$, $0 \leq i < N$. Let $S = \{S[1], \ldots, S[k]\}$ be $k$ centers **not in** $P$ (global array). Let $P^{(\ell)}$ = points of $P$ whose closest center is $S[\ell]$. Assume $k = O(\sqrt{N})$.
   1. Design a 2-round MR algorithm computing coreset $T \subset P$ with one point from each non-empty $P^{(\ell)}$.
   2. Analyze local and aggregate space. For full score: $o(N)$ local, $O(N)$ aggregate.
   3. Show how to use $T$ to approximate $\Delta(P) = \max_{x,y \in P} d(x,y)$; estimate approximation quality in terms of $R = \max_{x \in P} d(x, S)$.

4. Let $\mathcal{C} = (C_1, \ldots, C_k; S)$ be a $k$-clustering of $P$, each point $q$ represented by $(\text{ID}(q), (q, c(q)))$ where $c(q)$ is its center.
   1. Design a 2-round MR algorithm that for each center $c_i$ finds the most distant point in $C_i$.
   2. Analyze local ($o(N)$) and aggregate ($O(N)$) space.

5. Let $P$ be $N$ **bicolored** points in $k$ clusters, each $x$ represented by $(\text{ID}_x, (x, i_x, \gamma_x))$ with $\gamma_x \in \{0,1\}$.
   1. Design a 2-round MR algorithm that for each cluster $C_i$ outputs $(i, b_i)$ where $b_i = -1$ if mixed colors, else $b_i$ = common color.
   2. Analyze local ($o(N)$) and aggregate ($O(N)$) space.
