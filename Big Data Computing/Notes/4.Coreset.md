# 1. Coresets and Composable Coresets

## Coreset technique

Suppose we want to solve a problem $\Pi$ on instances $P$ which are **too large** to be processed by known algorithms.

> [!info] **Coreset technique**
> 1. Extract a **"small" subset** $T$ from $P$ (dubbed ***coreset***), making sure that it **represents $P$ well** w.r.t. solutions to $\Pi$.
> 2. Run the **best known (possibly slow) sequential algorithm** for $\Pi$ on the small coreset $T$, rather than on the entire input $P$.

**The technique works if:**
- $T$ can be extracted **efficiently** by processing $P$, possibly in a distributed or streaming fashion.
- The solution computed on $T$ is a **good solution** for $\Pi$ w.r.t. the entire input $P$.

![Coreset technique diagram](Pics/coreset/slide-05.png)

> *Coreset construction: extract a small set of representatives (coreset $T$) from the input dataset $P$. Apply the best known sequential algorithm to coreset $T$ to compute the final solution $S$.*

---

## Composable Coreset technique

The composable variant is designed for distributed or parallel computation.

> [!info] **Composable Coreset technique**
> 1. **Partition** $P$ into $\ell$ subsets $P_1, P_2, \ldots, P_\ell$, and extract a "small" coreset $T_i$ from each $P_i$, making sure that it represents $P_i$ well.
> 2. Run the best known (possibly slow) sequential algorithm for $\Pi$ on $T = \bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell} T_i$, rather than on $P$.

**The technique works if:**
- Each $T_i$ can be **extracted efficiently** from $P_i$ **in parallel** for all $i$'s.
- The final coreset $T$ is **still small** and the solution computed on $T$ is a **good solution** for $\Pi$ w.r.t. the entire input $P$.

![Composable coreset technique diagram](Pics/coreset/slide-07.png)

> *Each partition $P_i$ produces a local coreset $T_i$. All local coresets are gathered into the final coreset $T = \bigcup T_i$. The best sequential algorithm runs on $T$ to produce the final solution $S$.*

# 2. Case Study: k-center Clustering

## General definition of clustering

**CLUSTERING** groups a set of objects into several subsets (**clusters**) such that:
- Objects in the **same cluster** are ***"similar"*** to one another
- Objects in **different clusters** are ***"dissimilar"*** from one another

A **clustering problem** requires:
- A notion of **similarity**, usually captured by a ***distance function*** on the object space
- An **objective to optimize**, selecting the best among several solutions, and/or constraints to satisfy

## Metric Space

Clustering inputs are typically sets of points from a **metric space**.

> [!abstract] **Definition — Metric Space**
> A **metric space** is an ordered pair $(M, d)$ where $M$ is a set and $d(\cdot)$ is a **metric** on $M$, i.e., a function
> $$d : M \times M \to \mathbb{R}$$
> such that for every $x, y, z \in M$:
> - $d(x, y) \geq 0$
> - $d(x, y) = 0$ if and only if $x = y$
> - $d(x, y) = d(y, x)$ &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; *(symmetry)*
> - $d(x, z) \leq d(x, y) + d(y, z)$ &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; *(triangle inequality)*

> [!tip] **Remark**
> The choice of distance function can have a significant impact on cluster quality. The **triangle inequality** is especially useful in big data: it lets you bound unknown distances using known ones.

---

### Triangle inequality

Suppose distance represents dissimilarity. By the triangle inequality:

$$d(x,y) - d(y,z) \leq d(x,z) \leq d(x,y) + d(y,z)$$

If $d(y, z)$ is small, then $d(x, z) \approx d(x, y)$, so $z$ and $y$ are almost indistinguishable w.r.t. $x$ — one of them can be discarded. This is the geometric basis for coreset construction.

---

## Distance functions

### Minkowski distances ($L_r$-norm)

Let $X, Y \in \mathbb{R}^n$, with $X = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)$ and $Y = (y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_n)$. For $r > 0$ the $L_r$-distance between $X$ and $Y$ is:

$$d_{L_r}(X, Y) = \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i - y_i|^r\right)^{1/r}$$

| $r$ | Distance name | Use |
|-----|--------------|-----|
| $r = 2$ | **Euclidean distance** $(\|\cdot\|)$ | Standard distance in $\mathbb{R}^n$ |
| $r = 1$ | **Manhattan distance** | Grid-like environments |
| $r = \infty$ | **Chebyshev distance** | Maximum absolute difference of coordinates |

#### Example: Minkowski distances between $A=(2,1)$ and $B=(7,5)$

![Minkowski distance example](Pics/coreset/slide-17.png)

$$d_{L_1}(A,B) = (7-2)+(5-1) = 9$$
$$d_{L_2}(A,B) = \sqrt{5^2+4^2} \approx 6.403$$
$$d_{L_\infty}(A,B) = \max(5,4) = 5$$

#### Comparison of Minkowski distances (unit balls)

![Minkowski distance shape comparison](Pics/coreset/slide-18.png)

> *As $r$ goes from 0.5 to $\infty$, the unit ball transitions from a concave star to a diamond ($r=1$), a circle ($r=2$), and a square ($r=\infty$).*

---

### Angular distance

Used when points are **vectors** in $\mathbb{R}^n$. The angular distance between $X$ and $Y$ is the angle between them:

$$d_{\text{angular}}(X, Y) = \arccos\!\left(\frac{X \cdot Y}{\|X\| \cdot \|Y\|}\right) = \arccos\!\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i)^2} \cdot \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i)^2}}\right) \in [0, \pi]$$

**Ranges:**
- Non-negative coordinates: $d_{\text{angular}} \in [0, \pi/2]$
- Arbitrary coordinates: $d_{\text{angular}} \in [0, \pi]$

**Example:** For $X = (1, 2, -1)$ and $Y = (2, 1, 1)$:

$$d_{\text{angular}}(X, Y) = \arccos \left( \frac{3}{6} \right) = \arccos \left( \frac{1}{2} \right) = \frac{\pi}{3}$$

*Obs: In [LRU14] this is called cosine distance, but in the literature that term has a different meaning.*

To normalize to $[0,1]$, divide by $\pi$ or $\pi/2$ depending on whether coordinates are arbitrary or non-negative. Scalar multiples of a vector are treated as the same vector.

![Angular distance diagram](Pics/coreset/slide-20.png)

> [!tip] **Remark**
> Angular distance measures ratios among feature values, not their absolute values. This makes it well suited for comparing documents of different lengths, where a document $X$ over a vocabulary of $n$ words is represented as an $n$-vector with $x_i$ = number of occurrences of word $i$.

---

### Hamming distance

Used when points are **binary vectors**: $X, Y \in \{0,1\}^n$. The Hamming distance counts **the number of coordinates in which they differ**:

$$d_{\text{Hamming}}(X, Y) = |\{i \mid x_i \neq y_i\}|$$

**Example:** For $X = (0, 1, 1, 0, 1)$ and $Y = (1, 1, 1, 0, 0)$: positions 1 and 5 differ, so $d_{\text{Hamming}} = 2$.

> [!abstract] **Observation**
> $d_{\text{Hamming}}(X, Y) = d_{L_1}(X, Y)$ for binary vectors.

**Use cases:** fixed-length binary words, genetic distance.

---

### Jaccard distance

Used when points are **sets** (e.g., documents as bags of words). The Jaccard distance between sets $S$ and $T$ is:

$$d_{\text{Jaccard}}(S, T) = 1 - \frac{|S \cap T|}{|S \cup T|} = \frac{|S \cup T| - |S \cap T|}{|S \cup T|}$$

- Range: $[0,1]$; it is $0$ iff $S = T$ and $1$ iff $S$ and $T$ are disjoint.
- $|S \cap T| / |S \cup T|$ is called the **Jaccard similarity**.

![Jaccard distance diagram](Pics/coreset/slide-22.png)

> *With $|S \cap T| = 6$ (red dots) and $|S \cup T| = 18$ (all dots), $d_{\text{Jaccard}}(S,T) = 1 - 6/18 = 2/3$.*

**Use cases:** measuring overlap between sets of topics, documents, etc.

---

### Which distance should we use?

| Distance family | When to use |
|----------------|-------------|
| **Minkowski & Angular** | Objects characterized by **numerical feature values** (e.g., frequency). Minkowski aggregates the gap per dimension; Angular measures ratios among values. |
| **Hamming & Jaccard** | Objects characterized by **presence/absence of features** (no multiplicity). Hamming counts total differences; Jaccard measures the ratio of differences to total features. |

> [!abstract] **Observation**
> All of the above distances satisfy the four requirements for a metric space.

> [!example] **Exercise**
> Show that the $L_1$ distance satisfies the four requirements for a metric space.

## Types of clusterings

Given a set of points in a metric space, a clustering problem often specifies an **objective function** to optimize. This also allows comparing different solutions.

Clustering problems can be categorized based on whether:
- A **target number $k$** of clusters is given in input
- Each cluster must have an identified **center**
- **Disjoint clusters** are sought

---

## Background: optimization problem

> [!abstract] **Definition — Combinatorial Optimization Problem**
> A **(Combinatorial) Optimization Problem** is defined by:
> - A set $\mathcal{I}$ of **instances** (possible inputs)
> - A set $\mathcal{S}$ of **solutions** (possible outputs)
> - For each $i \in I$, a set $\mathcal{S}_i \subseteq \mathcal{S}$ of **feasible solutions**
> - An **objective function** $\Phi : \mathcal{S} \to \mathbb{R}$
>
> Given $i \in I$, the problem requires finding an **optimal solution** $s^* \in \mathcal{S}_i$ such that:
> - $\Phi(s^*) = \min\{\Phi(s) : s \in \mathcal{S}_i\}$ &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*(minimization)*
> - $\Phi(s^*) = \max\{\Phi(s) : s \in \mathcal{S}_i\}$ &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*(maximization)*
>
> *Obs. Technically, if $\mathcal{S}_i$ is not finite, min and max become inf and sup.*

![Optimization problem diagram: instance space I, feasible solutions S_i, optimal s*](Pics/coreset/slide-27.png)

---

## Background: approximation algorithm

> [!abstract] **Definition — Approximation Algorithm**
> Let $\Pi$ be an optimization problem. For $c \geq 1$, a ***c*-approximation algorithm** $A$ for $\Pi$ returns, for each instance $i \in \mathcal{I}$, a feasible solution $A(i) \in \mathcal{S}_i$ such that:
> - $\Phi(A(i)) \leq c \cdot \min\{\Phi(s) : s \in \mathcal{S}_i\}$ &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*(minimization)*
> - $\Phi(A(i)) \geq (1/c) \cdot \max\{\Phi(s) : s \in \mathcal{S}_i\}$ &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*(maximization)*
>
> $c$ is the **approximation ratio**; $A(i)$ is called a ***c*-approximate solution** for $i$.

### Why approximation algorithms matter in Big Data

> [!warning] **Observations**
> Approximation algorithms are important in big data computing for several reasons:
> - For many optimization problems, finding optimal solutions is very time consuming on large inputs:
>   - **NP-hard** problems (likely require exponential time)
>   - Problems where polynomial-time algorithms exist but require **superlinear time** (quadratic, cubic)
> - Allowing approximations widens the space of tractable solutions.
> - In practice, inputs are often noisy, so the "true" optimum may not be well defined anyway.

## Center-based clustering

Let $P$ be a set of $N$ points in metric space $(M, d)$, and let $k$ be the target number of clusters, $1 \leq k \leq N$.

> [!abstract] **Definition — k-clustering**
> A ***k*-clustering** of $P$ is a tuple $\mathcal{C} = (C_1, C_2, \ldots, C_k; S)$ comprising:
> - A **partition** of $P$ into $k$ clusters: $P = C_1 \cup C_2 \cup \cdots \cup C_k$, with $C_i \cap C_j = \emptyset$ $\forall i,j$
> - A set of $k$ **centers** $S = \{c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_k\}$, where $c_i \in C_i$ is the center of cluster $C_i$, for $1 \leq i \leq k$

> [!tip] **Remark**
> In this definition, centers are points of $P$. In some cases (e.g., Euclidean spaces), centers outside $P$ may be allowed.

#### Example of 5-clustering

![5-clustering example](Pics/coreset/slide-31.png)

> *Five clusters, each with a center (red dot). Every point belongs to exactly one cluster.*

## Popular center-based clustering problems

There are three common center-based clustering problems, each optimizing a different objective:

> [!abstract] **Definition — k-center, k-means, k-median**
> For any point $x \in P$ and any subset $S \subseteq P$, define:
> $$d(x, S) = \min_{y \in S} d(x, y)$$
> i.e., the distance from $x$ to the closest point in $S$.
>
> Given a finite pointset $P \subseteq M$ and an integer $k \leq |P|$, the three problems require returning a subset $S \subseteq P$ of $k$ **centers** minimizing:
>
> | Problem | Objective $\Phi$ | Formula |
> |---------|-----------------|---------|
> | **k-center** | minimize *max* distance to closest center | $\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S) = \max_{x \in P} d(x, S)$ |
> | **k-means** | minimize *sum of squared* distances | $\Phi_{\text{kmeans}}(P, S) = \sum_{x \in P} (d(x, S))^2$ |
> | **k-median** | minimize *sum* of distances | $\Phi_{\text{kmedian}}(P, S) = \sum_{x \in P} d(x, S)$ |

> [!tip] **Observation**
> For k-means and k-median, minimizing the sum of (squared) distances is equivalent to minimizing the **average** (squared) distance.

![k-center objective: minimize max distance from point to closest center](Pics/coreset/slide-33.png)

## Terminology and notations

For the k-center/means/median clustering problems:
- **Instance:** $(P, k)$
- **Feasible solutions:** all subsets $S \subseteq P$ of size $k$
- **Optimal solution:** $S^*$ — the feasible solution minimizing the appropriate objective

We define the **optimal objective values**:
$$\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}^{\text{opt}}(P,k) = \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S^*)$$
$$\Phi_{\text{kmeans}}^{\text{opt}}(P,k) = \Phi_{\text{kmeans}}(P, S^*)$$
$$\Phi_{\text{kmedian}}^{\text{opt}}(P,k) = \Phi_{\text{kmedian}}(P, S^*)$$

> *Why output only centers? The partition can always be recovered by assigning each point to its closest center.*

It is crucial to observe that given P and K and a set S of K centers, the "best" k-clustering of P around these centers is the one that associates each point $x \in P$ to the cluster represented by the closest center. 
The assignment of each point $x \in P$ to its closest center can be computed.

**Sequential algorithm**: $O(N\cdot K)$ time.
Map reduce
- Round: 1
- $M_L$: $O(K)$
- $M_A$: $O(N)$
Considering centers as a shared variable and using the Map Phase of the round to compute for every point the closest center. 

---

## Observations on the three problems

> [!warning] **Computational hardness**
> The k-center/means/median problems are **NP-hard** — searching for the exact optimal $S^*$ is not practical. Good approximation algorithms exist, but handling **large inputs efficiently is still an open problem**.

**When to use which:**
- **k-center** is appropriate when every point must be close to a center (e.g., facility placement, or when centers are used as data representatives). It is sensitive to outliers.
- **k-means/median** give guarantees on the average (squared) distance, so they are more robust to isolated noisy points, though k-means tends to be faster in practice.

> [!info] **k-center and k-median belong to the family of *facility-location* problems**

![Facility location: customers, open and closed facilities, active connections](Pics/coreset/slide-41.png)

- Squares are the possible locations for facilities
- Circles are customer locations
- K is the number of facilities to open
- Goal: find the best k locations for opening facilities, which minimize the max/avg distance of a customer from the closest facility.

# k-center

## Farthest-First Traversal: algorithm

A **2-approximation sequential algorithm** by T.F. Gonzalez [*Theoretical Computer Science*, 38:293–306, 1985].

- Simple, reasonably fast when the input fits in main memory.
- Also useful as a primitive for extracting representative samples.

**Input:** Set $P$ of $N$ points from a metric space $(M, d)$, integer $k > 1$

**Output:** A set $S$ of $k$ centers such that $\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S)$ is close to $\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}^{\text{opt}}(P, k)$

> [!example] **Algorithm: Farthest-First Traversal (FFT)**
> ```
> S ← {c₁}   // c₁ ∈ P, arbitrary point
> for i ← 2 to k do
>     Find the point cᵢ ∈ P − S that maximizes d(cᵢ, S)
>     S ← S ∪ {cᵢ}
> return S
> ```
> **Observation:** The clustering induced by $S$ can be computed by `Assign(P, S)`. The closest-center assignment can be maintained incrementally inside the for-loop.

> [!example] **Exercise**
> Show that the Farthest-First Traversal algorithm can be implemented to run in $O(N \cdot k)$ time.
> **Hint:** in each iteration $i$, each point $p \in P - S$ should know its closest center among $c_1, \ldots, c_{i-1}$ and the distance to it.

## Farthest-First Traversal: analysis

> [!abstract] **Theorem — FFT is a 2-approximation**
> Let $S$ be the set of centers returned by FFT on $P$. Then:
> $$\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S) \leq 2 \cdot \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}^{\text{opt}}(P, k)$$

> [!note] **Proof**
> Let $S = \{c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_k\}$ and let $\text{OPT} = \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}^{\text{opt}}(P,k)$.
>
> **Step 1 — Define $q$ and the witness set.**
> Let $q \in P$ be the point that would be chosen as the $(k+1)$-th center if FFT ran one more step:
> $$d(q, S) = \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S) = \max_{x \in P} d(x, S)$$
> Consider the set $W = \{c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_k, q\}$ of $k+1$ points.
>
> **Step 2 — Any two points in $W$ are far apart.**
> By construction of FFT, for any $c_j$ with $j \geq 2$:
> $$d(c_j, \{c_1, \ldots, c_{j-1}\}) \geq d(q, \{c_1, \ldots, c_k\}) = \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S)$$
> So for $i < j$: $d(c_i, c_j) \geq \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P,S)$, and $d(q, c_i) \geq d(q, S) = \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P,S)$.
> Every pair of distinct points in $W$ is at distance $\geq \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P,S)$.
>
> **Step 3 — Pigeonhole on the optimal solution.**
> $S^*$ has $k$ centers, $W$ has $k+1$ points. By the pigeonhole principle, at least two points $u, v \in W$ lie in the same optimal cluster $C_\ell^*$.
>
> **Step 4 — Triangle inequality.**
> Since $u, v \in C_\ell^*$:
> $$d(u, v) \leq d(u, c_\ell^*) + d(c_\ell^*, v) \leq 2 \cdot \text{OPT}$$
>
> **Step 5 — Conclude.**
> From Steps 2 and 4:
> $$\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S) \leq d(u, v) \leq 2 \cdot \text{OPT} \qquad \square$$

The diagram below shows the key argument: $q$ and $c_2$ land in the same optimal cluster, yet their distance is large.

![FFT analysis: q and centers, showing 2-approximation argument](Pics/coreset/slide-51.png)

> *$q$ is the most distant point from its center. Any two points in $\{c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4, c_5, q\}$ are at distance $\geq d(q, \{c_1,c_2,c_3,c_4,c_5\}) = d(q,c_2)$.*

---

## Observations on k-center clustering

> [!warning] **Properties and limitations**
> - The k-center objective measures worst-case distance to the closest center.
> - FFT's approximation guarantee is nearly optimal: computing a $c$-approximation to k-center is **NP-hard for any fixed $c < 2$**.
> - The k-center objective is **sensitive to outliers**. On noisy datasets, the solution that minimizes the k-center objective may be pulled toward outliers, missing the actual cluster structure.

# k-center Clustering for Big Data

## The scalability problem

> [!warning] **Observation**
> FFT requires $k-1$ full scans of $P$. For large $P$ and $k$ not small, this is impractical on a single machine.

**The question:** how to compute a good k-center solution when $P$ is too large for a single machine?

**The answer:** use the composable coreset technique, with FFT as the extraction primitive.

---

## Composable coresets for k-center clustering

The coreset extraction primitive is FFT itself.

---

## MapReduce-Farthest-First Traversal

> [!info] **Algorithm: MR-Farthest-First Traversal**
>
> **Round 1:**
> - **Map:** Partition $P$ arbitrarily into $\ell$ equal subsets $P_1, P_2, \ldots, P_\ell$.
> - **Reduce:** For each $i \in [1, \ell]$ separately, run FFT on $P_i$ to produce a set $T_i \subseteq P_i$ of $k$ centers.
>
> **Round 2:**
> - **Map:** empty.
> - **Reduce:** Gather the coreset $T = \bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell} T_i$ (size $\ell \cdot k$), then run FFT on $T$ in a single reducer to return the final set $S = \{c_1, \ldots, c_k\}$ of $k$ centers.

![MR-Farthest-First Traversal algorithm diagram](Pics/coreset/slide-66.png)

---

## Analysis of MR-Farthest-First Traversal

### Space complexity

> [!abstract] **Theorem — Space complexity**
> The 2-round MR-FFT algorithm runs using:
> $$M_L = O\!\left(\sqrt{Nk}\right) \quad \text{(local space)} \qquad M_A = O(N) \quad \text{(aggregate space)}$$

> [!note] **Proof**
> Set $\ell = \sqrt{N/k}$.
> - Each $|P_i| = N/\ell = \sqrt{Nk}$, which fits in local space. ✓
> - $|T| = \ell \cdot k = k \cdot \sqrt{N/k} = \sqrt{Nk}$, which fits in the single Round 2 reducer. ✓
> - $M_A = \sum_i |P_i| = N$. ✓ $\square$

### Quality of the coreset

> [!abstract] **Lemma — Coreset quality**
> Let $T = \bigcup_i T_i$ be the coreset produced by MR-FFT. For every $x \in P$:
> $$d(x, T) \leq 2 \cdot \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}^{\text{opt}}(P, k)$$

> [!note] **Proof**
> Each $T_i$ is produced by running FFT on $P_i$ with $k$ centers. By the 2-approximation guarantee:
> $$\forall x \in P_i : \quad d(x, T_i) \leq 2 \cdot \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}^{\text{opt}}(P_i, k)$$
> Since $P_i \subseteq P$, the optimal solution for $P$ is also valid for $P_i$, so $\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}^{\text{opt}}(P_i, k) \leq \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}^{\text{opt}}(P, k)$.
> Therefore $d(x, T_i) \leq 2 \cdot \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}^{\text{opt}}(P,k)$, and since $T_i \subseteq T$, this holds as $d(x, T)$ as well.
> Since this holds for every $i$, it holds for all $x \in P$. $\square$

> [!tip] **Observation**
> This lemma gives a quantitative measure of how well the coreset $T$ represents $P$ w.r.t. the k-center objective.

### Overall approximation quality

> [!abstract] **Theorem — MR-FFT is a 4-approximation**
> Let $S$ be the set of $k$ centers returned by MR-FFT on $P$. Then:
> $$\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S) \leq 4 \cdot \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}^{\text{opt}}(P, k)$$

> [!note] **Proof**
> Let $\text{OPT} = \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}^{\text{opt}}(P,k)$.
>
> **Step 1 — Quality of $S$ on $T$.**
> $S$ is produced by FFT on $T$. By the 2-approximation theorem:
> $$\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(T, S) \leq 2 \cdot \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}^{\text{opt}}(T, k)$$
> Since $T \subseteq P$, the optimal solution $S^*$ for $P$ is also valid for $T$, giving $\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}^{\text{opt}}(T, k) \leq \text{OPT}$.
> So $\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(T, S) \leq 2 \cdot \text{OPT}$.
>
> **Step 2 — Extend from $T$ to $P$.**
> For any $x \in P$, let $t_x$ be the closest point in $T$. By the triangle inequality:
> $$d(x, S) \leq d(x, t_x) + d(t_x, S) \leq d(x, T) + \Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(T, S)$$
> By the Coreset Quality Lemma, $d(x, T) \leq 2 \cdot \text{OPT}$. So:
> $$d(x, S) \leq 2 \cdot \text{OPT} + 2 \cdot \text{OPT} = 4 \cdot \text{OPT}$$
>
> **Step 3 — Take the max.**
> $$\Phi_{\text{kcenter}}(P, S) = \max_{x \in P} d(x, S) \leq 4 \cdot \text{OPT} \qquad \square$$

---

## Observations on MR-Farthest-First Traversal

> [!tip] **Summary**
> - FFT produces good local coresets $T_i$, so $T = \bigcup T_i$ ensures that any point in $P$ is well represented.
> - MR-FFT scales to very large pointsets, and the final approximation ratio (4) is not far from the sequential best (2).
> - No MR algorithm can be more accurate than the best sequential algorithm, since any MR algorithm reduces to a sequential one when run on a single machine.

### Low-dimensional pointsets

> [!info] **Improvement for low-dimensional data**
> When $P$ has low dimensionality, the approximation ratio of MR-FFT can be made arbitrarily close to **2** by using a slightly larger coreset, while still maintaining sublinear local space and linear aggregate space.

## Does a random sample provide a good coreset?

> [!question] **Question considered in class**
> Let $T \subseteq P$ be a coreset of $|T| = \sqrt{Nk}$ points drawn uniformly at random from $P$ (with replacement). Run FFT on $T$ to get $k$ centers $S$.
>
> *Is $S$ a good solution to k-center on $P$?*

Generally **no**. A random sample gives no guarantee that every region of $P$ is covered — FFT, by contrast, greedily spreads out its selections. An isolated cluster might not be sampled at all, which is enough to make the k-center approximation arbitrarily bad.
