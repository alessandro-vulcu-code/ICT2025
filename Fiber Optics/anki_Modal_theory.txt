What is Total Internal Reflection?	Consider a fiber. We have the core and the cladding. We have to respect two conditions: <br>     \begin{itemize} <br>         \item The refractive index of the incidence medium must be greater than the refractive index of the transmission medium ($n_1 > n_2$); <br>         \item The angle of incidence $\theta_i$ must be greater than the critical angle $\theta_c = \sin^{-1}(n_2/n_1)$. <br>     \end{itemize} <br>  <br>     When $\theta_i < \theta_c$ we have \textit{uniform trasmitted waves} and $n_1 \sin(\theta_i) = n_2 \sin(\theta_t)$.
Considered a step-index fiber; let us determine the maximum input angle that a ray     may have so that the ray refracted in the fiber core is guided by TIR.	\begin{itemize} <br>         \item \textbf{Snell's Law at the input interface (Air to Core):} <br>               \begin{equation} <br>                   n_0 \sin \beta_M = n_1 \sin \beta_l \implies \sin \beta_M = n_1 \sqrt{1 - \cos^2 \beta_l} \quad (\text{assuming } n_0 \approx 1) <br>               \end{equation} <br>  <br>         \item \textbf{Total Internal Reflection (TIR) condition at Core-Cladding interface:} <br>               The ray is guided if the incidence angle is greater than or equal to the critical angle $\theta_c$: <br>               \begin{equation} <br>                   \frac{\pi}{2} - \beta_l \ge \theta_c \implies \cos \beta_l \ge \sin \theta_c <br>               \end{equation} <br>  <br>         \item \textbf{Critical Angle definition:} <br>               \begin{equation} <br>                   \sin \theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1} <br>               \end{equation} <br>  <br>         \item \textbf{Substitution for Maximum Angle ($\beta_M$):} <br>               Substituting the limiting case $\cos \beta_l = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$ into the first equation: <br>               \begin{equation} <br>                   \sin \beta_M = n_1 \sqrt{1 - \left( \frac{n_2}{n_1} \right)^2} = \sqrt{n_1^2 - n_2^2} <br>               \end{equation} <br>  <br>         \item \textbf{Final Result (Numerical Aperture):} <br>               \begin{equation} <br>                   NA = \sin \beta_M = \sqrt{n_1^2 - n_2^2} <br>               \end{equation} <br>     \end{itemize} <br>  <br>     Large NA $\implies$ easier to couple light into the fiber, but can cause ISI (modal dispersion).
Estimate modal dispersion in a step-index fiber.	\begin{align} <br>          <br>         T_1               & = L \quad (\text{fiber length})                                                                                                                              \\ <br>         T_2               & = \frac{L}{\cos \beta_l}                                                                                                                                     \\ <br>          <br>         v                 & = \frac{c}{n_1} \quad (\text{speed of propagation})                                                                                                          \\ <br>          <br>         \Delta \tau       & = \frac{\Delta L}{v} = L \left( \frac{1}{\cos \beta_l} - 1 \right) \frac{n_1}{c}                                                                             \\ <br>          <br>                           & \text{Nonetheless, it must be: } \cos \beta_l \geq \frac{n_2}{n_1}                                                                                           \\ <br>          <br>         \Delta \tau       & = \frac{\Delta L}{v} = L \left( \frac{1}{\cos \beta_l} - 1 \right) \frac{n_1}{c} \leq L \left( \frac{n_1}{n_2} - 1 \right) \frac{n_1}{c} = \Delta \tau_{max} \\ <br>          <br>                           & \text{If we assume that } n_1 \approx n_2 \implies NA^2 \approx 2n_1(n_1 - n_2)                                                                              \\ <br>          <br>         \Delta \tau_{max} & \approx L \frac{NA^2}{2 n_2 c} \quad \text{(max delay the signal can experience)} <br>     \end{align}
What is Graded-Index Fiber?	A graded-index (GRIN) fiber is a type of optical fiber, typically multimode, designed specifically to reduce intermodal dispersion by managing how light rays travel through the core.\\ <br>     The refractive index $n(r)$ varies radially. It is highest at the fiber axis ($n_1$) and lowest at the core-cladding interface ($n_2$). This variation is often modeled by a power law (the $\alpha$-profile), which is typically parabolic ($\alpha \approx 2$): <br>     \[  <br>         n^{2}(r)=n_{1}^{2}\left[1-2 \Delta\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)^{g}\right] <br>      \] <br>     where: <br>     \begin{itemize} <br>         \item $n_1$ is the refractive index at the fiber axis; <br>         \item $n_2$ is the refractive index of the cladding; <br>         \item $\Delta = \frac{n_1 - n_2}{n_1}$ is the relative refractive index difference; <br>         \item $a$ is the radius of the core; <br>         \item $g$ is the profile parameter (typically $g \approx 2$ for parabolic profile). <br>     \end{itemize}
What are the rays type?	\begin{itemize} <br>         \item Ray Type 1: shortest path, highest refractive index, lowest propagation speed <br>         \item Ray Type 2: longest path, lowest refractive index, average propagation speed <br>         \item Ray Type 3: helical path, lowest refractive index, highest propagation speed <br>     \end{itemize}
Describe Guided modes and radiation modes.	\begin{itemize} <br>         \item \textbf{Guided Modes}: These are field structures that are confined within the fiber's core and guided by it. They are the desired modes for transmitting power. <br>         \item \textbf{Radiation Modes}: These are field structures that are not confined by the guiding structure of the fiber. Their excitation represents a loss of power and should be avoided. <br>     \end{itemize}
Step index fiber propagation modes.	General modes: <br>     \begin{itemize} <br>         \item Transverse electric ($TE_{0,p}$) <br>         \item Transverse magnetic ($TM_{0,p}$) <br>         \item Hybrid electric ($HE_{m,p}$) <br>         \item Hybrid magnetic ($HM_{m,p}$) <br>     \end{itemize} <br>     Fundamental mode: $HE_{1,1}$ which cut-off freq is zero.\\ <br>     First higher order mode: $TE_{0,1}$ and $TM_{0,1}$\\ <br>     Weak guide approximation: $n_{core} \approx n_{cladding}$ \\ <br>     LP modes: Linearly polarized modes
Modal theory	EM field decomposed into \textbf{transverse and longitudinal components} <br>     \begin{equation*} <br>         \boxed{ <br>             \begin{cases} <br>                 \bar{E}(x,y,z) = \left\{ \bar{E}_t(x,y) + E_z(x,y)\hat{z} \right\} e^{-j\beta z} \\ <br>                 \bar{H}(x,y,z) = \left\{ \bar{H}_t(x,y) + H_z(x,y)\hat{z} \right\} e^{-j\beta z} <br>             \end{cases} <br>         } <br>     \end{equation*} <br>     \begin{itemize} <br>         \item Propagation constant $\Rightarrow \beta$ unknown <br>     \end{itemize} <br>  <br>     \vspace{1em} <br>     \noindent If we insert the previous expressions in Maxwell's eq. we have: <br>  <br>     \begin{itemize} <br>         \item[\textbf{[1]}] $\nabla_t^2 \bar{E}_t - (\beta^2 - k^2) \bar{E}_t = 0 \Rightarrow$ Helmholtz eq. for transverse component of $E_t$ <br>  <br>         \item[\textbf{[2]}] $j \beta E_z = \nabla \cdot \bar{E}_t \Rightarrow$ This connects the longitudinal component of the electric field ($E_z$) to the divergence of transversal component of e.f. ($\nabla \cdot \bar{E}_t$) <br>  <br>         \item[\textbf{[3a]}] $j \omega \mu_o \bar{H}_t = \hat{z} \times (\nabla E_z + j \beta \bar{E}_t) \Rightarrow$ relation between transverse comp. of $H_t$ with electric field components and their derivatives <br>  <br>         \item[\textbf{[3b]}] $j \beta H_z = \nabla \cdot \bar{H}_t \Rightarrow$ relation between long. component of $H_z$ and the divergence of transv. component of magnetic field ($\nabla \cdot \bar{H}_t$) <br>     \end{itemize} <br>     where: <br>     \begin{equation*} <br>         k^2 = <br>         \begin{cases} <br>             \omega^2 \mu_0 \varepsilon_{co} = k_{co}^2 & \text{in the core}     \\ <br>             \omega^2 \mu_0 \varepsilon_{cl} = k_{cl}^2 & \text{in the cladding} <br>         \end{cases} <br>     \end{equation*} <br>     Also, the eigenvalue are defined as: <br>     \begin{align*} <br>         \chi_{co}^2 & = k_{co}^2 - \beta^2 = \omega^2 \mu_0 \epsilon_{co} - \beta^2 \\ <br>         \chi_{cl}^2 & = k_{cl}^2 - \beta^2 = \omega^2 \mu_0 \epsilon_{cl} - \beta^2 <br>     \end{align*} <br>     \noindent It can be shown that the propagation constant must fulfill the following important constraint: <br>     \begin{equation*} <br>         k_{cl} = \omega \sqrt{\mu_0 \epsilon_{cl}} < \beta < \omega \sqrt{\mu_0 \epsilon_{co}} = k_{co} <br>     \end{equation*} <br>  <br>     \noindent therefore $\chi_{co}^2 \geq 0$ and $\chi_{cl}^2 \leq 0$. <br>  <br>     \vspace{1em} <br>     \noindent It is convenient to define the following real quantities: <br>     \begin{equation*} <br>         \chi_{co} = \sqrt{k_{co}^2 - \beta^2}, \quad \quad \tilde{\chi}_{cl} = \sqrt{\beta^2 - k_{cl}^2} <br>     \end{equation*} <br>  <br>     \noindent Notice that: <br>     \begin{equation*} <br>         \chi_{co}^2 - \chi_{cl}^2 = \chi_{co}^2 + \tilde{\chi}_{cl}^2 = \left( \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \right)^2 \text{NA}^2 <br>     \end{equation*} <br>  <br>     we can also say that $\chi_\text{core}^2 \geq 0, \chi_\text{cladding}^2 \leq 0 \Rightarrow \chi_\text{cladding} \in \mathbb{C}$\\ <br>     Now we have to solve the Helmholtz equation: <br>     \[ \nabla^2\vec{E_t} + \chi^2\vec{E_t} = 0 \qquad \vec{E_t} = \left[\begin{matrix} <br>                 E_x \\ <br>                 E_y \\ <br>                 0 <br>             \end{matrix}\right] \] <br>     Here comes the LP assumption $\Rightarrow$ polarization stays the same everywhere on the cross section, hence: <br>     \[ \text{Y-polarization} \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad E_x = 0 \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \vec{E_t} = \left[\begin{matrix} <br>                 0   \\ <br>                 E_y \\ <br>                 0 <br>             \end{matrix}\right] \]
Explain briefly effective refractive index	The effective refractive index ($n_{\text{eff}}$ or $\bar{n}$) is a parameter that quantifies the phase velocity of a specific mode propagating within an optical fiber. It connects the propagation constant $\beta$ of the mode to the frequency of light $\omega$ and the speed of light $c$. <br>  <br>     \begin{itemize} <br>         \item \textbf{Definition:} It is defined by the relationship: <br>               \begin{equation*} <br>                   \beta = \frac{\omega}{c} n_{\text{eff}} \quad \left( \text{or equivalently } n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\beta}{k_0} \right) <br>               \end{equation*} <br>  <br>         \item \textbf{Physical Interpretation:} It can be interpreted as the average refractive index ``seen'' by the optical field of a guided mode as it propagates. Since the optical field extends across both the core and the cladding, $n_{\text{eff}}$ represents a weighted average of the refractive indices of these two regions. <br>  <br>         \item \textbf{Range:} For a guided mode, the effective refractive index is always bounded between the refractive index of the cladding ($n_{\text{cl}}$ or $n_2$) and the core ($n_{\text{co}}$ or $n_1$): <br>               \begin{equation*} <br>                   n_{\text{cl}} < n_{\text{eff}} < n_{\text{co}} <br>               \end{equation*} <br>  <br>         \item \textbf{Cut-off Condition:} $n_{\text{eff}}$ depends on the frequency (wavelength); if $n_{\text{eff}}$ decreases until it equals $n_{\text{cl}}$, the mode ceases to be guided and is radiated into the cladding (cut-off condition). <br>  <br>         \item \textbf{Normalization:} It is often expressed via the normalized propagation constant $b$, where: <br>               \begin{equation*} <br>                   b \approx \frac{n_{\text{eff}} - n_{\text{cl}}}{n_{\text{co}} - n_{\text{cl}}} <br>               \end{equation*} <br>               which ranges from 0 to 1. <br>     \end{itemize}
Sketch the way in which LP modes can be calculated	The \textbf{Weakly Guiding Approximation} assumes the refractive index difference between core and cladding is very small: <br>     \begin{equation} <br>         \Delta \approx \frac{(NA)^2}{2n_{cl}^2} \ll 1 \implies n_{co} \approx n_{cl} <br>     \end{equation} <br>  <br>     Under these conditions, the transverse components of the electric field ($E_x, E_y$) satisfy the scalar Helmholtz equation in cylindrical coordinates $\{r, \phi, z\}$: <br>     \begin{equation} <br>         \left[ \frac{\partial^2}{\partial r^2} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial \phi^2} + (\omega^2\mu\epsilon - \beta^2) \right] \vec{E}_t = 0 <br>     \end{equation} <br>  <br>     \noindent Using separation of variables ($E_t = f(r)g(\phi)$), the solutions are expressed via Bessel functions of order $n$: <br>     \begin{itemize} <br>         \item \textbf{Core ($r < a$):} $E_{y,co} = q \frac{J_n(\chi_{co}r)}{J_n(\chi_{co}a)} \cos(n\phi + \phi_0)$ (Oscillatory) <br>         \item \textbf{Cladding ($r \geq a$):} $E_{y,cl} = q \frac{K_n(\tilde{\chi}_{cl}r)}{K_n(\tilde{\chi}_{cl}a)} \cos(n\phi + \phi_0)$ (Evanescent) <br>     \end{itemize} <br>     where $\chi_{co} = \sqrt{k_0^2 n_{co}^2 - \beta^2}$ and $\tilde{\chi}_{cl} = \sqrt{\beta^2 - k_0^2 n_{cl}^2}$. <br>  <br>     \noindent Applying boundary conditions at $r=a$ yields the eigenvalue equation for $LP_{n,p}$ modes: <br>     \begin{equation} <br>         (\chi_{co} a) \frac{J_{n+1}(\chi_{co}a)}{J_{n}(\chi_{co}a)} = (\tilde{\chi}_{cl} a) \frac{K_{n+1}(\tilde{\chi}_{cl}a)}{K_{n}(\tilde{\chi}_{cl}a)} <br>     \end{equation} <br>  <br>     \textbf{Physical Constraints:} <br>     \begin{itemize} <br>         \item \textbf{Guidance Condition:} $n_{cl} < n_{eff} < n_{co}$ (where $n_{eff} = \beta/k_0$). <br>         \item \textbf{Cut-off:} Occurs when $\beta \to k_0 n_{cl}$ ($\tilde{\chi}_{cl} \to 0$). At this point, the mode is no longer confined to the core and leaks into the cladding. <br>     \end{itemize}
What is the cut-off frequency?	The cut-off frequency is the lowest frequency at which a mode can propagate in a fiber. <br>     The condition is: <br>     \[ \beta = k_{cl} \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \begin{cases} <br>             \chi_{co} = \sqrt{k^2_{co} -\beta^2} = \dots = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}NA \\ <br>             \tilde{\chi}_{cl} \to 0 <br>         \end{cases} \] <br>  <br>     Knowing the limit $\lim\limits_{u \to 0}\frac{uk_{n+1}(u)}{k_{n}(u)} = 2n$: <br>     \[ \omega \text{ cut-off frequency } \qquad :\qquad \frac{\omega a}{c_0}(NA)\frac{J_{n+1}\left(\frac{\omega a}{c_0}(NA)\right)}{J_{n}\left(\frac{\omega a}{c_0}(NA)\right)} = 2n \] <br>     Which are the first solutions? <br>     \[ LP_{0,1} =\begin{cases} <br>             n=0 \\ <br>             \omega_c = 0 <br>         \end{cases}\qquad \qquad LP_{1,1} = \begin{cases} <br>             n=1 \\ <br>             \omega_{1,1}\approxeq\frac{2.405 c_0}{a(NA)} <br>         \end{cases} \] <br>     With Cut-off frequency we block propagation of other modes except $LP_{0,1}$.
How can you verify the cutoff experimentally?	The cutoff wavelength is experimentally verified using the **bend-loss method**. <br>  <br>     The principle of this method is that the modes closer to the cut-off are more sensitive to bend losses. <br>  <br>     By measuring the spectrum difference between a straight and a bent fiber, one can observe loss peaks. These peaks correspond to the wavelengths at which specific mode groups (like LP$_{11}$, LP$_{02}$, LP$_{21}$, etc.) are "stripped away" by the bend.
Describe the properties and degeneracy of LP modes.	Mode degeneracy occurs when multiple modes share the same propagation constant ($\beta$), which results from the fiber's cylindrical symmetry. The LP modes exhibit two types of degeneracy: <br>  <br>     \begin{enumerate} <br>         \item Polarization Degeneracy: Every $\text{LP}_{n,p}$ mode has two orthogonal polarization states that share the same $\beta$, resulting in a two-fold degeneracy. <br>         \item Polarization Degeneracy: Every $\text{LP}_{n,p}$ mode has two orthogonal polarization states that share the same $\beta$, resulting in a two-fold degeneracy. <br>     \end{enumerate} <br>  <br>     So at the end: <br>     \begin{itemize} <br>         \item $\text{LP}_{0,p}$ modes (where $n=0$) are two-fold degenerate (polarization only). <br>         \item $\text{LP}_{n,p}$ modes (where $n \geq 1$) are four-fold degenerate (polarization and azimuthal). <br>     \end{itemize}
